## Why does autism develop, and what might influence it?

When a child receives an autism diagnosis, parents often ask why it happened.

Some think back to the pregnancy. Others try to connect the diagnosis with past illnesses, medicines or events in the child's first years.

Modern science, however, usually cannot identify one particular event that caused autism.

**Autism is a neurodevelopmental condition.** Its characteristics are related to how the nervous system develops and functions.

Studies show that genetic factors make a substantial contribution. Researchers also investigate prenatal development and certain environmental exposures.

Not all proposed factors are supported by evidence to the same degree.

For example, convincing evidence exists for developmental risks from exposure to some medications during pregnancy.

By contrast, an observed association between autism and parental age, an illness during pregnancy or air pollution does not prove that the specific factor caused autism.

We will examine what is relatively well established, which associations are still under investigation and why the evidence cannot be used to assign blame within a family.

## How are genetics and the environment connected?

Brain development starts long before birth.

Genes participate in the formation of nerve cells, their interactions and other biological processes. Yet the organism develops under particular conditions, which can matter as well.

Treating the question as "genes versus environment" is therefore an oversimplification.

Each person has a unique combination of genetic characteristics. Some variants are inherited from parents, while others may arise for the first time in the child.

A genetic variant does not always lead to a single outcome.

For instance, people carrying the same genetic change can have different developmental characteristics.

### How important is heredity?

In a [2019 study by Bai and colleagues in *JAMA Psychiatry*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31314057/), researchers analysed more than two million people in five countries.

They estimated autism heritability at approximately **80%**.

This figure must be interpreted carefully.

It **does not mean that genes caused 80% of a particular child's autism and the environment caused the remaining 20%**.

Heritability is a statistical estimate of how much variation in a characteristic or likelihood within the studied population is associated with genetic differences, under the study's assumptions.

It cannot identify the cause of an individual's autism.

Consider two autistic children. One has an identified genetic change known to be related to neurodevelopment. No explanatory variant is found when the other child is tested.

That does not mean that genetics matter only for the first child.

Current methods cannot detect or explain every genetic influence. A lack of known family history also does not exclude genetic contributions.

For more information, read [Autism and genetics: what role does heredity play?](../autism-genetics-and-inheritance/).

### Can the environment affect brain development?

Yes. Conditions in which the body develops can influence physiological processes.

However, "environment" means much more in research than pollution or chemicals.

It may include pregnancy circumstances, maternal health, nutrition, infections and other exposures.

Not every influence on brain development causes autism.

Researchers try to establish which factors are associated with autism likelihood, how reproducible those findings are and whether a causal interpretation is warranted.

## What does an "increased risk" mean?

News stories may report that a factor increases autism likelihood by 20%, 30% or 50%.

Such figures can sound alarming, but without baseline values they reveal little about the actual size of a difference.

Consider an imaginary example.

Researchers compare two groups of 100 children each.

In one group, the condition being studied is found in two children. In the other it is found in three.

The relative increase is **50%**.

The absolute difference, however, is **one additional case per 100 children**.

This is a mathematical illustration, not actual autism prevalence data.

It demonstrates why **relative risk and absolute probability** must be distinguished.

### Why does a statistical association not always imply causation?

Imagine a study in which autism is diagnosed more often among children born after a particular pregnancy complication.

There are several possible explanations.

The complication may indeed be associated with a biological process affecting development.

It is also possible, however, that both the complication and neurodevelopmental characteristics relate to other circumstances.

Family genetics, co-occurring health conditions and differences in healthcare may influence the results.

Researchers call these circumstances **confounding factors**.

Statistical methods are used to account for them. Studies may compare siblings from the same family, use large population samples or repeat comparisons in different countries.

Even these approaches cannot always exclude every alternative explanation.

"Autism is more common among children exposed to factor X" does not mean "factor X causes autism".

This distinction is especially important when discussing pregnancy and parental health.

## Which aspects of pregnancy do scientists investigate?

Many processes in nervous-system development occur before birth.

Researchers therefore examine parental age, pregnancy health, certain illnesses and exposures to particular substances.

Some of these factors show statistical associations with autism likelihood.

The strength of the evidence varies, and most findings cannot establish the cause for an individual child.

### Parental age

A [2024 systematic review and meta-analysis by Dehesh and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11583425/) found that higher maternal and paternal ages were associated with a higher likelihood of an autism diagnosis in children.

Researchers consider various explanations, including genetics and related circumstances.

Parental age alone is not a tool for predicting autism in a child.

Autistic children are born to parents across age groups, and most children with older parents are not diagnosed with autism.

An individual's outcome cannot be inferred from the age of either parent.

### Preterm birth

Some developmental features and diagnoses are more frequent among children born preterm than those born at term.

That does not mean preterm birth necessarily causes those outcomes.

A [2025 systematic review by Yang and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40678694/) examined factors associated with autism among people born preterm.

The researchers identified significant evidence gaps. For many proposed factors, there were too few comparable studies to calculate a reliable combined estimate.

It is difficult to distinguish the influence of gestational age at birth from neonatal health, pregnancy complications and other circumstances.

### Pregnancy illnesses and complications

Researchers also examine gestational diabetes, high blood pressure, pre-eclampsia, some infections and other conditions.

Study quality is particularly important.

A [2026 umbrella review by Zhu and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41643298/) examined **43 systematic reviews** on pregnancy complications and autism likelihood in children.

The authors found statistical associations involving conditions such as gestational diabetes, hypertension and pre-eclampsia.

However, most included reviews were of low or critically low methodological quality. No association met the authors' most convincing evidence category.

This does not imply pregnancy complications are harmless or do not need treatment.

Gestational diabetes, hypertension and other conditions can present health risks in their own right for the pregnant person and child.

Having such a condition does not prove that it caused the child's autism.

### Air pollution and chemicals

Some studies report associations between air-pollution measurements and autism likelihood.

But exposure is often only estimated approximately.

Researchers may use air-quality figures for a family's residential area, for instance.

Such measures cannot tell us exactly how much pollution an individual person was exposed to.

Neighbourhood characteristics, socioeconomic circumstances and other factors may also influence findings.

Reducing harmful exposures is important for general health, particularly during pregnancy.

But moving house, buying a filter or following a "detox" programme cannot be claimed to guarantee prevention of autism.

## Which medicines require particular attention during pregnancy?

Risks should be considered for the specific medicine in question.

It is inappropriate to treat all medications as one category.

Some have well-established risks to fetal development. For others, a proposed association with autism does not remain after more careful analysis.

The condition being treated and the possible consequences of withholding treatment matter as well.

### Valproate: an example of serious risk

Valproate is used for certain types of epilepsy and other conditions.

Convincing evidence links prenatal valproate exposure with congenital abnormalities and adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes.

A [2013 Danish study by Christensen and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23613074/) analysed **655,615 children**.

Of these, **508** had been exposed to valproate before birth.

Researchers estimated cumulative autism diagnosis likelihood during up to 14 years of follow-up:

- **4.42%** among children exposed to valproate prenatally.
- **1.53%** in the full study cohort.

These are estimates from one observational study, not forecasts for individual children.

The findings also do not imply that other valproate-exposed children could not have different developmental difficulties.

The evidence is consistent with serious warnings issued by regulators. The UK [Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/valproate-reproductive-risks) describes reproductive risks and restrictions requiring specialist oversight.

Prescribing rules and regulatory requirements may differ between countries.

Anyone taking valproate who plans a pregnancy should discuss treatment with their specialist in advance.

If pregnancy has already occurred, they should contact their treating clinician promptly.

**Do not stop valproate independently.** Abrupt withdrawal can have serious consequences, particularly when it is used to treat epilepsy.

### Paracetamol: why do studies reach different conclusions?

Researchers have examined whether prenatal paracetamol use is associated with a subsequent autism diagnosis.

Some observational studies found a small statistical increase.

However, there is an important methodological problem.

People do not take paracetamol at random. Reasons may include fever, infection or pain, which can themselves be related to other health circumstances.

Genetic and family characteristics may also affect results.

A [large Swedish study published in 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38592388/) examined almost **2.5 million children**.

A conventional analysis found a small statistical association.

Researchers then compared siblings to better account for some shared family circumstances.

The association with higher autism likelihood was no longer supported in that comparison.

An [umbrella review published in *The BMJ* in November 2025](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2025-088141) likewise concluded that the available evidence does not establish a causal association between prenatal paracetamol use and autism.

But the absence of a demonstrated causal autism link does not mean any medicine can be taken without restrictions.

Pregnancy pain and fever management should be discussed with a healthcare professional in light of the individual clinical situation.

## Can vaccines, parenting or diet cause autism?

Some explanations of autism continue to circulate despite lacking convincing scientific support.

Such claims may affect parents' decisions about their children's health.

### Vaccination

High-quality research does not support a causal link between vaccination and autism.

A [WHO systematic evidence review published on 3 September 2026](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240123533) assessed studies published from 2010 through August 2025.

This included research on vaccines containing thimerosal.

The reviewers concluded that the strongest studies do not support vaccination as a cause of autism.

Some developmental differences first become noticeable around the age of routine childhood immunisations.

The timing of two events, however, does not prove causation.

Declining recommended vaccination is not a way to prevent autism.

### Parenting and family relationships

Claims that autism is caused by emotionally cold parenting or poor upbringing lack scientific evidence.

Family relationships and living conditions certainly affect wellbeing, learning opportunities and emotional security.

Those influences are not equivalent to causing autism.

Parents have no scientific basis for blaming their child's diagnosis on insufficient affection or a particular communication style.

### Food and screen time

Ordinary childhood diet and use of digital screens are not established causes of autism.

Nutritional quality, sleep duration, physical activity and patterns of screen use still matter for overall health and development.

For instance, prolonged screen use may displace other activities, and screens before bedtime may interfere with rest.

But effects on sleep or opportunities to communicate are not evidence that screens cause autism.

Highly restrictive diets and "detoxification" programmes have also not been shown to eliminate autism.

Specific nutritional or medical difficulties should be assessed on their own merits.

## Can autism be prevented?

No known set of actions can guarantee prevention of autism.

Many neurodevelopmental characteristics arise from complex interacting biological processes.

Autism should not be portrayed as the inevitable outcome of one action or mistake during pregnancy.

This does not make preventive healthcare unimportant.

Preconception planning, management of chronic illness and appropriate pregnancy care help reduce other established risks to the health of the pregnant person and child.

### What is known about folic acid?

Folic acid is recommended before and in early pregnancy to help prevent certain neural-tube defects.

That is an established medical reason for its use.

Researchers separately investigate associations between prenatal folic acid use and autism likelihood.

A [2025 umbrella review by Abate and colleagues in *PLOS ONE*](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0334852) combined findings from **eight systematic reviews involving more than three million mother-child pairs**.

Use of folic acid or multivitamins during pregnancy was associated with a lower likelihood of autism in children.

However, most underlying findings were observational.

They do not establish that extra vitamin use prevents autism in a particular child.

Supplement doses should not be increased independently in an attempt to prevent autism.

Folic acid and other supplements should be used according to appropriate medical guidance and individual indications.

### What if a child already has an autism diagnosis?

A diagnosis does not mean that something harmful must have happened during the pregnancy.

Even a statistically associated factor is not automatically a demonstrated cause in one person.

Trying to reconstruct a single "mistake" from pregnancy memories therefore rarely has a scientific basis.

## What does this knowledge mean for parents?

Studying genes and environmental factors helps science understand nervous-system development.

But the findings do not allow researchers to reconstruct the exact individual origin of autism for every child.

### What is relatively well established?

Genetic factors are an important contributor to autism.

Genetic characteristics can differ, and the same variant need not produce identical developmental outcomes in different people.

Serious evidence links certain prenatal exposures, especially valproate, with adverse neurodevelopmental risks.

Vaccination is not an established cause of autism, and poor parenting does not explain its origins.

### Which questions are still being investigated?

Research continues on parental age, pregnancy complications, air pollution and other factors.

Some are statistically associated with autism, but their causal importance remains unclear.

Scientists also investigate how different genetic characteristics might interact with conditions during early development.

### What cannot currently be concluded?

One event during pregnancy cannot identify the cause of autism in a particular child.

Population risk estimates are not precise individual forecasts.

Nor is there evidence that a special diet, supplement, lifestyle change or "detox" programme can guarantee prevention or elimination of autism.

### What can families usefully do?

When planning pregnancy, families should discuss chronic health conditions, medication and standard preventive care with a clinician.

When a child already has an autism diagnosis, the practical question is what support they need now.

How do they communicate their needs? Which situations are difficult? Is assessment of sleep, nutrition or co-occurring medical conditions needed?

In certain situations a clinician may recommend genetic counselling or testing to investigate a possible genetic condition and its associated health needs. Such testing does not replace clinical autism assessment.

**Research into autism risk factors helps us understand its biology, but provides no scientific basis for blaming parents or searching for one universal cause.**
