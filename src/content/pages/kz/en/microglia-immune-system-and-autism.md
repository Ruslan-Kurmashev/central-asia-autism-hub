## What are microglia and why does the brain need them?

When discussing how the brain works, we often think of neurons. These nerve cells receive and transmit signals that support movement, perception, learning and communication.

Neurons do not work alone. Other cells around them help maintain conditions in which the nervous system can function.

One of these cell types is called **microglia**.

Microglia are resident immune cells of the brain and spinal cord. They respond to changes in surrounding tissue, interact with other cells and participate in some processes involved in developing connections between nerve cells.

Unlike many immune cells circulating in the blood, microglia enter the nervous system early in embryonic development. Thereafter, their population is largely maintained within brain tissue itself.

Why are microglia studied in relation to autism?

Research indicates that these cells contribute to processes important for nervous system development. Some studies have also reported differences in particular immune and glial measures among the autistic participants examined.

This does not demonstrate that microglia are the sole or established cause of autism.

To understand the findings, it helps to first distinguish microglia from other brain cells and explore what they do under ordinary conditions.

## How do microglia differ from neurons?

Neurons and microglia share the same tissue but have different roles.

Neurons specialise in receiving, processing and transmitting nervous signals. Their connections create circuits and larger brain networks.

Microglia do not transmit signals in this way. Instead, they interact with their surroundings, respond to cellular signals and can engulf certain particles and cellular material.

Microglial cells often have numerous thin processes. These processes continually change position, allowing the cells to interact with their immediate environment.

When tissue is injured or its condition changes, microglia can change their shape and functions.

It is important, however, to distinguish a normal cellular response from disease. A change in the shape of a microglial cell does not, by itself, demonstrate harmful inflammation. Similar changes can occur in different physiological and pathological conditions.

The nervous system also contains astrocytes and oligodendrocytes, among other cells. They contribute to maintaining the tissue environment, metabolism and the functioning of nerve fibres.

It would therefore be misleading to explain the brain solely through neurons or solely through microglia.

## How do microglia support normal brain function?

Consider a child's developing brain.

Nerve cells establish many contacts, exchange signals and gradually form interacting networks. At the same time, normal tissue renewal and cell changes take place.

Microglia participate in several of these processes.

First, their processes contact neighbouring cells and detect chemical signals.

Second, microglia can engulf cell debris and some tissue components. This is one normal function of immune cells.

Third, they exchange signalling molecules with neurons and other cells.

Finally, under certain circumstances, they influence the development and remodelling of neural connections.

All these functions vary with age, brain region and tissue condition.

Microglia should not therefore be portrayed only as cells that fight infections or destroy damaged structures. They are part of the normal biology of the nervous system.

**Microglial activity alone does not mean inflammation or disease.**

## How do microglia participate in changes between neurons?

Specialised contacts between nerve cells are called synapses. They allow neurons to pass signals to one another.

During development, these connections change. Some form, some become more effective, and others weaken or are removed.

The removal of some neural contacts is known as **synaptic pruning**.

It is a normal aspect of nervous system development, not a process in which all supposedly unnecessary connections are removed according to one universal rule.

The number and function of synapses depend on age, brain region, nerve-cell activity and many other factors.

### What have experiments shown?

In a [2011 study by Paolicelli and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21778362/) in *Science*, researchers investigated brain development in mice.

They found that microglia could engulf synaptic material and participate in the remodelling of neural connections after birth.

A separate [2012 study by Schafer and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2012.03.026) in *Neuron* examined the developing mouse visual system.

It showed microglial involvement in removing particular neural contacts, with participation from the immune complement system.

These are informative findings in the specific experimental models studied.

Both studies, however, used animals. They do not establish that microglia remove synapses incorrectly in every autistic child.

The claim that autistic people necessarily have too many connections in their brains sometimes appears online. This sweeping statement is not established by the evidence.

Studies of particular brain regions and laboratory models have reported some differences in synaptic structures. They cannot establish the number of contacts throughout the entire brain of an individual person.

Nor does a higher or lower number of particular connections automatically indicate better or worse brain function.

Synaptic pruning remains an important topic of investigation, not a universal explanation for autism.

## What have researchers found in autism studies?

Scientists use different methods to investigate microglia, including postmortem brain tissue, gene-expression analysis, laboratory cell models and specialised brain imaging.

Each method addresses particular questions and has important limitations.

### Studies of brain tissue

In a [2005 study by Vargas and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15546155/) published in *Annals of Neurology*, researchers examined postmortem brain samples from **11 autistic people**.

They reported indications of altered glial and immune activity in some regions examined. This drew attention to a possible role for neuroimmune processes in autism.

However, the study could not establish when the changes arose or whether they caused autistic characteristics. The sample was also small.

Later work adopted more detailed molecular methods.

For example, [Voineagu and colleagues (2011)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21614001/) reported differences in expression of particular gene groups in postmortem cortical samples. Some of those groups were associated with immune and glial processes.

Changes in gene expression do not automatically establish inflammatory disease. They also do not show whether the differences were causes or consequences of other processes.

### Why do brain imaging findings disagree?

Researchers sometimes use positron emission tomography (**PET**) to investigate aspects of neuroimmune processes.

One frequently studied marker is the protein **TSPO**, but TSPO is not specific to microglia alone.

Autism findings have differed across studies.

In [Suzuki and colleagues (2013)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23404112/), researchers compared **20 autistic adult men** with **20 comparison participants**. In some brain regions, the autistic group had higher binding measures for the tracer used.

A [2020 study by Zurcher and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41380-020-0682-z) instead reported lower measures with a different TSPO tracer in particular regions.

In [Simpson and colleagues (2022)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35383319/), a study involving **13 autistic and 13 non-autistic participants**, the principal comparison found no statistically significant difference.

These are three relatively small studies with differences in methods and participants.

Together, their findings do not show that microglia are in one identical state in every autistic person.

### What can laboratory models tell us?

Researchers also investigate cells grown using modern culture techniques. These models can help explore how certain genetic changes affect microglial function.

In a [2025 study by Teter and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41380-025-02997-z), investigators examined the effects of autism-associated genes in human microglia-like cells.

They found that altering **ADNP** gene activity influenced particular cellular processes, including uptake of material and interactions with nerve cells.

This is useful for exploring biological mechanisms. A laboratory cell culture, however, does not fully reproduce a developing human brain.

The observed cellular effect is not automatically an explanation of autism or a justification for clinical treatment.

## Does autism mean the brain is inflamed?

No. Present evidence does not support classifying autism as a universal inflammatory disease of the brain.

The term **neuroinflammation** is used in nervous system research, but it may refer to different kinds of changes.

Scientists might observe altered activity of immune-related genes, cell numbers, signalling molecules or imaging measurements. These are not equivalent findings.

Every immune-associated change cannot automatically be treated as evidence of chronic harmful inflammation.

Studies of microglia in autism are heterogeneous. Results differ according to participant age, the brain region studied, sample characteristics and the measurement method.

A [2026 review in *Frontiers in Immunology*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42131345/) also emphasises that the evidence does not support one microglial state characterising the entire autism spectrum.

Some cellular characteristics may relate to particular biological processes. Others may be tissue responses to other changes. For many findings, the direction of cause and effect remains unknown.

### What about immune activity during pregnancy?

Researchers also study possible links between certain immune processes during pregnancy and the development of a child's nervous system.

In animal experiments, particular immune exposures can sometimes be accompanied by differences in the offspring's brain development.

Human studies examine infections, autoimmune conditions and other factors.

An association between a pregnancy-related factor and a later autism diagnosis does not establish that the factor caused autism. Genetic and other circumstances may affect the result.

It would therefore be wrong to say that a mother's ordinary cold, fever or stress explains autism in an individual child.

## Can microglia be assessed through blood tests or MRI?

Parents may encounter offers to identify "neuroinflammation in autism" through blood tests or specialised examinations.

At present, **there is no accepted clinical test that measures a child's microglial condition and confirms autism**.

Scientists may measure cytokines, complement proteins or other immune markers. But those substances do not directly measure what microglia are doing inside the brain.

For instance, blood immune markers can change during an ordinary infection. Their interpretation depends on many factors, including health at the time of sampling.

A single elevated value therefore cannot establish the cause of autism.

Ordinary MRI cannot show the condition of individual microglial cells either.

Specialised PET techniques are used mainly in research, and their findings are not stand-alone diagnostic tests for autism.

If a child shows signs of a particular medical condition, a clinician may request investigations for appropriate medical reasons. That is different from conducting immune testing in search of a universal cause of autism.

### Can anti-inflammatory medicines help?

The involvement of immune cells in brain function does not imply that suppressing immune activity can eliminate autism.

Research on individual cellular mechanisms does not justify prescribing anti-inflammatory drugs, immune suppressants or special supplements to every autistic child.

Such interventions may have adverse effects and need specific medical indications.

[NICE guidance for autistic children and young people](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations) does not recommend universal immune correction to change core autistic characteristics.

When a child has a genuine infectious, allergic, autoimmune or other medical condition, it should be assessed and managed according to relevant clinical guidance.

An autism diagnosis alone is not a reason to prescribe immune-directed treatment.

## What do these findings mean for parents?

Research on microglia helps scientists understand nervous system development and the interactions between different cell types.

It does not yet identify the cause of an individual child's autism or allow clinicians to choose support based on a personal "immune profile".

For families, a more useful starting point is the child's observable needs.

For example, if a child begins sleeping poorly, cries more frequently or avoids familiar activities, it would be inappropriate to assume that these changes prove brain inflammation.

Possible explanations include physical discomfort, pain, routine changes, sleep problems, anxiety and features of the environment.

It helps to establish when the difficulties occur, what precedes them and whether there are signs of a medical problem requiring assessment.

For a child who struggles with loud sounds or bright light, families can consider adjustments to the surroundings.

For communication difficulties, it is more useful to identify ways the child can express needs and obtain appropriate support.

There is no need to attempt to infer microglial activity from a child's behaviour.

Autism research continues to clarify biological mechanisms, but no single immune model currently explains the whole spectrum.

### What is reasonably well established?

Microglia are a normal part of the nervous system. They interact with neighbouring cells, contribute to immune processes and participate in some stages of remodelling neural connections.

Specific experimental models demonstrate their involvement in synaptic changes.

Human studies report some neuroimmune and glial differences, but the findings are varied and do not define one autism mechanism.

### What remains unknown?

Researchers cannot yet say with confidence which reported cellular changes come first, which are responses to other processes or how closely they relate to individual developmental characteristics.

Nor has it been established that microglial activity can be used to diagnose autism or select a universal treatment.

For parents, the practical conclusion is that microglia are important to brain biology, but decisions about a child's health and support should be based on actual needs and clinically established indications.
