Play helps a child explore the world, express interests and interact with others. During play, there may be opportunities for communication, imitation, shared activities and learning skills useful in everyday life.

Different approaches are used to support these skills in autistic children. Some focus on sharing attention to an object or event, others on communication, participation in play or learning during ordinary activities. They are not a single type of "play therapy". Programmes differ in their goals, organisation and research evidence.

The starting point should not be how closely a child's play resembles other children's play, but which forms of communication and participation are accessible and meaningful to that child.

## Joint attention: sharing an interest

Joint attention means sharing attention to an object, event or activity with another person.

For example, a child notices an aeroplane, points to it or communicates about it through AAC, and a parent responds to the same object. Alternatively, an adult shows something interesting and the child shifts attention towards it.

Joint attention does not mean requiring a child to "look an adult in the eyes". Eye contact may occur, but sustained eye contact by itself is not a prerequisite for sharing attention.

### Responding and initiating

Joint attention is commonly described using two related components.

**Responding to Joint Attention (RJA)** means another person directs the child's attention to an object or event, and the child notices the cue and shifts attention. For example, the adult points at a cat and the child looks towards it.

**Initiating Joint Attention (IJA)** means the child independently tries to bring another person's attention to an object, event or activity to share interest. For example, the child sees an aeroplane and points it out, brings over an interesting object, or uses a gesture, word or AAC message.

RJA and IJA are related but different skills. A child may respond readily to another person's pointing while rarely initiating joint attention, or the reverse.

### Example of joint attention

A child loves toy cars. An adult notices an unusual car and points to it. If the child shifts attention to the car, this is an example of RJA.

Later the child spots another car and:

- points towards it
- brings it to the adult
- says "look"
- selects a message on AAC.

That may illustrate IJA.

Joint attention does not require prolonged eye contact. Do not physically turn a child's face or hold their gaze. Interest can be shared through gesture, words, interaction with an object or AAC.

## Engagement and reciprocal communication

### What engagement means

Engagement is participation in an activity or interaction. A child may:

- join a shared game
- respond to another person's actions
- initiate interaction
- stay involved in a meaningful activity.

Engagement should not be measured only by sitting quietly or looking at an adult. Someone may be engaged while moving, using AAC, not maintaining eye contact or interacting in their own way.

### Exchanging messages and initiatives

Reciprocal communication means exchanging messages and initiatives between people, including:

- initiations
- responses
- questions
- comments
- shared actions.

It may happen through speech, AAC, gestures, pictures, play or several modes together. It should not be assessed only by counting spoken words.

### Including AAC in play

Children who use augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) should not be excluded from developmental or play-based activities because their spoken language is limited or absent.

A systematic review and meta-analysis by Pope and colleagues included 29 NDBI studies involving autistic children with minimal speech. Aggregated language effects were larger when aided AAC was included, but only three studies directly compared NDBI with and without AAC. [Pope et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38848009/).

A cautious conclusion is that AAC can be integrated into naturalistic developmental support, while the extra benefit attributable to AAC within NDBI is based on few direct comparisons.

[More about AAC](/kz/en/parents/aac-communication-guide/).

Spoken language is not a prerequisite for shared play. A child can participate through gestures, movement, actions with objects, facial expression and AAC.

## Play has value in itself and can support learning

Play can be a meaningful activity in itself, a way to interact, a setting for learning or an outcome measured by an intervention.

NICE recommends considering specialist social-communication interventions using play-based strategies for joint attention, engagement and reciprocal communication. These should fit the child's developmental level. For preschool-age children, NICE recommends considering parents, carers or teachers in delivery, and for school-age children it mentions peers. [NICE CG170](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations).

### Following a child's interests

If a child enjoys trains, dinosaurs, maps, water, numbers or another topic, those interests may offer a setting for engagement, shared play, communication and learning.

A child's favourite items should not be deliberately withheld and then provided only as something the child has to earn.

### When shared play is not needed

Not all play needs to involve another person. Being able to rest alone, play independently, pursue interests and choose leisure activities can be meaningful aspects of independence.

Not every action should become a teaching trial. Constant questioning and demands to repeat can make play less enjoyable and voluntary. The option to choose, take a break or play independently matters.

### Discussing goals for play

Examples include:

- broadening the range of accessible play activities
- inviting someone to join
- communicating ideas
- accepting or offering turns
- developing a shared play story
- enjoying meaningful free time independently.

The goal does not have to be "play like other children". Free play and rest can be meaningful outcomes. Unusual ways of using toys are not automatically a reason to insist on "correct" play. Base the goal on the child's interests, opportunities and functional needs.

## Approaches that may be used

### Naturalistic Intervention

Naturalistic Intervention means teaching a skill within real everyday contexts, such as play, meals, outings, household routines, school activities or tasks linked to a child's interests.

Hume and colleagues (2021) included Naturalistic Intervention among practices meeting their evidence criteria. The review, however, covered research only through the end of 2017. [NCAEP review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8510990/).

A skill can be practised where it actually matters. A child might communicate "open" using a word, gesture or AAC message when they want to open a toy box. The natural consequence is that the box is opened.

### Learning versus free play

Structured learning in a natural situation differs from free play. Teaching usually includes a specific target, defined strategies, support, opportunities to practise and an observable result.

Free play remains valuable and need not continually become an intervention.

### NDBI: naturalistic developmental behavioural interventions

Naturalistic Developmental Behavioral Interventions (NDBI) are a broad family of interventions combining child development principles, behavioural learning methods and teaching within natural interaction.

NDBI is not one programme. "NDBI works" is too general a claim. Clarify:

- the specific programme or procedure
- intended age group and goal
- intensity
- who delivers it
- how outcomes are measured.

### Parent-mediated support

Parent-mediated support means parents or other carers learn strategies they can use during natural interaction. NICE recommends considering parent, carer or teacher involvement in social-communication support for preschoolers.

That does not mean "a parent must be a therapist all day". A parent remains a parent first. Intervention should not turn every family interaction into a lesson or impose an unsustainable burden.

### Peer-mediated support

Peer-mediated approaches involve prepared peers to support interaction, engagement, communication and shared activities. NICE suggests considering peers in social-communication interventions for school-age children.

The goal should not be for peers to train an autistic child to look or act "normal". Accessible, reciprocal participation is a more appropriate aim.

### Imitation and reciprocal imitation

Imitation may help a child learn new actions and take part in play or interaction. It should not become a universal requirement that "the child must copy the adult".

Some approaches use reciprocal imitation, where the adult also imitates the child's actions to support the exchange.

## What the research shows

### Joint attention

A 2026 meta-analysis included 18 randomised controlled studies involving 1,165 autistic children under five. The average effect on joint-attention skills was:

**Hedges' g = 0.53, 95% CI 0.34-0.72.**

This represents a moderate average effect with substantial differences between studies. Hedges' g is a standardised effect size, not the percentage of children who acquired the skill. It is not an individual prognosis.

Child age and the type of joint attention were associated with differences in effect. Effects on play were also reported but require cautious interpretation. [Joint-attention meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41493690/).

The responsible conclusion is that joint-attention interventions have research support in young autistic children, but effects vary between children and programmes.

### NDBI: the 2019 meta-analysis

Tiede and Walton analysed 27 group-design NDBI studies among young autistic children. Reported average effects included:

- expressive language: g = 0.32
- play skills: g = 0.23
- social engagement: g = 0.65
- overall cognitive development: g = 0.48
- joint attention: g = 0.14, small and borderline in statistical significance
- receptive language: g = 0.28.

The authors also reported indications of publication and reporting bias for language outcomes. [Tiede and Walton](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31018655/).

The findings depend on exactly which skill was measured.

### Why the assessment context matters

Project AIM analysed 27 NDBI studies and 454 outcomes. Effects were larger for skills directly targeted by the intervention and for outcomes measured in settings similar to intervention sessions.

Effects were smaller for more distal and generalised outcomes. Methodological concerns, including some measures at high risk of assessment bias, limit confidence in positive pooled estimates. [Project AIM](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33480474/).

A skill shown during a therapy session does not necessarily transfer to home, school or another setting.

### What the 2025 review adds

A 2025 mixed-methods systematic review included 25 studies, 16 randomised controlled trials and nine qualitative studies. It combined early intensive behavioural interventions and NDBI, so its pooled effects cannot be attributed only to NDBI.

Its quantitative analysis reported significant mean effects for:

- adaptive behaviour: SMD = 0.31, low GRADE certainty
- daily living skills: SMD = 0.36, low certainty
- language: SMD = 0.42, moderate certainty
- joint attention: SMD = 0.27, low certainty.

The authors also described a high risk of bias in many included studies and substantial variation in outcomes. [Mixed-methods review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41454358/).

Parents should therefore not be promised that a developmental intervention will improve every aspect of development.

### Play-based interventions: the 2026 meta-analysis

A meta-analysis by Çuvadar Baş and colleagues included only seven studies. The authors reported statistically significant average changes in social and play skills, but heterogeneity was extremely high:

- social skills: I² = 93.730%
- play skills: I² = 94.768%.

These percentages are measures of between-study heterogeneity, not the proportion of children who benefited or the probability of individual success. With such variation and only seven studies, subgroup analyses cannot be used as a reliable league table of programmes or proof that one method is superior.

A cautious conclusion is that certain play-based interventions have positive research findings, but the category is too heterogeneous to promise the same effect for every programme.

### Interpreting the limitations

A large average effect in a small meta-analysis does not mean every child will experience a large change. Consider together:

- only seven included studies
- very high heterogeneity
- differences between programmes
- differences between outcomes
- the methodological quality of individual studies.

Findings for social or play skills cannot automatically be extended to language, adaptive functioning, quality of life or other domains that were not measured.

Useful questions for families include: which programme is used, what exactly is targeted, whether it fits the child's age and communication methods, how the outcome is measured and whether studies concern that specific programme.

### Pretend play

Some programmes address symbolic or pretend play, such as feeding a toy bear, imagining a box is a car or inventing a story.

The presence or absence of a particular play style should not be the sole measure of a child's development, well-being or quality of life. If pretend play is a selected goal, explain why it matters for this child.

Study averages cannot predict an individual's outcome. Selecting support requires evidence relevant to the programme, age, goal and assessment method.

## Supporting interaction during ordinary play

If a child likes toy cars, an adult may join, imitate an action using another car, pause and leave space for the child to continue or suggest the next step. A response can be a movement, gesture, word or AAC message. The adult responds to the child's initiative rather than requiring a single "correct" answer.

### Supporting joint attention

Support may involve:

- play
- the child's interests
- allowing time for their initiative
- modelling
- natural situations
- shared activities.

Adults can notice the child's interests, join their play, point out interesting events, allow time for a response and respond when the child shares an interest.

Do not turn every interaction into a test of "look", "show me" or "what is that?". Free play and the right not to join every exchange should remain available.

If the child does not wish to interact, respect that choice. Play without adult involvement can also be a meaningful activity.

## Evaluating the benefit of support

### Functional outcomes

Instead of saying "became more social", define a functional outcome. Examples include:

- independently initiating shared activity
- showing or communicating interest in an object
- responding to another person's initiative
- participating in several reciprocal exchanges
- using AAC while playing
- enjoying a wider range of meaningful play activities
- using the skill at home, at school and in other settings.

### What is not sufficient evidence of success

Do not automatically make the following primary measures of success:

- seconds of eye contact
- suppression of harmless stimming
- sitting silently
- imitation of neurotypical gestures
- absence of unusual play.

If one of these is measured, its functional meaning for the person must be clear.

### Generalisation

Check whether skills appear at home, on playgrounds, at school, with different adults, with peers and across activities.

If joint attention or another skill appears only with a specialist and one toy, generalisation has not yet been demonstrated.

## When to reconsider an approach and what to discuss with a professional

### Family workload

If intervention requires parents to run many hours of structured sessions each day, constantly count data or turn every exchange into training, evaluate family burden separately.

More hours do not automatically lead to better outcomes for every child or programme.

### Reasons to review a plan

Review the approach when:

- the child regularly experiences marked distress
- goals are unclear or unrelated to real needs
- interaction is controlled almost entirely by adults
- the child has little opportunity to choose or decline
- skills do not transfer into everyday life
- family workload is excessive
- outcomes are measured only during therapy sessions
- the intervention promotes masking rather than meaningful participation.

### Questions for a professional

1. What specific goal was chosen for the child, and why?
2. How are their interests, preferences and right to refuse respected?
3. How can the child communicate, including through AAC?
4. What is measured, and how is generalisation to daily life checked?
5. What evidence applies to this programme, age and goal?
6. What demands will be placed on the family, and when is the plan reviewed?

### Considerations in Kazakhstan

Play and developmental approaches may be used within speech and language support, psychology, education, developmental programmes, occupational therapy and other multidisciplinary services.

A programme name or certificate for a specific method does not establish professional competence. Check core education, professional role, scope of practice and applicable requirements for services in Kazakhstan separately.
