## Communication is more than spoken language

**Communication** means sharing information, wishes, feelings and meaning. Spoken speech is only one way to do this.

A child may communicate through looking, gestures, pointing, movement, pictures, writing, familiar phrases or a speech-generating device. Some autistic children speak fluently, others use a few words and some do not use spoken language.

**Not speaking does not mean having no thoughts, wishes or ability to communicate.** Equally, speaking in long sentences does not necessarily mean that conversation and understanding hints are easy (National Autistic Society).

## Speech, language comprehension and communication: what differs?

| Concept | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Speech** | Producing spoken sounds and words. | A child says “water”. |
| **Receptive language** | Understanding words and the meaning of messages. | A child understands a request to bring a cup. |
| **Expressive language** | Using language to express meaning. | A child types “I want a drink” on a device. |
| **Communication** | The wider exchange of messages in different ways. | A child points to a door to indicate a wish to go outside. |
| **Social communication** | Using and understanding communication in interactions with other people. | A child and an adult negotiate how to play together. |

These abilities can develop unevenly. A child may speak in complex sentences but struggle with ambiguous instructions. Another may speak little yet understand familiar messages and make choices using pictures.

**The number of spoken words cannot by itself establish intelligence or comprehension.**

## Why doesn't a child always respond straight away?

A child may need additional time to process information, may not have heard the question, may not understand the wording, may be absorbed in another activity, tired or anxious, or may prefer to respond in a different way.

Silence is therefore not always disobedience. But it is also wrong to attribute all response difficulties to autism. Hearing, health and other possible causes may need assessment.

Clear short phrases, more time and the option of responding with a gesture or picture can sometimes help. NICE recommends adapting communication to individual needs and using suitable visual supports.

## What is echolalia?

**Echolalia** means repeating words, phrases or longer passages of speech heard from other people or media.

- **Immediate echolalia:** an adult asks, “Shall we go for a walk?”, and the child immediately repeats, “Shall we go for a walk?”
- **Delayed echolalia:** the child repeats a familiar line from a conversation, song or cartoon later.

Echolalia may serve different functions. ASHA describes situations where repetition can maintain contact, request something, affirm or refuse. At other times, the child might enjoy the sound of the phrase or repeat it for another reason.

**Not every repeated phrase should be dismissed as meaningless.** Yet it is also wrong to assume that every repetition communicates a specific intention.

### Example: a line from a cartoon

A child says “Let's go home!” in a noisy room. This might be a request to leave, a favourite quotation or something else. The surroundings and the child's other ways of communicating matter when interpreting it.

There is no validated universal scheme that uses the form of echolalia to predict language development in every autistic child. ASHA cautions against overgeneralising theories. Repetition alone **does not prove** that a child is progressing through mandatory special language stages, such as those proposed by Natural Language Acquisition (NLA). Reliable evidence of the effectiveness of specialised stage-based protocols remains insufficient. Echolalia should be understood in context rather than automatically suppressed. Read the [2025 review of interventions for echolalia](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2025_AJSLP-24-00211).

## How do children who speak little communicate?

Besides speech, many other methods are available.

**Gestures and movements.** Pointing, nodding, reaching or individualised signs.

**Pictures and symbols.** Cards, charts, pictograms or photographs for making requests, refusing, choosing and expressing other messages.

**Writing and typing.** Messages on paper, a tablet, a computer or another device.

**Augmentative and alternative communication (AAC).** An umbrella term for methods that supplement or replace speech. AAC ranges from simple tools to electronic speech-generating devices.

There is no single suitable system for everybody. Communication should not be limited to requesting objects. People should be able to refuse, share feelings or opinions and ask questions as well.

## Does AAC stop a child from learning to talk?

No. According to ASHA, early AAC use **does not prevent speech development**. Accessible communication should not be postponed out of fear that a child will “stop trying to speak”.

At the same time, AAC does not guarantee speech development for every child. Its central purpose is to provide access to communication and everyday participation.

Choosing a system, access method and educational support requires an individual approach. More detailed information is available in the Russian-language guide [AAC: augmentative and alternative communication](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/ru/parents/aac/).

## Why doesn't eye contact measure understanding?

Some people find eye contact comfortable, while others find it difficult or distracting. A child may listen and understand while looking away or engaging in a calm repetitive movement.

Therefore, communication should not be judged only by eye contact, familiar facial expressions or speed of response. More important is **whether both people were able to understand each other and share the intended meaning**.

Misunderstandings can occur on both sides: an adult may misread a gesture, while a child may not understand figurative language. Both communication partners may need to adapt.

## What can parents pay attention to?

- Which accessible methods does the child already use to express requests, refusal, interest or discomfort?
- In which settings is communication easier, and when are there barriers?
- Can the child say “no”, “it hurts”, “break” or ask a question in an accessible way?
- Do they need more time to respond?
- Do different adults understand the signals the child uses?
- Does communication remain accessible when moving between home, school and other places?

These questions are not a test. They help make visible that communication is broader than speaking words.

## What can't be concluded from speech?

**“Speaks little, so understands little.”** One does not follow from the other.

**“Repeats phrases, so cannot communicate.”** Echolalia can sometimes be communicative.

**“Does not make eye contact, so is not listening.”** Gaze direction alone cannot justify this conclusion.

**“If AAC is used, speech will never develop.”** The cited professional sources do not support this.

**“Speaks in sentences, so does not need support.”** Speech does not capture all aspects of communication.

## When should a professional be consulted?

Seek qualified assessment when there are persistent concerns about comprehension, no accessible way to express important needs, possible hearing difficulties, loss of previously acquired skills or a need to select AAC. Assessment should recognise accessible communication methods rather than relying only on spoken responses.
