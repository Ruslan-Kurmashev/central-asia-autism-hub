## Why do autism statistics differ so much across five neighbouring countries?

Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan, Tajikistan and Turkmenistan make up Central Asia. They are geographically close, yet autism-related statistics are published in different ways. Some countries report medical monitoring data, others publish individual figures for diagnosed children, while comparable national ASD figures could not be verified in the public sources reviewed for some countries.

The question **'which country has more autistic children?'** therefore cannot currently be answered reliably using a single table. We need not just numbers but an understanding of **what was counted in each country**.

This article maps information available **as of 15 October 2026**. New national publications may require reassessing these findings.

**Important:** lack of a verified published number **does not mean there are zero autistic people** or prove that a country has no internal registration system.

## Kazakhstan: a medical-monitoring figure was reported

In 2026, the Kazakhstan Ministry of Health reported **17,196 children with ASD under ongoing medical monitoring**.

This is a relatively specific measure, referring to children known to healthcare services over a given period. Even an official count, however, cannot automatically be converted into the true prevalence of autism among all children in the country.

Prevalence requires agreement on the relevant age group, the population denominator and how completely cases were identified, in addition to the number of recorded children.

Kazakhstan researchers have also published national registry analyses covering 2016-2022 and 2014-2024. They illustrate how registration depends on ages, diagnostic criteria, follow-up periods, service availability and reporting practices.

For details, consult two AutismHub articles:

- [How many children with autism are registered in Kazakhstan? Statistics for 2020-2026](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/en/research/autism-registration-statistics-kazakhstan-2020-2026/).
- [Why are recorded autism diagnoses increasing in Kazakhstan?](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/en/research/why-autism-diagnoses-are-increasing-in-kazakhstan/).

These data can inform planning for assessment and support, but they do not establish that Kazakhstan has a higher biological population prevalence of autism than its neighbours.

## Kyrgyzstan: do not confuse existing registrations with new cases

In November 2025, according to media reporting, **Tamilla Kadyrova** of the Kyrgyz State Medical Academy presented figures from the Ministry of Health's electronic health centre at a roundtable at the National Center for Maternal and Child Welfare.

For **2024**, reports mentioned **792 registered children with pervasive developmental disorders**, including **423 children recorded with autism** and **332 with atypical autism**. Categories are quoted as reported. Without the original table, it cannot be assumed they cover every ASD case or can be added into one official ASD total.

Separately, in February 2026, media reported comments by first deputy health minister **Aibek Matkerimov** to the Jogorku Kenesh. Those reports referred to **first recorded autism cases**:

| Year | First recorded cases |
| --- | ---: |
| 2022 | 99 |
| 2023 | 90 |
| 2024 | 82 |

*Source: [Vesti.kg, 10 February 2026](https://vesti.kg/obshchestvo/item/147829-minzdrav-ne-znaet-skolko-detej-s-dtsp-autizmom-i-sindromom-dauna-est-v-strane.html), citing the deputy minister. The numbers have not been independently reconciled against a public primary ministry table.*

### Why 423 and 82 need not contradict each other

Imagine a school library. It might hold 423 books of a particular category while acquiring only 82 new books in the current year. The total stock and new acquisitions answer **different questions**.

Likewise, a count of children already registered with a diagnosis may differ from the number added to records in a year. However, Kyrgyz legislators also raised inconsistencies among statistical sources. It is therefore important **not to assume that the underlying methods fully explain every discrepancy**.

The coverage of ages, diagnosis definitions and primary source table would have to be checked before making claims about completeness.

### Does the fall from 99 to 82 mean autism is becoming less common?

No. Three annual values of new recorded cases are far too limited for that conclusion. Referral pathways, reporting procedures, specialist availability and other factors may affect them.

Estimating true prevalence would require comparable population-based research among children.

## Uzbekistan: autism research exists, but no comparable national count was confirmed

Uzbekistan publishes information on disability, social protection, education and inclusion, including materials from the national statistics authority and UNICEF.

But disability statistics **are not the same as ASD statistics**. An autistic child may have no formally recorded disability. Conversely, general disability counts cover many other conditions.

In the review as of 15 October 2026, no public nationwide table could be confirmed that specified all of the following:

1. the number of children specifically with ASD,
2. age limits and diagnostic categories,
3. the reporting year and reference date,
4. the registration source and collection method,
5. explanations of completeness of coverage.

The comparison table therefore uses **'not verified'** rather than inventing a number.

Uzbekistan-based researchers and professionals have published studies of autism diagnosis and family experiences. Yet a small clinical or qualitative sample cannot replace national population statistics.

UNICEF's [situation analysis of children in Uzbekistan](https://www.unicef.org/uzbekistan/en/reports/situation-analysis-children-and-adolescents-uzbekistan) discusses important barriers to services, but must not be presented as a national ASD prevalence study.

## Tajikistan: a study found low recorded rates and large regional differences

In 2026, Tajikistan researchers published a study in the *Bulletin of Postgraduate Education in Healthcare* analysing registered ASD cases in **2020-2025**.

The authors examined government Form No. 12 data for children aged **0-17 years**. The observation unit was a **registered case**.

Across six years, the study reported **170 registered ASD cases** and a rate rising from **0.4 per 100,000 children in 2020 to 1.2 in 2025**.

| Indicator | Reported value |
| --- | ---: |
| Registered ASD cases, total for 2020-2025 | 170 |
| ASD cases registered in 2025 | 45 |
| Rate in 2020 | 0.4 per 100,000 children |
| Rate in 2025 | 1.2 per 100,000 children |
| Share of registered cases attributed to Dushanbe across six years | 65.3% |

*Source: [Abdullozoda and colleagues, 2026, journal website](https://www.vestnik-ipovszrt.tj/ru/detail.php?id=106), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.66269/2414-0252-2026-16-2-258-266). These are registered cases within a specified period, not a census of all autistic children.*

### Does this mean autism is extraordinarily rare in Tajikistan?

**No.** The authors themselves discuss low registration as a possible indication of under-identification. Concentration of cases in Dushanbe and the absence of registrations in some regions point to questions about access to assessment.

A zero recorded for a region means **zero cases in the analysed reporting source**, not evidence that no autistic children live there.

The 170 cannot safely be treated as a count of different children living in Tajikistan on a single date. The study summed registration events across several years and does not establish that they represent 170 unique people.

## Turkmenistan: why a general child development survey cannot supply an autism figure

In February 2026, UNICEF and Turkmenistan's State Statistics Committee presented the results of **MICS7**, a large survey of children and women. Data collection took place in 2024.

Such surveys help describe child health, education and development. But measures of child functioning, learning difficulties or disability **are not an autism diagnosis**.

The reviewed public materials did not provide a reliable nationwide ASD estimate with verified methods, ages and reporting period. No unverified number has therefore been inserted for Turkmenistan.

This is **a gap in confirmed accessible evidence**, not a claim that Turkmenistan has no autism research or medical services, or that no autistic people live there.

[Official UNICEF announcement of MICS7](https://www.unicef.org/turkmenistan/press-releases/turkmenistan-releases-mics7-survey-findings-report-step-forward-childrens-data).

## Why the WHO global estimate does not replace national data

In 2025, the World Health Organization reported that approximately **one in 127 people** globally was estimated to be autistic in 2021.

This is a model-based worldwide population estimate, **not a measured child prevalence figure for five Central Asian countries**.

Online discussions sometimes multiply a country's population by the global proportion and present the result as its autistic population. This may illustrate a possible scale, but **it is not nationally observed prevalence**.

A valid country comparison requires local samples, consistent diagnostic criteria and appropriate handling of different population ages.

### What about international model-based estimates?

Sources such as [Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/number-with-autistic-spectrum-disorder) present estimates derived from global burden-of-disease models. Models can help explore possible levels when primary data are incomplete.

But a modelled number is **not a count of people individually examined and clinically confirmed in that country**. Where research is sparse, estimates may rely heavily on assumptions and data from other populations.

For that reason, model-based results are not placed alongside administrative registers in one numerical column. A future comparison of model estimates on AutismHub would need its own explanation of methods and uncertainty.

## What a scientific review of the five countries found

In 2024, *Autism* published a review by **Rano Zakirova-Engstrand and Gulnoza Yakubova** of autism-related research conducted in five Central Asian countries.

The literature search ended before 2022. Of the publications identified, **11 met the inclusion criteria**, ten concerning Kazakhstan and one Uzbekistan.

No study from Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan or Turkmenistan met those criteria within that historical search. The authors also identified a lack of research on monitoring systems and epidemiological infrastructure.

This describes **the research literature available for the review's period**, not a claim that no later studies exist in those countries as of 2026. For example, the Tajikistan paper discussed above appeared after the search period.

[Review in the journal Autism](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613231170553).

## What information a reliable map of Central Asia needs

To compare countries in a single table, the same variables must be collected:

| Required information | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| A consistent age range | Rates for children under 14, 16 and 18 are different |
| A uniform definition of ASD | Childhood autism, atypical autism and pervasive developmental disorders may use different categories |
| Clearly defined indicator type | New diagnoses, registered children and prevalence estimates are not interchangeable |
| Child population denominator | Makes it possible to calculate rates per 100,000 children |
| Same reference date | Prevents mixing results from different years |
| Assessment of undercounting | Helps account for children outside public registers |
| Full source description | Shows whether a number comes from a register, survey or model |

Without these conditions, a numerical diagram covering five countries could appear authoritative while creating false precision.

## What this means for parents

Differences in published statistics do not mean parents in one Central Asian country should worry more about autism than in another. Counts of recorded diagnoses primarily reflect **how many cases a particular system identified and registered**.

It is more useful to find out what support is available locally:

- timely discussion of a child's development with a qualified professional
- professional assessment when concerns about development arise
- clear explanations of a child's needs and strengths
- individually appropriate educational and family support
- reliable research information in a language people can use.

A low count in a government report does not erase families' real needs. A high count does not mean that children in that country necessarily require more medical treatment.

## What we know and what remains unknown

**Confirmed:** separate administrative indicators and scientific studies exist across the region. Counting methods differ considerably. Kazakhstan has published a medical-monitoring child count and Tajikistan a study of registrations across six years.

**Partly confirmed:** different kinds of Kyrgyzstan registration figures have been reported with attribution to public authorities, but a single accessible primary dataset and the precise comparability of its categories were not independently verified.

**Not established from the reviewed sources:** true autism prevalence measured through a consistent population-based method across all five countries. Comparable national ASD measures could not be confirmed for Uzbekistan or Turkmenistan.

**Main conclusion:** Central Asia needs not only more figures but more transparent methods for collecting them. A scientifically useful map begins by stating **what is known, where the numbers came from and what the evidence cannot support**.
