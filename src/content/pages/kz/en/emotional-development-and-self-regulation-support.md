A child cries when a walk ends, covers their ears in a shop, scatters blocks when a building falls, goes silent in a crowded place or struggles to switch from a favourite activity to getting dressed.

Adults often want to “calm” the child immediately. But **developing self-regulation does not mean suppressing emotions or always appearing calm**. The child needs safety and a response to their communication, and over time may develop more accessible ways to request help, a break or support.

This guide considers what can help **before a difficult moment, during severe distress and afterwards**. It is not an autism test or individual treatment plan.

## 1. Emotions, self-regulation and co-regulation

**Emotions** help us respond to events: joy, sadness, fear, curiosity, anger and other feelings. No emotion is inherently “wrong.” Adults nevertheless have a responsibility to keep people safe if a child's behaviour could cause harm.

**Self-regulation** is a developing ability to notice one's state, adjust to or tolerate demands and use suitable ways to cope. Young children are not required to calm themselves alone.

**Co-regulation** means an adult helps the child manage demands: making the situation more predictable, noticing signals, offering support, respecting boundaries and avoiding further escalation.

For example, if a loud sound frightens a child, an adult can reduce the noise, offer a quieter room and say, “Too loud. Let's go somewhere quiet.” Teaching a “correct reaction” immediately is not necessary.

AAP describes co-regulation as an important aspect of responsive relationships. CDC also recommends helping young children notice and name feelings while maintaining safety and clear boundaries.

## 3. Noticing demands and overload in advance

Before a difficult situation, consider:

- Is the child hungry, thirsty or sleepy?
- Is there pain, constipation, illness, fever or another physical discomfort?
- Is the environment too loud, bright, crowded or hot?
- Does the child understand what comes next?
- Can they say “no,” request help or ask for a break in an accessible way?
- Have many tasks, people or transitions occurred in a short period?

This is **not a checklist of “bad behaviour causes.”** A few answers cannot establish why a child is distressed, but they can highlight conditions an adult might change.

### Example: returning from childcare

A child returns home and becomes upset by small things. Instead of another lesson or detailed questioning, offer water, food if needed, quiet and familiar objects.

If a rest helps, that is a useful observation. It **does not prove** that childcare or sensory overload was the only cause. Discuss repeated significant difficulties with educators and professionals.

## 4. Twelve practical situations and possible responses

Adapt these examples to the individual child. You need not use everything, and the child's preferences and right to refuse matter more than completing a script.

| Situation | What to try | Short phrase |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Play needs to end** | Show the next step beforehand and offer a choice of which toy to put away first. | “A little more play, then dinner. The car can go on the shelf.” |
| **2. A shop is too loud** | Move away from noise, offer an exit or quiet spot and familiar hearing protection if appropriate. | “It's loud here. We can leave.” |
| **3. Building blocks will not fit** | Acknowledge frustration and offer help or a break without changing the game without consent. | “That's difficult. Help or a break?” |
| **4. Time to get dressed** | Use fewer instructions, offer comfortable clothing and consider unpleasant fabrics. | “Socks first or T-shirt?” |
| **5. The child is angry after being told no** | Acknowledge feelings, keep necessary boundaries and offer a safe alternative. | “You're angry. I understand. Hitting is not OK. I'm here.” |
| **6. The child does not want to talk after childcare** | Postpone questions, allow rest and support accessible requests. | “You can rest. I'm nearby if you need me.” |
| **7. Visitors arrive** | Give advance notice, allow a quiet separate space and do not demand greetings or hugs. | “Visitors are coming. You can stay in your room.” |
| **8. The usual route changes** | Briefly explain and show the new route or next stop. | “A different road today. Then we're home.” |
| **9. The child cannot explain what is wrong** | Offer gesture, picture, AAC or genuine choices without demanding speech. | “Pain? Too loud? Break? Show me if you can.” |
| **10. Waiting for a turn** | Show approximately how long the wait is and offer an independent activity. | “We are waiting. Book or toy?” |
| **11. The child refuses touch** | Do not insist on hugs or massage. Offer to stay nearby at a distance. | “OK, I won't touch. I'm here.” |
| **12. Tension before bedtime** | Reduce light, noise and tasks, retain familiar routines and check discomfort. | “Quiet time now. Then a book if you want.” |

**The goal is not to eliminate every tear or protest.** It is to reduce unnecessary distress, prevent unsafe actions and support accessible communication of needs.

## 5. Five approaches to try during calm periods

### Approach 1. “Now - next”

Instead of a long warning, use two familiar pictures or objects:

- **Now:** playing.
- **Next:** having lunch.

Show the cue in advance if the child understands it. Do not turn it into a threat or force actions despite strong discomfort.

### Approach 2. The right to request a break

During calm periods, offer an accessible way to communicate “break,” such as a word, gesture, large card or AAC button. Then **actually provide a break** whenever it is safe.

The child should not have to “earn” rest or formulate a perfect request.

### Approach 3. Two genuine choices

Where possible, offer two equally acceptable and safe options: “Sit in the quiet room or take a walk?” or “Should I stay near you or move away?”

Avoid false choices with threats such as “Do it now or lose your favourite toy.”

### Approach 4. A calm place without punishment

Provide a safe, accessible space with gentle light and familiar objects. It might be a corner, a chair or another room from which the child can call an adult.

**Never lock a child away, physically hold them there or use the space as punishment.** Some children do not want to be alone.

### Approach 5. Notice comfortable sensations and ways to recover

When calm, explore what the child enjoys: soft music, a book, movement, a favourite blanket, drawing or quiet time.

Offer breathing games **only voluntarily**. Demanding “deep breaths” during intense overload may increase distress. Sensory tools are not universally suitable. Avoid unsafe restrictive practices.

## 6. Four illustrated communication cards

![Four communication cards: BREAK, HELP, QUIETER and STOP, with a seated child, outstretched hands, a finger near the lips and raised palms.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/parent-emotions-communication-cards-approved-2026-10-26.webp)

These cards are **examples**, not a standardised communication system. They can be printed and tried to see whether the child understands them.

| Message | How an adult can respond |
| --- | --- |
| **Break** | Pause a nonessential activity and provide a safe break. |
| **Help** | Approach and clarify the needed help accessibly. |
| **Quieter** | Reduce noise or offer another space where possible. |
| **Stop** | End an unwanted touch, game or other nonessential activity. |

A child can communicate these messages **without cards**, too. Walking away, covering ears, a refusal gesture or individual AAC also deserve attention. Consider accessibility for visual and motor difficulties.

## 7. When the child is already overwhelmed

There is no universal sequence. Often it helps to:

1. **Check safety.** Remove sharp or heavy objects from immediate risk areas and give others space where possible.
2. **Consider medical causes.** Pain, illness, injury, hearing or digestive problems may accompany severe distress.
3. **Reduce demands.** Lower music volume, reduce crowds and postpone nonessential tasks.
4. **Speak briefly.** Offer a clear choice without long questions. Sometimes being calmly available without speaking is better.
5. **Respect space and refusal.** Do not force hugs, restrain the body, demand eye contact or impose unwanted touch.
6. **Allow recovery.** When ready, return to routines or briefly discuss what helped.

Seek emergency assistance if there is an immediate risk of serious harm. Do not use this website as an instruction manual for physical restraint or crisis intervention.

### If the child screams, cries or stops responding

Outward reactions **cannot establish the cause of an episode on their own**. Intense overload in autistic children is sometimes described as a “meltdown” or “shutdown,” but these are not standalone diagnoses or explanations for all episodes. Pain, fear, changes to surroundings or ordinary disappointment may also be involved.

See [“Meltdowns and shutdowns in autism”](../meltdaun-i-shatdaun-pri-autizme/). Do not assume every cry is a meltdown or every silence a shutdown.

## 8. Supporting the child after a difficult episode

When the episode ends, **there is no need to demand an immediate explanation of “why you behaved that way.”** Allow recovery and assess whether medical help is needed.

Later, if the child is ready, you can:

- Briefly name the event without blame: “The shop was very loud.”
- Acknowledge their message: “You showed that you wanted to leave.”
- Ask a preference: “Next time, shall we go earlier or bring ear defenders?”
- Discuss environmental changes with teachers or other adults.
- Record one or two observations to understand patterns over time.

Do not demand an explanation, apology or admission of “bad behaviour” after intense overload. When real harm occurs, ensure safety and later help repair relationships without humiliating the child.

## 9. Seven mistakes to avoid

| Avoid | Try instead |
| --- | --- |
| “Calm down immediately!” | Offer safety, help or a break in simple words. |
| Demanding eye contact. | Accept different forms of attention and response. |
| Holding a child for a “required hug.” | Respect refusal of touch while remaining available. |
| Assuming all crying is manipulation. | Consider feelings, surroundings, comprehension, pain and needs. |
| Punishing the expression of emotion. | Set limits on unsafe actions without banning feelings. |
| Applying the same sensory technique to everyone. | Consider individual preferences and contraindications. |
| Drawing conclusions from one episode. | Observe context and patterns, and consult a clinician if needed. |

## 10. A short observation diary

Record **observable facts**, not assumed psychological causes or scores. Even two or three episodes may help discussions with professionals.

| Field | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Before** | “After childcare we went straight to a supermarket with loud music.” |
| **What happened** | “The child covered their ears, cried and pulled the adult towards the exit.” |
| **What we tried** | “We left the shop and stayed together somewhere quiet.” |
| **What we noticed** | “After a while, the child stopped covering their ears and asked for water.” |
| **Question** | “Could we plan shopping differently or check other causes of distress?” |

The record **cannot establish** a sensory disorder or show that music was the sole cause. It simply documents conditions worth exploring.

## 11. When professional help is needed

Consult a paediatrician or another appropriate professional if distress episodes are frequent, severe or substantially disrupt daily life, especially if:

- The child injures themselves, runs away dangerously or there are significant safety risks.
- There are marked changes in behaviour, sleep, eating, activity or usual communication.
- Pain, physical illness or other medical symptoms are possible.
- Previously established language, social or motor skills are lost.
- The family cannot maintain safety and everyday routines.

**Sudden confusion, loss of consciousness, breathing difficulty, severe injury or another immediate health threat** requires urgent medical attention.

Aggression and self-injury call for professional assessment of possible causes, surroundings, communication and health. NICE advises consideration of pain, co-occurring conditions, sensory needs, unpredictability and communication barriers rather than simply labelling the child “bad.”

## 13. What is supported, and what remains unknown

CDC, AAP and WHO guidance emphasises responsive adults, understandable communication and supportive relationships. NICE and NHS highlight the roles of the physical environment, pain, predictability, sensory needs and communication access in severe distress.

There is no evidence that a particular picture card, “quiet corner,” universal breathing technique or emotion chart **will always prevent distress episodes or suit every child**. The value of any approach depends on circumstances and on whether it supports safety, comfort and participation.

Where formal intervention is required, it should be tailored and delivered by qualified professionals. This article does not reproduce the full WHO Caregiver Skills Training programme.

## Key points to remember

**Before distress:** predictability, rest and accessible choice.

**During distress:** safety, checking health, reducing unnecessary demands and respecting boundaries.

**After distress:** recovery, nonjudgmental observation and clarification of needs.

The goal is not for a child always to look calm, but to feel safer and understood and gradually gain accessible ways to communicate emotions and needs.
