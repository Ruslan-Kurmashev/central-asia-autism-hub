## Why study school inclusion as a research question

Whether an autistic child can attend a mainstream school is often discussed as an administrative question about admission. The research question is broader: **what conditions allow a child not simply to be present at school, but to take part in school life, communicate, learn and feel safe**?

Inclusive education means organising teaching to accommodate differences among pupils. For autistic children, this may involve accessible communication, a clear school-day structure, flexible tasks, a predictable environment, sensory needs and opportunities to participate with other children.

Yet two autistic children may have very different strengths, needs and preferences. **There is no single school adaptation that works for everyone with autism.**

Kazakhstan has published studies involving parents, special educators, teacher trainees and practising teachers. They help explain barriers in the education system, but not all directly measure **the educational outcomes of autistic children**. The types of evidence are distinguished below.

## School access, presence, participation and learning: what is the difference?

![Original AutismHub diagram showing school access, presence, participation, learning and conditions supporting inclusive education.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/research/kazakhstan-inclusive-education-participation-path-en.svg)

*Figure 1. An AutismHub editorial illustration. These are conceptual questions for examining a school environment, not an official national assessment, measured statistics or a diagnostic tool.*

| Level | Question for parents and schools | What should not be considered sufficient |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Access** | Can the child enrol in an appropriate school? | An enrolment record alone |
| **Presence** | Do conditions allow regular participation in the school day? | Physical presence in the building without necessary support |
| **Participation** | Can the child communicate, join activities and take part in shared tasks? | Continued exclusion from common classroom activities |
| **Learning** | Can the child understand and engage with the curriculum in an accessible way? | Identical assignments without considering individual needs |

This is a useful educational model but has not itself been tested as a standardised scientific scale. It cannot be converted into a score, school ranking or medical conclusion.

Sample size alone does not establish the quality of a study. Interviews help us understand how people describe their experiences. Surveys of thousands of teachers reveal patterns in their responses. But **no questionnaire measuring teachers' opinions proves that individual pupils improved their academic skills or wellbeing**.

Moreover, most larger studies covered multiple types of special educational needs and disabilities. They cannot be described as studies solely of autistic pupils.

## Study 1. What parents of autistic children reported

In *Families in Transition*, published online in August 2018 and in a 2020 journal issue, **Sofiya An, Chee Kai Chan and Botagoz Kaukenova** of Nazarbayev University examined the experiences of **17 parents** of autistic children in two major Kazakhstan cities.

Focus-group participants discussed access to healthcare, social services and education. Themes included difficulties accessing special education, a shortage of inclusive programmes, limited professional support and insufficient public awareness.

Qualitative research is particularly valuable for understanding **how problems arise**, rather than simply how many people report them.

However, 17 parents do not represent all families in Kazakhstan. The study also does not establish that every parent faces the same challenges today, years after the data were collected.

**Main implication:** access to education should be assessed alongside families' real experiences, not only administrative records and the number of available places.

[An, Chan and Kaukenova, original study](https://doi.org/10.1080/1034912X.2018.1499879).

## Study 2. What teachers working with autistic children lacked

In 2022, **U. R. Yessirkepova, G. Zh. Lekerova and S. D. Polatova** published a study of special education teachers' difficulties in *The Journal of Psychology and Sociology*.

The authors conducted individual interviews with **16 teachers of special classes at School No. 39 named after M. Zhumabayev**. Teachers discussed their preparation, experience with autistic pupils and ways of organising lessons.

They described varying understandings of autism, differences in approaches to teaching and limited specialised preparation during initial teacher education.

Two limitations matter:

1. **These were special-class teachers at one school**, not a random national sample of mainstream school staff.
2. The researchers gathered **teachers' views**. They did not compare teaching methods in a controlled experiment or measure subsequent pupil learning outcomes.

The findings therefore support further examination of teacher preparation, but **do not demonstrate** that one specific educational method is more effective than another.

[Special-class teacher study, 2022](https://doi.org/10.26577/JPsS.2022.v81.i2.04).

## Study 3. What teachers in four Central Asian countries think about inclusion

In May 2024, **Daria Prisiazhniuk, Tsediso Michael Makoelle and Irina Zangieva** published a study in *Children and Youth Services Review*.

They surveyed **869 teachers** from Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan and Uzbekistan. Statistical analysis examined teachers' attitudes towards schooling for pupils with a broad range of special educational needs and disabilities.

The authors identified **six groups of attitudes**, ranging from more favourable to mixed and negative. Attitudes towards inclusive schooling were therefore not uniform across participants.

### Why 869 should not be described as the number of teachers from Kazakhstan

The figure **869** refers to the **combined sample across four countries**, not to Kazakhstan teachers alone. Nor can we assume that every respondent worked with autistic children: the pupil categories covered a much wider range of needs.

The study helps explain the regional educational context, but **does not measure the views of Kazakhstan's entire population on autism** and is not a national trial of improving autistic pupils' learning.

[Prisiazhniuk and colleagues, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.childyouth.2024.107535).

## Study 4. How ready are future special education teachers?

In 2024, **Zulfiya Movkebayeva, Darkhan Bilyalov, Bibigul Dyussenbayeva, Ulbosyn Kudaibergenova and Ainur Kabdyrova** published a study of prospective specialists' preparation.

It included **1,005 special education students** at eight purposively selected Kazakhstan universities.

Participants completed a questionnaire on their perceived preparedness to teach children with different disabilities and developmental differences. They tended to rate themselves as more prepared for some speech or hearing difficulties, and less prepared for complex or substantial support needs.

The authors also identified concerns about learning outcomes, communication between children and parents' expectations.

### What does 'self-rated professional preparedness' mean?

A student's confidence in their knowledge **does not demonstrate** professional competence in a real classroom.

A more objective assessment would require observation of teaching, practical skills assessments and analysis of pupils' educational outcomes. These were not the main measures in the 2024 study.

**Practical implication:** teacher education programmes should be considered not just in terms of course lists, but also access to real practice, supervision and methodological support.

[Movkebayeva and colleagues, 2024](https://ejer.com.tr/article-view/?id=1862).

## Study 5. Barriers described by 41 special educators

In another 2024 study, **Darkhan Bilyalov, Zulfiya Movkebayeva, Dana Khamitova and Aitolkyn Duzelbayeva** interviewed **41 special educators** from eight Kazakhstan cities.

They used semi-structured interviews and thematic analysis. Participants described six interrelated areas of difficulty:

- insufficient resources and time
- challenges collaborating with parents
- inadequate methodological guidance
- school infrastructure and organisational barriers
- the volume and complexity of documentation
- challenges providing individual behavioural support when needed.

These were themes **emerging from participants' accounts**, not six universal explanations for unsuccessful inclusion at every school.

The authors also discussed professional burnout and the need for practical teacher training. The study did not randomly compare schools using different staff training programmes.

[Bilyalov and colleagues, 2024](https://ejer.com.tr/article-view/?id=1916).

## Study 6. What responses from 6,186 Kazakhstan teachers revealed

In May 2025, **Laura Butabayeva and colleagues** published a Kazakhstan validation study of the **Teacher Efficacy for Inclusive Practices (TEIP)** scale in the *International Journal of Developmental Disabilities*.

The participants were **6,186 teachers** from three cities of national significance and 17 regions. Among them, **3,821 (61.8%)** worked in rural schools and **2,365 (38.2%)** in urban schools.

The researchers examined three aspects of teachers' perceived self-efficacy:

| TEIP component | Mean on a 1-6 scale |
| --- | ---: |
| Managing classroom behaviour | **4.45** |
| Collaboration | **4.48** |
| Inclusive instruction | **4.66** |
| Overall average | **4.53** |

*Source: [Butabayeva and colleagues, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/20473869.2025.2492091). Scores indicate teachers' perceptions of their own professional confidence, not school quality grades.*

### Why a high TEIP score does not guarantee good teaching

The scale captures how confident teachers feel in their ability to organise inclusive instruction, collaborate with others and handle demanding classroom situations.

But confidence **is not the same as** independently measured teaching effectiveness. Nor does it ensure that the school has the staff, adaptations, equipment and time needed to apply those practices.

The authors found differences associated with teacher characteristics, including preparation and work experience. These are **observational associations**, not proof that a particular training course automatically improved children's outcomes.

The study is valuable because the researchers tested the measurement properties of the scale in the Kazakhstan context rather than simply translating a foreign questionnaire. Nevertheless, this does not turn TEIP into a measure of an individual child's autism, support needs or academic achievement.

[Original study by Butabayeva and colleagues, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/20473869.2025.2492091).

## Another perspective: a survey of Almaty teachers

In October 2025, **Darkhan Orynbassarova, Abdidauitkozha Maulen and Kamila Rollan** published an article online that was subsequently included in a 2026 journal issue.

They surveyed **59 teachers working in inclusive classrooms in Almaty**, exploring educational opportunities for pupils with special educational needs and their transition into working life.

Respondents described limited resources, large classes, difficulties providing individual support and inadequate preparation for students' transition to employment.

**Limitation:** this was a survey of 59 teachers' views in one city, not a comparison of autistic pupils' learning outcomes across Kazakhstan's schools.

[Orynbassarova and colleagues, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/1034912X.2025.2571766).

## What UNICEF research and reports add

Surveys of teachers are complemented by education-system assessments undertaken by UNICEF Kazakhstan with national partners.

### Evaluation of government education programmes

In 2024, UNICEF published *Evidence on Inclusive Education in Kazakhstan based on a Formative and a Big Data Evaluation*.

It combined two distinct sources of evidence:

1. A **formative evaluation of education programmes from 2011 to 2021**, examining how the inclusive system developed and operated.
2. **Analysis of public online discussions** about education, including topics and views appearing in social media and other media.

This combination can highlight governance questions and public expectations. But social media comments **are not a representative survey of all parents**. Those who do not post online may have different experiences.

[UNICEF report, 2024](https://www.unicef.org/kazakhstan/en/reports/evidence-inclusive-education-kazakhstan-based-formative-and-big-data-evaluation).

### Accessible communication and assistive technology

In December 2025, UNICEF published a separate review of children's needs for assistive technology and its provision in Kazakhstan.

*Assistive technology* includes technical, digital and other tools that help children access learning. One example is **augmentative and alternative communication (AAC)**: images, symbols, communication boards or suitable devices through which a child can express themselves.

These tools may matter for some autistic children who use little or no spoken language. But not all autistic children need the same tool.

The report stresses that effective provision requires **needs assessment, trained staff, equipment and use within real learning activities**.

This was a review of the assistive-technology ecosystem as a whole, not a randomised clinical trial of one technology specifically for autistic children.

[UNICEF assistive technology report, 2025](https://www.unicef.org/kazakhstan/en/reports/assistive-technologies-childs-needs-assessment-and-ecosystem-kazakhstan).

## What conditions can make learning genuinely accessible?

Recurring themes in the studies and reports suggest **areas to discuss with a school**. These are neither mandatory interventions for every child nor a newly approved national AutismHub method.

### 1. Clear lesson content

Some children may benefit from instructions presented not only verbally but also in short written steps, diagrams or concrete examples. Others may need alternative ways of showing what they know.

The goal of adaptation is **to preserve access to learning**, not automatically to lower every expectation placed on the child.

### 2. Accessible communication

A school needs to understand how the child expresses agreement, refusal, interest, fatigue and requests for help. Limited spoken language does not mean a child lacks understanding or cannot learn.

AAC and other communication supports should be chosen according to individual needs, not prescribed as a universal 'autism method'.

### 3. Sensory differences and the school environment

For some children, noise, harsh lighting, crowded spaces or unexpected changes can make participation considerably harder.

Possible changes should be discussed with the child, family and educators. **Sensory needs cannot be inferred uniformly from an autism diagnosis**, and adaptations should be assessed according to their actual usefulness.

### 4. Teacher preparation and support

The studies highlight the importance of time, practice-based preparation, methodological support and professional collaboration. But the number of courses a teacher completes does not by itself tell us whether pupils' learning has improved.

The opportunity to apply knowledge in actual classrooms also needs assessment.

### 5. Family-school communication

Studies of parents and teachers show that communication between them may be difficult. Discussion of specific goals and conditions, without mutual blame, is more constructive.

When parents describe behaviour at home and teachers see something different at school, this may reflect differences in environments and demands. Such observations should be **considered together** rather than assuming one account is incorrect.

## Practical questions to discuss with a school

Parents do not need to memorise every study. Research is more useful when it helps them ask concrete questions:

| Question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| How will the child receive and understand classroom instructions? | To discuss access to learning, not just enrolment |
| Which communication methods are available throughout the school day? | So the child can communicate needs and participate |
| What helps the child join group activities? | To assess opportunities to take part in shared learning |
| Which environmental adaptations are planned, and how will their usefulness be assessed? | To avoid replacing individual support with a standard package |
| How are the goals and observations of the family and teachers coordinated? | To bridge differences between home and school |
| How does the school track learning progress and the child's wellbeing? | To evaluate outcomes rather than attendance alone |

This table is an **editorial list of questions**, not a legal requirement for schools or a validated rating scale.

For details on Kazakhstan's current education pathway, PMPK and psychological-pedagogical support services, see AutismHub's separate guide: [Autism and school in Kazakhstan: enrolment, PMPK and educational support](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/en/help-kazakhstan/autism-school-enrolment-support-kazakhstan/).

## What the available studies cannot establish

### A teacher questionnaire cannot measure the percentage of autistic pupils in 'successful inclusion'

TEIP responses, student preparedness questionnaires and attitude surveys measure **adult responses and perceptions**. They do not tell us how many autistic pupils study in an accessible setting or what educational outcomes they achieve.

Such conclusions require agreed outcome definitions and studies involving the pupils themselves.

### Interviews cannot rank one region as best or worst

Samples of special educators and parents were selected to explore experiences and problems, not to rank every region or school. Individual cities and institutions may differ in available resources.

### An educational programme's effectiveness cannot be demonstrated without a suitable design

A teacher's positive opinion of a teaching method or course is useful feedback. But claims that a programme improves communication, participation or educational outcomes require appropriate comparisons and predefined outcomes.

### Evidence involving all special educational needs cannot automatically be applied to autistic pupils

Broad studies of inclusive education help us understand school systems, but they do not automatically capture the experiences of autistic children, including their individual communication methods and sensory preferences.

## What research is particularly needed in Kazakhstan?

The remaining gaps point to several possible research priorities:

1. **Longitudinal studies of autistic pupils' school experiences**, tracking participation, accessible learning and quality of life.
2. **Evaluation of specific school adaptations** under real conditions, including benefits, acceptability and limitations.
3. **Research involving autistic pupils themselves**, not only parents and educators, with accessible and alternative communication options.
4. **Comparisons of urban and rural schools** in access to specialists, equipment and educational opportunities, without turning differences into a blame-based ranking.
5. **Assessment of teachers' preparation through demonstrated practical skills**, not only self-reports and certificates.
6. **Studies of transitions between education stages** and later learning or employment.

These are **research priorities proposed by AutismHub's editors**, based on the available literature, not a government-approved research programme.

## What is known and what still needs investigation

**Supported by available research:** studies from Kazakhstan and Central Asia identify teacher preparation, resource access, collaboration between professionals and families, and attitudes towards inclusion as important education-system issues.

**Supported with limitations:** in large samples, teachers and students reported differing degrees of preparedness or confidence. Their responses are not independent measures of professional competence or children's outcomes.

**Insufficiently studied:** which adaptations, in which schools and for which groups of autistic pupils improve participation, learning, wellbeing and long-term opportunities.

**Main conclusion:** **good inclusion does not end with school enrolment**. It should be judged by access to communication, the child's participation, meaningful opportunities to learn and attention to individual needs.
