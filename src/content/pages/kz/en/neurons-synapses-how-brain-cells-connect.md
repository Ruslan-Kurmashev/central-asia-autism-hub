## How do brain cells communicate?

When a child hears a familiar voice, picks up a toy or learns a new word, many cells in the nervous system are active at the same time. They receive signals, pass them on and help coordinate a response.

These cells are called **neurons**. They take part in how we perceive sounds, light and touch, move, remember and learn. Neurons do not work in isolation. Most everyday activities depend on interactions among large numbers of cells.

One way to picture this is as a communications network. A single telephone is not enough. There must be other people to connect with and a way to exchange messages. This is a simplified analogy, but it shows why the connections between cells matter as much as the cells themselves.

## What is a neuron made of, and what does it do?

A neuron has a cell body and specialised extensions. These extensions are usually divided into two main types.

**Dendrites** help the neuron receive signals from other cells. The **cell body** maintains the cell and participates in processing incoming information. The **axon** carries a nerve impulse towards other cells.

The signal travels along the axon through changes in the electrical properties of the cell membrane. This is not the same as electricity moving through an ordinary metal wire.

Neurons differ in their structure and function. Some carry information about sensations, some help control muscles, and others communicate within networks in the brain. A description of one neuron therefore cannot explain how the entire brain works.

The [NICHD overview of the nervous system](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/health/topics/neuro/conditioninfo/parts) explains these parts in more detail.

## What is a synapse, and how is a signal transmitted?

Neurons communicate through specialised points of contact called **synapses**.

At many synapses, communication is chemical. A very narrow gap called the **synaptic cleft** separates the cells. When a nerve impulse reaches the end of the first neuron, it may release chemical messengers called **neurotransmitters**.

These molecules cross the gap and bind to receptors on the next cell. Depending on the messenger, the receptors and the cell's current state, this may increase or decrease the likelihood that the next cell will become active.

The basic sequence is:

1. A neuron receives many incoming signals.
2. If a nerve impulse is generated, it travels along the axon.
3. The axon terminal releases neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft.
4. Receptors on the next cell respond to the chemical message.

This describes a **chemical synapse**, one of the main types of contact between neurons. **Electrical synapses** also exist, but they work differently.

Different neurotransmitters contribute to many processes. No single substance, on its own, determines speech, behaviour or autism. The US National Institute of General Medical Sciences offers a [plain-language explanation of neurotransmitters](https://nigms.nih.gov/biobeat/2024/08/what-is-a-neurotransmitter).

## Why do connections between neurons change?

The brain does not stop changing after birth. As a person develops and gains experience, some connections become stronger, others weaken, new contacts form, and existing ones are reorganised.

These changes are part of **neuroplasticity**. They contribute to learning and the development of skills. For example, when a child learns a new way to communicate, different brain networks are involved, and those networks continue to develop.

Neuroplasticity does not mean that every feature of the nervous system can be changed at will. Nor can we judge whether an activity is effective simply by claiming that it “creates more synapses”.

When evaluating support, it is more useful to look for observable changes in accessible communication, independence, participation in family life and the child's wellbeing.

## How might neuronal connections relate to autism?

Researchers study synapses partly because some genetic variants associated with autism affect proteins involved in forming and maintaining connections between nerve cells.

For example, the **SHANK3** gene provides instructions for a protein that helps organise certain synapses. Pathogenic changes in this gene can occur in particular neurodevelopmental conditions, but such a variant is not present in every autistic person. [MedlinePlus Genetics](https://medlineplus.gov/genetics/gene/shank3/) describes the function of SHANK3.

Researchers also study **NRXN1** and **SYNGAP1**, genes involved in different aspects of communication between neurons. These are examples of several molecular pathways, not features that must be present in autism.

Other studies examine changes in synaptic strength or reorganisation in experimental models. Their findings depend on the genetic variant, cell type and study conditions.

**There is no basis for claiming that all autistic people have too many synapses, too few synapses or synapses that function abnormally in the same way.** Autism is heterogeneous and cannot be reduced to a single defect in communication between cells.

## Why might sounds, light and touch feel different?

Some children are particularly sensitive to noise, bright light or touch. Others seek particular sensations, such as movement, pressure or repeated sounds.

Sensory perception relies on many cells and brain networks working together. Researchers investigate how the brain processes sensory input, allocates attention and regulates responses to stimulation.

But a single response to sound or touch cannot reveal which synapses might function differently or whether a child has a “deficiency” of a particular neurotransmitter. Current research does not support such a simple explanation.

Consider a child who finds a noisy room distressing. It is more useful to notice when discomfort occurs and, where possible, reduce the noise or offer a quieter space than to attribute the child's reaction to an assumed chemical imbalance.

Sensory differences deserve attention even when their precise cellular mechanisms are unknown.

## How do researchers study neurons and synapses?

Researchers use microscopy, electrophysiology, laboratory cell models and genetic studies to investigate individual nerve cells.

Each approach answers a different question. In a laboratory, scientists can measure how a cell responds to a signal or investigate the role of a particular protein. Genetic studies can identify variants associated with specific neurodevelopmental conditions.

MRI and EEG provide different kinds of information. For particular clinical or research questions, they may help assess brain structure or patterns of activity across groups of cells. They do not show the activity of every individual synapse.

Findings from experiments on cells or animals cannot be applied directly to an individual child's development. Further studies are needed before a laboratory finding can become an established clinical tool.

At present, no single test of synapses or neurotransmitters, EEG or MRI can independently confirm or rule out autism.

## What does this mean for parents?

Families do not need to memorise every part of a neuron or the names of synaptic proteins. It is more useful to understand that the brain continues to develop and that children can experience their surroundings in different ways.

Synapse research helps scientists investigate some of the biological mechanisms associated with autism. A single measurement, however, cannot tell us a child's abilities, needs or future.

If a child finds communication difficult, adults can explore accessible ways for the child to express needs and interests. If noise or touch causes discomfort, the environment can be adjusted. Other medical concerns should be assessed separately by an appropriate professional.

The purpose of support is not a promised “repair of synapses”. It is to build useful skills, reduce barriers and improve quality of life.
