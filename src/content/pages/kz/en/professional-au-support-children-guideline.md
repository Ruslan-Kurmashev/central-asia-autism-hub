| **Developer** | Autism CRC, Australia |
| **Type** | National professional guideline for delivering support |
| **Release date** | 16 February 2023 |
| **Number of recommendations** | 84 consensus-based recommendations with Good Practice Points |
| **NHMRC approval** | 6 December 2022, stated approval period of five years |
| **Population** | Primarily autistic children aged 0-12 and their families |
| **Types of support** | Non-pharmacological support in clinical and community settings |
| **Official source** | [Autism CRC - Supporting Autistic Children Guideline](https://www.autismcrc.com.au/best-practice/supporting-children) |

**Status:** NHMRC approval confirms the quality of the development process. Autism CRC itself states that the guideline **is not mandatory for all Australian practitioners**. For Kazakhstan, it does not constitute a national clinical or legal standard.

Autism CRC issued the document under its former name. On 10 August 2026, the organisation announced its renaming to Sylvia Rodger Institute. The historical developer name and document details are retained. [Developer announcement](https://www.autismcrc.com.au/news/latest-news/were-evolving).

## 1. Purpose of the guideline

The document is not concerned with establishing a diagnosis, but with **organising safe, beneficial and acceptable support for the child and family** after needs have been identified. Its central idea is to support learning, participation in life and wellbeing rather than choosing a programme solely by its name or the diagnosis.

The guideline primarily covers non-pharmacological support delivered by practitioners in clinical and community settings. Support specifically designed for delivery by educators directly within the education system, and pharmacological treatment, are **outside its main scope**. This does not mean that schools and treatment of co-occurring medical conditions are unimportant.

The guideline focuses on children aged 0-12 but takes a lifespan perspective. For example, an accessible way of communicating pain or asking for help matters not only for the current task, but also for future safety and independence.

## 2. Five sections of the guideline

| Part | Main question for the practitioner | Example of practical focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Guiding Principles | Are the child's rights, safety and preferences respected? | Family and child participation, respect for communication and culture |
| 2. Goal Setting | Is the goal meaningful to this particular child and family? | Functional, observable and agreed goals |
| 3. Selecting and Planning Supports | Why was this approach selected and who is competent to deliver it? | Benefits and risks, qualifications, alternatives and implementation plan |
| 4. Delivering Supports | How accessible and coordinated is delivery? | Format, intensity, participation of people around the child, supervision |
| 5. Outcomes, Quality and Safeguarding | How are outcomes assessed and harm prevented? | Monitoring, adjustment, stopping unnecessary or unwanted support |

This is an overview of the guideline structure, not a verbatim reproduction of all 84 recommendations.

## 3. Support starts with goals, not therapy names

The authors emphasise child and family participation in goal setting. A professional plan should not be reduced to a general aim such as reducing manifestations of autism.

Example of reframing professional goals:

| Vague goal | More useful question |
| --- | --- |
| Improve behaviour | In which situation does the child experience difficulty, and which communication supports or adjustments may help? |
| Develop sociability | How does the child want to participate in communication, and what barriers exist? |
| Reduce sensory characteristics | Which environmental changes can reduce overload and improve comfort? |

These are **illustrations of goal-setting principles** prepared by AutismHub, not quotations or ready-made prescriptions from Autism CRC.

## 4. Selecting and planning support

The official summary describes three complementary areas: developing skills personally meaningful to the child, increasing the competence of people around the child and creating a more accessible environment.

When choosing a programme, practitioners should discuss expected benefits, potential risks, family capacity, the child's preferences and the grounds for anticipated effectiveness. Practitioner qualifications, skills and supervision matter. If the required competence is absent, consider referral to an appropriate professional.

When planning intensity, consider demands on the child and family, resources, opportunities to use skills in everyday life and expected outcomes. Neither the diagnosis nor the status of the guideline establishes a universal number of hours.

**Neither the number of sessions itself nor the popularity of a commercial method establishes effectiveness for a particular goal.**

## 5. Coordination and delivery of support

The guideline emphasises choosing the format, conditions and participants with the family. Balance anticipated benefit against demands on the child.

Professional coordination may include agreeing goals across practitioners, clear documentation of decisions, discussing schedules and evaluating implementation in natural settings. Coordination does not mean that every institution is legally obliged to provide a personal coordinator under Kazakhstan law.

When assistants, parents or other practitioners participate in delivery, agree their roles, preparation, supervision and arrangements for contacting the responsible practitioner. This does not authorise procedures outside their competence.

### Consent and the child's willingness to participate

The developer separately considers consent and assent. Obtain legally valid consent under applicable law, and establish the child's willingness to participate in an accessible way. Absence or withdrawal of clear willingness calls for assessment of the situation, communication adjustments and discussion of next steps. The guideline does not prescribe one procedure for every age and circumstance. [Developer explanations](https://www.autismcrc.com.au/best-practice/supporting-children/guideline/reading-the-guideline).

## 6. Outcome monitoring and safeguarding

The fifth section concerns **Outcomes, Quality and Safeguarding**. It is necessary not only to document a goal but to establish in advance how actual outcomes will be evaluated.

It is useful to distinguish:

- **Child outcomes:** participation, communication, everyday independence, comfort or another meaningful goal.
- **Demands and safety:** adverse effects, stress, accessibility of participation and feedback.
- **Quality of delivery:** consistency with qualifications, the plan and agreed scope of work.
- **Decision about continuation:** whether the need remains, the programme requires adjustment or unnecessary intervention should stop.

Lack of desired progress requires review of goals, circumstances and initial assessment. The child or family must not automatically be blamed for insufficient motivation.

AutismHub example: if the child begins avoiding sessions and the agreed functional goal is not achieved, the team investigates possible causes, including fatigue, pain, environmental demands and adverse effects. It then discusses adjustments, a pause or stopping the programme with the child and family. Lack of progress alone does not justify increasing intensity.

## 7. Evidence: what NHMRC approval establishes

The authors used an evidence-based practice framework combining research, clinical expertise, consultations with autistic people and families and individual circumstances. Representatives of different groups participated in development, and more than 1000 people participated in community consultations.

**84 consensus-based recommendations are not 84 separate clinical trials or proof that one commercial programme is superior.** The guideline defines criteria for appropriate support delivery. Effectiveness of a specific intervention must be assessed against the relevant research literature and the individually defined goal.

The Reading the Guideline section provides for review within five years of first publication. This is a future review target, not confirmation that a new edition has been released.

## 8. Applicability in Kazakhstan

| Potentially useful principle | What must be checked in Kazakhstan |
| --- | --- |
| Shared goal setting with the family and child | Legal consent procedures and professional responsibility |
| Assessment of functional changes and quality of life | Available monitoring approaches and instruments |
| Environmental adaptation and support for natural communication | Educational and organisational circumstances |
| Preventing harm and avoiding unjustified promises | Legal authority, clinical protocols and service requirements |
| Coordination between practitioners | Actual referral pathways and data-sharing rules |

Kazakhstan regulatory documents are collected separately in the [catalogue of laws, orders and protocols](../kazakhstan-documents/). Foreign NHMRC approval should not be used as evidence that an approach is mandatory in Kazakhstan.
