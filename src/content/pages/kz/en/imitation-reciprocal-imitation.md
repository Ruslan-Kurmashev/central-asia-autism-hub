A child sees an adult stack one building block on another and tries the same action. A little later the adult notices that the child has arranged the blocks in a long row and copies their idea. Both can continue the game in their own way.

In the first example, the child imitates the adult. In the second, the adult imitates the child. Either may become part of shared play, but not every such exchange is a formal intervention.

Imitation is reproducing an observed action, gesture, sound or way of using an object. Reciprocal imitation adds a two-way exchange: adults also notice and copy children's initiatives. Reciprocal Imitation Training (RIT) is a distinct intervention studied mainly in young autistic children.

## Types of imitation

Children may imitate body movements, gestures, actions with toys, sequences in play, sounds or words. These abilities do not always develop together. A child might imitate actions with a toy but find imitating a gesture or sound difficult. This does not mean they lack all ability to imitate.

Imitation can support new ways of playing and everyday learning through observation. But imitation is not the same as obedience. "The child can learn a useful action by watching" is different from "the child must copy everything on command".

Sometimes the child imitates the meaning but not the precise movement. An adult taps a block on a box and the child taps a different object on a table. This may be a meaningful attempt. Research assessments, however, often use specific criteria for accuracy, so a family's assessment of practical value and a standardised imitation score may differ.

## Reciprocal imitation in play

Everyday play can include two-way imitation. The illustration presents three examples with the same characters.

![Three scenes of play: the mother models an action with blocks, then imitates the child's play with a toy car, and finally they play together with cars beside a bridge.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/diagrams/imitation-reciprocal-three-scenes-approved.webp)

1. **The child imitates the adult.** The mother stacks blocks. The child observes and tries the same action.
2. **The adult imitates the child.** The child moves a toy car, and the mother uses another car to follow the child's play.
3. **Shared play develops.** They play with cars beside a bridge. The adult may offer a new action, while the child can repeat it or invent a different one.

This illustrates everyday play, not a clinical RIT protocol.

Exact matching is not the only thing that matters. The exchange allows people to notice each other's actions and participate together. The adult is not only providing an example but also responding to what the child initiates.

Such activities can simply be play, without a therapeutic goal. If an adult or professional is intentionally teaching imitation, they should define what the child may gain and why it is meaningful.

## What Reciprocal Imitation Training involves

RIT was developed as a naturalistic intervention to support imitation during social interaction and play. In the studied approaches, adults copied children's actions, described them, modelled new object actions or gestures and created repeated opportunities for responses. Researchers assessed particular measures of elicited and spontaneous imitation.

Early RIT studies also used prompting, including physical prompts. It would therefore be inaccurate to describe the original protocol as entirely free play without demands or prompting. In current support, the child's assent, refusal, breaks and acceptable prompting must be discussed separately. Coercion should not be justified by the programme's name.

Not every instance of a parent copying a child's actions counts as RIT. Nor do all naturalistic developmental behavioural interventions (NDBI) follow the same protocol. A programme label does not tell a family how it will actually be delivered.

## What RIT studies found

### The 2010 pilot randomised trial

In Ingersoll's study, 21 young autistic children were included in the final analysis, 11 in the RIT group and ten in the control group. The RIT group showed larger gains in elicited and spontaneous imitation. [Ingersoll, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20155309/).

This is encouraging for those measured skills, but the sample was small and baseline elicited-imitation scores differed between groups. The study does not establish equal effectiveness for every child or automatic benefits for language and other domains.

### The 2021 review

Paparella and Freeman examined ten studies of motor-imitation interventions in young autistic children. Four concerned RIT, three video modelling, two ABA methods and one a naturalistic developmental behavioural approach. One used a randomised controlled design and another a quasi-experimental pre-post design. Most remaining studies used single-case designs.

The authors described generally positive changes for imitation of actions with objects and gestures, but called for stronger research with larger samples. This was a review of several imitation approaches, not a pooled meta-analysis of RIT alone. [Paparella and Freeman, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/B978-0-323-85031-5.00010-4).

## Generalisation and other developmental outcomes

A small study by Ingersoll and Schreibman (2006) involving five children reported generalisation of imitation to new contexts, alongside changes in play, language and joint attention. A comparison of RIT and video modelling by Cardon and Wilcox (2011) involved six boys, with variable imitation improvements in both conditions. It used a multiple-baseline design with small participant groups, so conclusions about one method being superior are not reliable.

In a separate randomised study involving 27 children, Ingersoll (2012) reported greater changes in initiation of joint attention and some socio-emotional outcomes after a focused imitation intervention. However, mediation analysis did not confirm that the social changes were explained by improvements in imitation itself. The socio-emotional outcomes were parent-reported, and the small sample limits generalisation.

These results do not justify the promise "teach movement imitation first and speech will necessarily follow". Motor imitation, vocal imitation, joint attention and language are related but distinct outcomes.

[Ingersoll and Schreibman, 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16568355/) · [Cardon and Wilcox, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20697791/) · [Ingersoll, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22146934/).

## Can parents use reciprocal imitation?

In a small study by Ingersoll and colleagues published in 2007, three families participated. Parents learned RIT strategies, and children's spontaneous imitation increased. Generalisation outside the initial sessions and one-month follow-up were also examined. This supports exploring parent participation, but it does not establish universal effectiveness or a need for formal training in every family.

[Ingersoll and Gergans, 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16603337/).

During ordinary play, a parent might notice the child's action, join in and, if the child is interested, introduce a small variation. This is an example of responsive interaction, not a do-it-yourself guide to delivering clinical RIT.

Every game does not have to become a lesson. If the child is tired or unwilling, change or end the activity rather than increasing demands.

## Communication, gestures and AAC

Shared play does not require speech. A child may indicate whether they want to repeat an action, choose an object or end play through gestures, pictures, a communication device or another form of augmentative and alternative communication (AAC).

Motor imitation should never be made a prerequisite for AAC access. A useful communication device should not be removed to practise vocal sounds or words.

## How to tell whether a skill is useful

Before starting formal imitation teaching, identify a functional goal. During play, for example, the child might independently try a new way of using a toy after observing an adult or peer. This is more meaningful than the vague goal "improve imitation".

Consider the difference between:

1. Spontaneously repeating a new action and doing so only after being told to "copy me".
2. Imitation without prompts and imitation after extra help.
3. Using the skill with new objects, people and settings.
4. Maintaining the skill over time.
5. Whether it helps with play, learning, communication or everyday activities.

Successfully copying one gesture does not establish that a skill has been learned and generalised. Conversely, not precisely copying one movement does not imply a lack of interest in shared play or an inability to learn another way.

## When to reconsider the approach

A separate imitation programme is not necessarily needed when a child already learns meaningful actions through observation or other methods. Review the plan when tasks lack a clear purpose, cause persistent distress, suppress the child's own initiatives or mainly reward following "copy me" instructions.

Do not demand perfect movement matching only for an assessment score, restrict communication or take away free play. These are principles of respectful, individualised support, not specific effects proven by the cited RIT studies.

## Questions for a professional in Kazakhstan

Imitation goals may appear within early intervention, speech and language support, education and psychological services. An exercise labelled "copy me" does not establish that RIT or any particular programme is being used.

Before sessions, ask:

1. What kind of imitation is targeted, and why does this child need it?
2. Is this a defined RIT protocol or another teaching approach?
3. What training and supervision does the professional have?
4. What prompts are used, and how are refusals and breaks respected?
5. How is independent imitation distinguished from following instructions?
6. Is generalisation to other actions, toys, people and settings assessed?
7. When and how will goals be reviewed if progress is limited?

A certificate or imported method name alone does not establish a practitioner's competence, safety or effectiveness.

## Key takeaways

Imitation can be a way to learn useful actions by observing others. Reciprocal imitation also allows adults to respond to a child's own actions rather than only setting the example. RIT uses these principles within a specific intervention.

Research suggests benefits for some imitation skills in young children, but samples are small and controlled evidence is limited. Improvements in imitation do not guarantee spoken language or broad social-development gains. What matters is whether the skill helps the child act independently and participate in meaningful activities.
