## What are sensory differences?

Our brains receive and process information from our senses. We hear sounds, see light, and experience smells, tastes, touch, body position and movement. Autistic people may process this information differently. For example, a sound that one person barely notices may be highly distracting or uncomfortable for someone else (WHO, 2025, National Autistic Society).

These differences do not mean a sensation is "imaginary" or that a child is deliberately exaggerating their response. At the same time, there is no single sensory profile that applies to every autistic person.

## Heightened and reduced sensitivity

**Hypersensitivity** means that certain sensations may feel especially intense or unpleasant. A person may cover their ears in everyday noise, avoid bright lights, particular fabrics or unexpected touch.

**Hyposensitivity** means that a person may respond less strongly to some sensations or notice them later. For example, they may prefer more vigorous movement, press harder on objects or seek particular kinds of tactile input.

These experiences can occur together. A child may avoid loud sounds but enjoy swings, like one type of fabric but refuse clothes made from another. Responses may also depend on tiredness, the environment, how predictable a situation is and other factors.

## Which sensations may be different?

- **Sound.** Classroom noise, household appliances, unexpected alarms or several conversations at once may be difficult to process.
- **Light and visual information.** Bright lamps, flickering light or a visually busy environment may be uncomfortable for some people.
- **Touch.** Clothing seams, haircuts, hair washing or an unexpected hug may feel unpleasant to one child even when another child finds them neutral.
- **Smells, tastes and food textures.** Sensitivity to smells and the consistency of food can influence food preferences, although feeding difficulties can also have other causes.
- **Movement and body position.** Some children enjoy spinning, rocking or active movement, whereas similar sensations may frighten or tire others.

These are examples, not required signs of autism. Sensory sensitivities also occur in people who are not autistic.

## What is sensory overload?

When many unpleasant or hard-to-process sensations arrive at the same time, a person may find it harder to concentrate, speak or continue an everyday activity. A busy shopping centre, for example, can combine music, bright lighting, many voices, smells and moving crowds.

A response may look like wanting to leave, covering the ears, freezing, crying, not responding or showing intense distress. **However, behaviour that looks similar is not always caused by sensory overload.** Pain, fear, not having an accessible way to ask for a break, hunger, tiredness or another condition can also affect behaviour (NICE CG170).

## What should parents notice in a child's behaviour?

Describing a specific situation is more useful than immediately assigning it an explanation.

For example, instead of saying "my child is being disobedient when the vacuum cleaner is switched on", you could observe that "after the vacuum cleaner starts, my child covers their ears and goes into another room". This is an observation that can be discussed and compared with what happens in other circumstances.

A few simple questions may help parents:

1. What happened immediately before the response?
2. What sounds, sensations, demands or changes were present?
3. How does the child communicate discomfort or ask for a break?
4. Could physical well-being or another cause also be relevant?

Do not intentionally expose a child to uncomfortable sensations simply to "test" their sensitivity.

## Why do the child's own preferences matter?

What helps one person feel calm may be unpleasant to another. One child may benefit from quiet, while another prefers familiar music or movement. Preferences can change throughout the day, so no universal set of sensory strategies works for everyone.

If a child does not use spoken language, pay attention to the ways they can communicate comfort, refusal or choice. Whenever possible, changes to the environment should be discussed with the child and with adults familiar with their everyday responses.

## When should you speak with a professional?

It can be helpful to seek advice if sensory difficulties significantly affect sleep, eating, learning, hygiene, communication or visiting familiar places. If a strong reaction, pain or other symptoms appear suddenly, do not assume autism explains everything. Seek an appropriate medical assessment.

This article introduces sensory differences. It is not a diagnostic tool or a therapy plan.
