## What is stimming?

**Stimming** (short for *self-stimulatory behaviour*) refers to repeated movements, sounds or actions. A child might rock, move their hands, tap an object, repeat a tune or rub fabric that feels pleasant to touch.

Repetitive actions also occur in non-autistic people. For example, someone might bounce a leg or twirl a pen. The frequency, visibility, function and importance of these actions to a person's wellbeing may differ. **Stimming on its own does not establish an autism diagnosis** (National Autistic Society).

## What kinds of repetitive actions are there?

| Category | Possible examples |
| --- | --- |
| Movements | Rocking, hand flapping, jumping or spinning. |
| Touch | Rubbing fabric or squeezing a safe object. |
| Sounds | Humming, tapping or repeating a melody or sound. |
| Visual experiences | Watching a repeating pattern or the movement of a toy. |
| Body position | Rhythmically changing posture or swaying while seated. |

These are examples, not a test. One person may use several forms of repetitive movement, while another may show few noticeable repetitive actions.

## Why might a child do this?

1. **Joy.** Some people flap their hands or jump when excited about something pleasant.
2. **Self-regulation.** A rhythmic action may help someone manage many impressions or remain calm.
3. **Pleasant sensations.** Touching a particular surface or repeating a sound may feel enjoyable in itself.
4. **Response to stress.** Repetitive movements sometimes increase during tiredness, anxiety or sensory overload.

Avoid assigning a single meaning to a movement in advance. Rocking, for instance, can accompany excitement or tiredness, or be an attempt to calm down. The context matters, as does the child's own explanation if they are able to communicate it.

## Should stimming be stopped?

**If an action is safe, causes no significant harm and does not create substantial barriers, it does not need to be stopped automatically** simply because it looks unusual. The National Autistic Society describes harmless stimming as a possible form of self-regulation and warns about the negative effects of forcing autistic people to mask their characteristics.

More useful questions are: “What does this action do for the child?”, “Are they comfortable?” and “Would support help here?”

For example, gently rocking while listening to a story does not prove that a child is not paying attention. But if spinning near a staircase creates a risk of injury, making the environment safe comes first.

## Stimming, focused interests, routines and echolalia

These concepts may overlap, but they are not identical.

- **Stimming** usually describes repeated movements, sensations and sounds.
- **Focused interests** concern topics or activities that are particularly important to a person.
- **Familiar routines** may make everyday life more predictable.
- **Echolalia** means repeating heard words or phrases. Sometimes it serves a communicative purpose.

In clinical descriptions, some of these behaviours are grouped under restricted or repetitive patterns of behaviour and interests. In daily life, their functions can be different.

## When is additional help needed?

Pay particular attention if an action:

- leads to head banging, skin injury, hair pulling or other harm
- appears suddenly or changes noticeably
- occurs alongside pain, severe fear or worsening wellbeing
- creates an immediate safety risk for the person or others
- interferes with essential daily activities so much that the child or family needs support.

**Not every self-injurious action is stimming.** It can have medical, sensory, emotional or other causes. NICE recommends considering physical pain, sleep problems, communication, anxiety and the environment before drawing conclusions about the reasons for behaviours that cause concern.

Serious injury or an immediate threat to safety requires medical attention. Do not use painful or restrictive interventions based on advice found online.

## Example: one movement, different explanations

A child flaps their hands before entering a play centre. They might be happy, anxious about the noise, looking forward to a familiar activity or simply enjoying the movement.

Their intention cannot be established from the gesture alone. It can help to notice what happened beforehand, how the child communicated their wishes and what changed after moving to another environment.

Such observations are not a diagnosis. If there are substantial difficulties, a professional will consider the broader context.

## What should parents remember?

1. Safe repetitive actions do not necessarily need correcting.
2. Explore the meaning of a movement rather than guessing from its appearance.
3. Sudden changes or self-injury require assessment of possible causes and safety.
4. Accessible communication helps a child express discomfort.
5. One action cannot determine a person's intelligence, support needs or future.
