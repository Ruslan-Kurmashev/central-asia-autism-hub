When parents have questions about their child's development, they may encounter several terms: monitoring, screening, testing, assessment, diagnosis and a diagnostic report. These terms are related, but they describe different stages.

Screening helps determine whether a more detailed assessment may be needed. A diagnostic assessment brings together and compares information about a child's development. The diagnostic conclusion is based on the evidence as a whole and established criteria, not on a single behaviour, questionnaire or test.

## Four different concepts

### Developmental monitoring

Monitoring helps people notice how a child's skills change over time: how they communicate, play, learn, move and participate in daily life. Parents, other adults close to the child and professionals can all observe development.

Observation is not a test and does not itself produce a diagnosis. It helps people formulate specific questions and recognise changes worth discussing.

### Screening

Screening is a relatively brief, standardised check. It may involve questions to parents, a questionnaire or another structured tool.

Its purpose is not to diagnose but to identify whether a more detailed assessment is indicated. Screening may concern general development or increased likelihood of a particular condition, including autism.

### Diagnostic assessment

A diagnostic assessment is a more detailed process. It helps build an overall picture of development, consider possible explanations for observed characteristics and determine whether the available information meets diagnostic criteria.

### Diagnostic conclusion

A conclusion follows a review of information from available sources. Diagnosing autism involves looking not at one score or observation but at a combination of characteristics, when they appeared, how they present in different situations and how they affect daily life.

## What does a screening result mean?

Each standardised instrument has its own rules for administration, scoring and interpretation. Its result cannot be understood properly without considering the purpose of the tool, the child's age and how it was used.

A result above a defined threshold may warrant a discussion or a more detailed assessment. It does not mean autism has already been diagnosed. NICE notes that a positive result on a tool identifying an increased likelihood of autism can also have other explanations.

A result below the threshold does not establish the absence of autism either. If developmental concerns persist, the observations themselves need discussion, rather than relying solely on the total score.

In short:

- a positive screening result is not a diagnosis
- a negative result does not guarantee that autism is absent
- the result helps guide decisions about further assessment.

## Why an online test cannot diagnose autism

Whether a questionnaire is online does not by itself determine its quality. Some validated questionnaires can be completed electronically. Other websites promise a "percentage of autism" or a definitive diagnosis after just a few questions. Such claims exceed what screening can do.

Before using a questionnaire, consider:

- who developed the instrument
- which ages and situations it is intended for
- what it actually assesses
- whether it is a screening or diagnostic instrument
- how its results should be interpreted
- what next step is recommended after the result.

For example, its developers describe the official M-CHAT-R/F as a two-stage parent-report screening tool for assessing likelihood of autism in children of specified ages. It has its own procedures and decision rules, but it is not a standalone diagnosis.

## What may be included in a diagnostic assessment?

The specific process depends on the child's age, the clinical situation and the healthcare system. It may include:

- discussing the family's questions and observations
- reviewing early developmental history and emerging skills
- observing communication, interaction, play and behaviour
- reviewing medical history
- information from preschool, school or other settings, when available and relevant
- considering other possible explanations for observed characteristics
- assessing the child's strengths, skills and needs
- using standardised tools when appropriate.

A child's behaviour in a consultation can differ from what happens at home or in an educational setting. NICE recommends gathering further information or, when necessary, observing the child in other settings if substantial differences emerge.

Parents' observations are an important part of assessment. Specific examples help: what happened, in which setting, how often it occurs and what helps the child.

## Why one sign or one instrument is insufficient

There is no single characteristic that automatically confirms or rules out autism. For example, good eye contact, smiling, attachment to family members, play or timely speech development do not by themselves rule out autism. Equally, delayed speech or not responding to one's name does not by itself confirm it.

Professionals may use tools to collect information or observe behaviour systematically. However, NICE recommends not using any autism-specific diagnostic assessment instrument as the sole basis for diagnosis. Information from all available sources must be considered alongside professional clinical judgement.

## Can a blood test confirm autism?

No single blood test or other medical investigation can independently establish an autism diagnosis. CDC states that diagnosis is based on developmental history and behaviour.

This does not mean that additional medical investigations are never needed. They may answer other clinical questions. NICE does not recommend routinely performing medical tests on every child as part of autism diagnosis. In particular circumstances, genetic testing may be considered when certain clinical signs are present, or electroencephalography (EEG) may be considered when epilepsy is suspected. A qualified healthcare professional makes these decisions in the context of the individual child.

## What outcomes might an assessment have?

The result is not always a simple "yes" or "no".

### Diagnostic criteria are met

The family is given an explanation of the findings and the basis for them. The child's strengths and support needs should also be discussed.

### Diagnostic criteria are not met

This does not mean parental concerns were unreasonable or that support is unnecessary. Assessment may identify other characteristics or needs requiring attention.

### Uncertainty remains

Sometimes there is not enough information to reach a confident conclusion. Further observation or additional information may be needed. NICE acknowledges that diagnostic uncertainty can remain in some cases.

Whatever the outcome, the family can ask for an explanation and a written report. NICE guidance provides for a written report describing assessment findings and the reasons for conclusions.

## Support does not depend on diagnosis alone

Diagnosis and need for support are not the same thing. A child may still need support with communication, learning, everyday skills, sensory environments, physical health or emotional wellbeing even if autism diagnostic criteria are not fully met.

NICE recommends considering the child's individual profile and appropriate support based on their needs.

## What to remember

Monitoring helps identify differences and changes in development. Screening helps decide whether more detailed assessment is needed. A diagnostic assessment gathers information from several sources, and the conclusion is based on the full picture and established criteria.

No single characteristic, questionnaire or diagnostic tool independently confirms or excludes autism. General information can help families prepare for a discussion but cannot replace assessment of an individual child by a qualified professional.
