[Development, interaction and play](/kz/en/parents/development-interaction-play/)

A child is enjoying toy cars and wants the adult to continue playing. They may offer a car, gesture, say "more" or choose a message on an augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) device. The adult notices the child's initiative and responds. If the family or specialist has agreed on a goal such as communicating requests or participating in shared interaction, this situation may become a learning opportunity.

Naturalistic Developmental Behavioral Interventions (NDBI) are built around this general principle. They combine knowledge of child development with behavioural learning principles, with skills practised through play and everyday interaction. In their conceptual paper, [Schreibman and colleagues (2015)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25737021/) described shared characteristics including natural settings, child and adult influence over the interaction, natural consequences and developmentally appropriate goals.

NDBI is not one named therapy. Programmes differ in goals, procedures, intensity and staff training. Support should be selected for a particular child's needs, not because a label is popular.

## How learning works in NDBI

"Naturalistic" means trying to embed learning within familiar activities rather than limiting it to separate exercises. Developmental principles guide the choice of goals that fit the child's abilities. Behavioural learning principles include clearly defined targets, suitable support, repeated opportunities for the skill and observation of outcomes.

For example, an adult joins railway play. The child chooses a carriage, invites the adult to continue or signals a wish to stop. The adult may model an accessible request, offer a choice and respond to the child's communication. It is important to distinguish what the child does independently from what still requires prompting.

This does not mean turning every game into a lesson. Free play, rest, individual interests and interaction without learning demands have value in their own right.

## Skills NDBI may support

Studies of NDBI investigate communication and language, joint attention, play, social engagement, imitation, cognition and some everyday abilities. The fact that these domains have been studied does not mean every programme improves all of them.

A functional goal should describe an action meaningful to the child. Rather than "improve social skills", a specific goal might be "during a familiar game, the child can independently ask to continue or request a break using an accessible form of communication".

Communication may involve speech, gestures, pictures or AAC. Eye contact or one predefined "correct" answer should not be required for participation.

## How NDBI differs from Naturalistic Intervention

[Learning in everyday situations (Naturalistic Intervention)](/kz/en/parents/naturalistic-intervention/) describes targeted learning embedded in ordinary contexts. NDBI combines naturalistic methods with more explicitly defined developmental and behavioural learning principles within particular programmes.

These categories overlap, but they are not synonyms. Examples commonly classified as NDBI include the Early Start Denver Model (ESDM), Pivotal Response Treatment (PRT) and JASPER. Their protocols and goals differ.

If a centre advertises NDBI, ask which programme is used, what it targets, how staff are trained and how progress is assessed. The name of the intervention class does not demonstrate service quality.

## What is known about effectiveness

Meta-analytic findings depend on the programme, participant age, target skill, intervention setting and study quality. It matters not just whether average results are positive, but how they were obtained.

### Tiede and Walton meta-analysis (2019)

The authors analysed 27 group-design studies of NDBI. Average effects differed across outcomes and were reported using Hedges' g:

- expressive language: 0.32
- play skills: 0.23
- social engagement: 0.65
- overall cognitive development: 0.48
- receptive language: 0.28
- joint attention: 0.14
- measures of autism characteristics on the scales used: -0.38.

Findings for receptive language and joint attention were borderline, and the authors detected signs of publication and reporting bias in language outcomes. The negative g for autism characteristics reflects the direction of average scale scores used by the researchers, not the "disappearance" of autism. An effect size is neither the proportion of children who benefited nor a prediction for an individual child.

[Tiede and Walton meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31018655/).

### What Project AIM found

Crank and colleagues (2021) examined 27 NDBI studies with 454 separate outcomes. Average effects were larger for skills directly targeted by the intervention and outcomes assessed in similar contexts. Evidence was less convincing for distal and generalised outcomes, with methodological limitations including outcome-measurement bias.

The Project AIM update by Sandbank and colleagues (2023) included 252 studies of non-pharmacological early autism interventions involving 13,304 children. These figures cover multiple approaches, not NDBI alone. When randomised NDBI studies were analysed, average positive results appeared for some measures of adaptive behaviour, language, play and social communication. After additionally excluding outcomes assessed by researchers who knew the child's assigned intervention group, only one pooled effect remained statistically significant: measures of diagnostic autism characteristics (g = 0.30, 95% confidence interval 0.03 to 0.57). For some social-communication outcomes, sufficiently reliable pooled estimates were not available under this restriction. The authors suggested the remaining effect probably reflected social communication in particular. However, scores on diagnostic scales are not the same as quality of life, independence or the level of support a child needs. Adverse effects were inadequately monitored in many studies.

A positive outcome in a session therefore does not establish that the child will use the same skill at home or with other people. And a lack of reported adverse effects does not establish that every intervention is safe.

[Project AIM 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33480474/) · [Project AIM update 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37963634/).

### What later reviews add

The umbrella review by Song, Reilly and Reichow, published in a 2025 journal issue, found positive results for some communication, cognitive and adaptive outcomes. Findings for other outcomes, including autism characteristics and repetitive behaviour, were mixed.

The review by Han and colleagues, published online in 2025 and in a 2026 issue, combined 16 randomised trials and nine qualitative studies. It covered both early intensive behavioural intervention (EIBI) and NDBI, so its pooled effects cannot be attributed to NDBI alone. The authors reported significant limitations in evidence quality.

[Song and colleagues' review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38170431/) · [Han and colleagues' review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41454358/).

## Parent involvement and family workload

Some programmes teach parents responsive ways to interact with their child. A systematic review by Song and colleagues, appearing in a 2026 issue, included eight randomised studies of parent-mediated NDBI involving children aged from birth to six years. The authors described possible benefits for social communication, language and parent-child interaction, while noting differences in methodological quality.

Parent involvement does not mean providing therapy throughout the day. Learning should fit family life, respect adults' capacity and leave room for rest and ordinary interaction. A programme demanding constant monitoring of every action may need adjustment.

[Review of parent-mediated NDBI](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40120018/).

## NDBI and augmentative and alternative communication (AAC)

AAC can be part of play-based learning. Children may use cards, communication devices, gestures or other accessible tools to ask for help, choose, interact or refuse.

Pope, Light and Laubscher reviewed 29 studies of NDBI with autistic children who had minimal spoken language: 17 single-case and 12 group studies. Combining NDBI with aided AAC was associated with positive language outcomes. However, only three studies directly compared conditions with and without AAC, leaving the additional effect of AAC uncertain.

Do not delay AAC access while waiting for spoken language or remove useful communication tools merely to reduce the apparent number of prompts.

[Pope and colleagues' review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38848009/) · [More about AAC](/kz/en/parents/aac-communication-guide/).

## Safety, participation and respect for the child's choices

The following principles relate to respectful, individualised support. They should not be presented as separate effects proven in the NDBI meta-analyses, or as though each rule has been tested independently in a randomised trial. [NICE CG170](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations) recommends adapting play-based social communication support to the child's developmental level and communication needs. These are UK recommendations, not Kazakhstan regulations.

Support should not be reduced to requiring constant eye contact, suppressing safe repetitive movements, unconditional obedience or playing only as adults expect.

Respecting a child's choices and boundaries means:

- the child can communicate agreement, refusal or a need for a break accessibly
- their interests inform the setting, while favourite objects are not withheld as a reward they must "earn"
- essential assistance and AAC are available regardless of task performance
- prompts do not substitute for the child's own initiative
- free play remains available without additional demands.

The term NDBI itself does not specify a required number of intervention hours. Intensity should be discussed with regard to goals, the child's response, observed outcomes and family demands. More hours do not automatically mean better support.

## Assessing outcomes in everyday life

A sign of progress may be the child's independent request to continue play, ask for help or indicate a wish to stop. Adults need to distinguish:

1. an action initiated by the child
2. an action after prompting
3. use of a skill with a familiar professional
4. use at home, at preschool or with other people
5. maintenance of the skill over time.

One successful episode does not establish durable generalisation. Observation notes are for discussing progress with the team, not constantly grading the child's behaviour.

Reconsider the plan if the child regularly experiences distress, refusal is ignored, AAC access is restricted, skills do not transfer beyond sessions or the workload becomes excessive. Do not blame a lack of progress on the child not trying hard enough.

## Choosing a professional or programme in Kazakhstan

A programme may be advertised as NDBI, ESDM, PRT or "developmental behavioural intervention". Neither a programme label nor a short-course certificate alone establishes professional competence.

Discuss six questions before starting:

1. What specific programme is used, and what training has the professional received?
2. Which meaningful goal was chosen for the child, and why?
3. How are the child's initiative, refusal, interests and communication methods respected?
4. How will independent skill use and generalisation to everyday life be measured?
5. What workload is expected for the child and family, and can it be adjusted?
6. Which studies concern this particular programme, and when will the plan be reviewed?

Check the person's core qualification, professional role, additional training and supervision where relevant. International guidance such as [NICE CG170](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations) helps explain social-communication support principles but does not establish licensing or qualification requirements for Kazakhstan.

## Key takeaways

NDBI combines developmental and behavioural strategies embedded in a child's everyday activities. Research suggests benefits in some skills, but outcomes cannot be guaranteed or assumed to be identical across children and programmes.

When selecting support, focus on what the child gains the ability to do independently, whether it matters in daily life and whether their interests and boundaries remain respected.
