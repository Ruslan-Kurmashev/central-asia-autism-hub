A child enjoys toy cars and wants an adult to continue playing. The child may hand over a car, gesture, say “more”, or select a message on an augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) device. The adult notices and responds. If they have agreed on a goal such as developing ways to request something or participate in shared interaction, this everyday exchange can become an opportunity for learning.

Naturalistic Developmental Behavioral Interventions (NDBI) are built around this idea. They are a group of approaches that combine developmental knowledge with behavioural learning principles and support new skills through play and everyday interaction. In their conceptual paper, [Schreibman and colleagues (2015)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25737021/) described shared NDBI characteristics including natural settings, both child and adult influencing the interaction, natural consequences of actions, and developmentally appropriate goals.

NDBI is not the name of one therapy. Programmes differ in their aims, procedures, intensity and practitioner training. Support should be chosen for the needs of the particular child, not because a programme has a popular label.

## How learning works in NDBI

“Naturalistic” means that learning opportunities are built into familiar activities rather than confined to separate exercises. The developmental component informs goals that match the child's current abilities. The behavioural component involves defining a goal, providing suitable support, creating repeated opportunities to use a skill, and observing outcomes.

For example, an adult joins a game with a toy train. The child chooses a carriage, suggests continuing or communicates that they want to stop. The adult may model an accessible request, offer a choice and respond to the child's message. It matters whether an action is initiated independently or still requires a prompt.

This does not mean that every game should be turned into a lesson. Free play, rest, independent interests and communication without an instructional target have value in their own right.

## Skills that may be supported

NDBI research examines communication and language, joint attention, play, social engagement, imitation, cognition and some everyday skills. This list of investigated outcomes does not mean that every NDBI programme improves all of them.

A practical goal should identify an action meaningful to the child. Rather than simply saying “improve social skills”, a goal might be “during a familiar game, the child can independently suggest continuing or request a break using an accessible method”.

Speech, gestures, pictures or AAC can all serve as communication. Participating in play should not depend on maintaining eye contact or reproducing a single “correct” response.

## How NDBI differs from Naturalistic Intervention

[Learning in everyday situations (Naturalistic Intervention)](/kz/en/parents/learning-in-everyday-situations/) describes purposeful teaching in ordinary contexts. NDBI combines naturalistic methods with more explicitly defined developmental and behavioural learning principles in particular programmes.

These concepts overlap but are not exact synonyms. Well-known examples of NDBI include the Early Start Denver Model (ESDM), Pivotal Response Treatment (PRT) and JASPER. They differ in their protocols and goals.

If a centre says it offers NDBI, ask which programme is used, its aims, the practitioner's training and how progress is assessed. The umbrella term does not itself demonstrate service quality.

## What is known about effectiveness

Meta-analysis findings vary according to the programme, participant age, skill measured, intervention setting and study quality. Positive averages must therefore be considered alongside how those estimates were obtained.

### Tiede and Walton's meta-analysis (2019)

The authors combined 27 group-design studies of NDBI. The average Hedges' g effect sizes differed by outcome:

- expressive language: 0.32
- play skills: 0.23
- social engagement: 0.65
- overall cognitive development: 0.48
- receptive language: 0.28
- joint attention: 0.14
- scores measuring autistic characteristics on the instruments used: -0.38.

Findings for receptive language and joint attention were borderline, while the authors detected indications of publication and reporting bias in language outcomes. The negative g for measures of autistic characteristics reflects a change in the direction defined by the researchers, not the “disappearance” of autism. Hedges' g is not the percentage of children helped and does not predict an individual child's outcome.

[Tiede and Walton's meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31018655/).

### Findings from Project AIM

Crank and colleagues (2021) analysed 27 NDBI studies and 454 individual outcomes. On average, effects were greater for skills directly targeted by the intervention and for assessments made in contexts resembling the intervention. Evidence for more distal and generalised outcomes was less convincing. Methodological concerns included measurement bias.

The Project AIM update by Sandbank and colleagues (2023) covered 252 studies of non-pharmacological interventions involving 13,304 children. This was the full evidence set across different approaches, not exclusively NDBI. In randomised NDBI studies, average positive effects were found for some measures of adaptive behaviour, language, play and social communication. After further excluding assessments conducted by people who knew which intervention group a child belonged to, only one pooled effect remained statistically significant: scores on measures of diagnostic autism characteristics (g = 0.30, 95% confidence interval 0.03 to 0.57). Under those restrictions, sufficient robust pooled estimates were unavailable for some separate social-communication measures. The authors suggested that the remaining effect was probably largely related to social communication. Scores on diagnostic instruments, however, are not equivalent to quality of life, independence or level of support needs. Many studies did not adequately monitor adverse effects.

A positive result during an intervention session therefore does not prove that a child will use the skill at home or with other people. Likewise, a lack of reported harms does not mean that every version of an intervention is safe.

[Project AIM 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33480474/) · [Project AIM update 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37963634/).

### Findings from later reviews

An overview of meta-analyses by Song, Reilly and Reichow, appearing in a 2025 journal issue, identified positive results in some areas of communication, cognition and adaptive skills. Results for other outcomes, including scores for autistic characteristics and restricted or repetitive behaviour, were mixed.

The review by Han and colleagues (published online in 2025 and in a 2026 journal issue) included 16 randomised controlled studies and nine qualitative studies. It combined early intensive behavioural interventions with NDBI. Pooled effects cannot therefore be attributed solely to NDBI. The authors highlighted important limitations in the quality of evidence.

[Song and colleagues' overview](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38170431/) · [Han and colleagues' review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41454358/).

## Parent involvement and family burden

Some programmes teach parents ways of interacting responsively with their child. A systematic review by Song and colleagues, published in a 2026 journal issue, covered eight randomised studies of parent-mediated NDBI involving children from birth to age six. The authors reported possible benefits for social communication, language and parent-child interaction, while noting variation in methodological quality.

Parent participation does not mean providing intervention all day. Training should fit family life, take account of adults' capacity and leave room for rest and ordinary interaction. A programme that requires constant monitoring of everything a child does may impose a burden that needs to be reconsidered.

[Review of parent-mediated NDBI](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40120018/).

## NDBI and augmentative and alternative communication (AAC)

AAC can be included in play-based learning. A child can use picture cards, a communication device, gestures or other accessible methods to ask for help, make a choice, take part in communication or refuse.

A review by Pope, Light and Laubscher covered 29 NDBI studies involving autistic children with minimal spoken language: 17 single-case design studies and 12 group studies. Combining NDBI with aided AAC was associated with positive language outcomes, but only three studies directly compared conditions with and without AAC. The exact additional contribution of AAC is therefore uncertain.

Access to AAC should not be postponed until speech emerges. Nor should a useful communication aid be removed to achieve a formal reduction in prompting.

[Pope and colleagues' review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38848009/) · [More about AAC](/kz/en/parents/aac-communication-guide/).

## Safety, child participation and respect for choice

The following are principles of respectful, individualised support. They must not be represented as findings of NDBI meta-analyses or as if each had separately been tested in a randomised trial. [NICE CG170](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations) recommends tailoring play-based social-communication support to a child's developmental level and considering their communication methods, needs and relationships with carers. These are UK recommendations, not Kazakhstan's regulatory requirements.

Support should not centre on requiring constant eye contact, suppressing safe repetitive movements, unquestioningly obeying instructions or playing only in ways adults expect.

In practice, respect for the child's choices and boundaries means that:

- the child can express agreement, refusal and requests for breaks in an accessible way
- the child's interests help identify learning situations, but favourite items are not withheld to make the child “earn” them
- necessary support and AAC remain available regardless of task performance
- prompts do not replace the child's own initiative
- free play remains possible without additional demands.

The NDBI label does not specify a required number of hours. Intensity should be discussed in relation to goals, the child's response, actual outcomes and family burden. More hours do not automatically mean better support.

## Checking outcomes in daily life

A sign of progress might be that a child independently asks to continue a game, requests help or says they wish to stop. Adults should distinguish:

1. an action initiated independently
2. an action following a prompt
3. using the skill with a familiar practitioner
4. using the skill at home, in preschool and with different people
5. retaining the skill after some time.

One successful episode does not demonstrate lasting generalisation. Observation notes can support discussions with the team without making every action by the child a continual assessment.

Reconsider the plan if the child repeatedly experiences significant distress, refusal is ignored, AAC is restricted, skills do not extend beyond sessions or the family is given an unmanageable workload. Lack of progress should not be attributed to insufficient effort by the child.

## Choosing a specialist or programme in Kazakhstan

A service might use the terms NDBI, ESDM, PRT or “developmental behavioural intervention”. The label or a short-course certificate alone cannot establish a practitioner's competence.

Before starting, discuss six questions:

1. What is the specific programme, and what training has the practitioner completed?
2. What goal meaningful to the child has been selected, and why?
3. How are the child's initiative, refusal, interests and communication methods respected?
4. How are independent skills and their generalisation to everyday life measured?
5. What burden is expected of the child and family, and can it be adjusted?
6. Which studies apply to this particular programme, and when will the plan be reviewed?

Check the practitioner's core education, professional role, additional training and supervision, where relevant, separately. International guidance, including [NICE CG170](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations), can inform general principles of social-communication support but does not establish licensing or qualification requirements for services in Kazakhstan.

## Key points to remember

NDBI combines developmental and behavioural strategies within a child's familiar activities. Research suggests potential benefits for several skills, but cannot guarantee the same outcomes for every child or programme.

When choosing support, the central questions are what new independent opportunity the child gains, whether it helps in everyday life, and whether the child's interests and boundaries are respected.
