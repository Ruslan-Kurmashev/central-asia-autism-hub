Parents of autistic children often face dozens of offers: speech and communication sessions, sensory programmes, device-based procedures and proprietary methods. Almost all are advertised as effective.

But how can you tell what might actually help a child and what rests mainly on promises?

You do not need to be a doctor or scientist. It helps to know what to look for, where to find reliable information and which questions to ask before starting.

## 1. What does an "evidence-based method" mean?

When someone says that research supports a method, ask what the studies actually found. The existence of a published paper alone does not establish benefit.

Imagine ten children communicate better after a course of sessions. This does not prove the sessions caused that improvement. During the same period, the children may have developed naturally, received other support or learnt new skills at home.

Researchers therefore compare groups, check whether other studies replicate improvements and examine whether the changes matter in everyday life.

No method should be described as effective "for autism as a whole". Evidence may support an approach for one skill or difficulty without supporting it for every developmental characteristic.

## 2. Five questions to ask before beginning sessions

### Question 1. What specific difficulty does the method address?

Claims such as "activates the brain", "restores neural connections" or "eliminates autism" sound convincing but do not explain what will change for the child.

A useful goal is clear and observable.

For example, a child may ask for help more independently, learn to use communication pictures or find certain everyday situations easier to manage.

Ask the professional: "What exactly are we trying to improve, and how will we know when this happens?"

If the goal cannot be explained simply, it will be difficult to assess the programme's proposed benefit.

### Question 2. Is there scientific research supporting the benefit?

Ask to see the studies or clinical guidelines on which the programme is based.

Consider what kind of evidence is being shown.

Parents' testimonials, videos of successful sessions and "before and after" photographs can describe personal experience, but cannot establish that a method helps most children.

Even a published scientific paper is not automatically reliable. It may have included only a few children, lacked a comparison group or been conducted solely by the method's developers.

Well-conducted systematic reviews, which bring together and evaluate multiple studies, generally provide a more dependable overall picture.

You do not have to read every page of a scientific paper. Ask the professional to explain three things: who was studied, which improvements were found and how confident the researchers are in the findings.

### Question 3. Is this method suitable for my child's goals and needs?

Even a well-studied approach will not be equally useful to everyone.

For example, research on communication support for preschoolers cannot automatically be applied to teenagers or presented as evidence that sleep will improve.

Age, communication methods, co-occurring difficulties, personal interests and family priorities all matter.

Ask whether the approach has been studied for similar needs and how it will be adapted for the individual child.

Pay particular attention to whether the professional respects the child's comfort, their ability to express assent or disagreement in an accessible way and their need for breaks.

### Question 4. What are the risks and limitations?

An approach can carry risks even when it involves no medicines or medical procedures.

Sessions may cause substantial stress, exhaustion or physical discomfort. They may also take up so much time that the child has little opportunity to rest or play.

Medicines, restrictive diets, medical devices and procedures require separate assessment of safety and clinical indications.

Ask about known adverse effects, how the child's wellbeing is monitored and what would happen if their health worsened.

Cost matters too. An expensive programme with little convincing evidence can consume resources that might otherwise provide more suitable support.

### Question 5. How will we measure outcomes?

Agree on specific goals and how to assess changes before sessions begin.

Suppose a child finds it difficult to ask for a break. The professional and parents can first record how the child currently communicates this need and then observe whether a clearer, more independent way of communicating emerges.

Evaluate not only task performance during a session but also whether the child can use the new skill at home, in preschool or with other people.

Progress is not always fast or consistent. Nevertheless, the professional should explain what is being assessed, which changes are emerging and when the plan should be reviewed.

## 3. How to check information about a method yourself

Imagine a centre offers a programme and says: "Its effectiveness is scientifically proven."

Start with the method's precise name. Check whether the research concerns that actual programme, rather than a broad field whose name is used in marketing.

Then consult trustworthy sources.

**[NICE, the UK organisation producing clinical guidance](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations).** Its guidance on autism in children reviews support approaches and interventions not recommended for certain purposes.

**[Cochrane, an international evidence-review organisation](https://www.cochrane.org/).** You can search for reviews of benefits, limitations and potential harms.

**[The Australian guideline for supporting autistic children](https://www.autismcrc.com.au/best-practice/supporting-children/guideline).** It explains how to choose support considering research, individual needs, safety and family preferences. Registration may be needed to access some detailed recommendations.

Not finding the method's name does not prove that it is useless. Equally, claims that a method is "proven" should not be accepted without appropriate research.

If you receive a link to a scientific publication, check its title and conclusions. In particular, verify that researchers actually studied the outcome being promised.

## 4. Which promises should raise concern?

Some statements need especially careful scrutiny:

- "We will completely cure autism" or "remove its cause".
- "Our method helps absolutely every child."
- "100% effective, results guaranteed."
- "You must pay for the full course immediately or you will permanently miss your chance to help your child."
- "We don't need research, parent testimonials are enough."
- "Stop seeing other professionals and abandon other treatment, our programme replaces everything."

Treat terms such as "certified", "patented" or "licensed" with care. Such documents may establish a particular legal status, professional qualification or training history. They do not, by themselves, prove a method is effective.

A professional's certificate and evidence that a particular programme benefits children are two different issues.

## 5. How can you tell whether sessions are helping?

Research evidence about a method and progress in an individual child are related, but they are not identical.

Studies help estimate whether an approach is likely to be beneficial. Observing a child helps establish whether the agreed support goals are being met.

For example, before sessions begin, a child rarely communicates that they need help independently. After some time, parents and the professional notice that the child more often uses a gesture, word or picture in everyday settings.

This is a useful observation, but it does not prove that sessions alone caused the improvement.

For more consistent monitoring, make brief notes of:

- the agreed goal
- what the child could do before the programme
- changes at home and in sessions
- how the child feels during support
- whether to continue as planned or review the approach.

Complex spreadsheets are not necessary. Short notes once a week may be sufficient.

If sessions cause substantial distress or do not bring the expected benefit, discuss changing the approach with the professional. Do not stop prescribed medical treatment independently.

## 6. What if research is insufficient?

Lack of convincing evidence does not always mean a method definitely does not work.

Different situations are possible:

- **Insufficient data.** The method has barely been studied, or studies are too unreliable for firm conclusions.
- **Mixed findings.** Some studies report improvements while others do not confirm them.
- **No convincing expected benefit found.** The method has been studied, but the claimed effect has not been demonstrated convincingly.

These are not interchangeable conclusions.

When evidence is limited, give particular weight to safety, the child's wishes, costs, demands and the availability of better-studied alternatives.

For simple, safe adjustments or environmental changes, the child's comfort and preferences can matter greatly. Medical procedures, risky interventions and expensive programmes require a much stronger justification.

## Before making a decision

The essential questions are what goal the programme pursues, what supports its claimed benefit, which risks are involved and how the outcome will be assessed.

A professional should be willing to discuss limitations, take the child's needs into account and review a support plan that is not delivering expected benefits.

You do not have to agree immediately. You can request the research, discuss other options and decide without pressure.
