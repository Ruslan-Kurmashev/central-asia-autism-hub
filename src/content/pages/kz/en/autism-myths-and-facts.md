## Why are there so many myths about autism?

Autism presents differently from person to person. Short descriptions, social-media stories and portrayals in films can therefore create misleading expectations. Below, we distinguish popular claims from what research and clinical guidelines actually support. This is not a diagnostic checklist: no single characteristic can confirm or rule out autism.

## Myth 1. Autism is caused by poor parenting

**Fact:** autism is related to brain development. Genetic and other biological influences may interact, but a lack of attention, strict parenting or particular family relationships are not established causes of autism (WHO, 2025).

Parents do not need to search for a personal "mistake". A more useful focus is what helps their child communicate, learn and feel safe.

## Myth 2. Vaccines cause autism

**Fact:** high-quality research has not found a causal relationship between vaccination and autism. In December 2025, the World Health Organization reaffirmed this conclusion after reviewing 31 studies published between January 2010 and August 2025.

Developmental differences sometimes become more noticeable around the age when children receive routine vaccinations. A coincidence in timing does not mean one event caused the other. Questions about vaccinations and contraindications should be discussed with a healthcare professional. The autism myth should not be used as a reason to forgo protection against infectious diseases.

## Myth 3. All autistic children are unable to speak

**Fact:** speech and communication abilities vary. Some children use fluent spoken language, while others speak only a little or do not use speech. Communication may include gestures, pictures, writing and augmentative and alternative communication (AAC).

Being able to speak does not, on its own, rule out autism. What matters is not simply the number of spoken words, but whether a person has accessible ways to express thoughts and understand others (WHO, 2025, NICE CG128).

## Myth 4. Autistic people have no feelings and cannot experience empathy

**Fact:** emotions and empathy are not absent in all autistic people. People vary in how they perceive, express and recognise feelings, and others may misinterpret these differences. A qualitative study of the experiences of 76 autistic participants described a wide range of empathic responses rather than one universal deficit (National Autistic Society, 2024). Its findings cannot automatically be generalised to all autistic people.

Do not judge a child's feelings solely from facial expressions, eye contact or familiar styles of communication.

## Myth 5. If a child makes eye contact and smiles, they cannot be autistic

**Fact:** comfortable eye contact, smiling, showing affection and even reaching typical speech milestones do not rule out autism. NICE specifically cautions against making such assumptions. Professionals assess development and interaction as a whole rather than relying on one observation.

The reverse is also true: avoiding eye contact does not prove that someone is autistic.

## Myth 6. Only boys can be autistic

**Fact:** autism is identified in people of different sexes. NICE guidelines specifically note that autism may go unrecognised in girls. Whether characteristics are noticed can depend on an individual's communication style, adaptation to environmental demands and other people's expectations.

This does not mean all autistic girls present in the same way or that there is one universal "female type" of autism.

## Myth 7. All autistic people either have low intelligence or exceptional abilities

**Fact:** intellectual functioning, learning abilities and individual strengths vary widely. Some autistic people have intellectual disabilities, while others do not. Neither extraordinary talent nor lack of ability should be assumed based on an autism diagnosis.

Learning about a person's particular skills and needs is more useful than relying on stereotypes.

## Myth 8. A diet or treatment can eliminate autism

**Fact:** there is no proven universal method to "remove" autism. NICE does not recommend exclusion diets, such as gluten-free or casein-free diets, to address the core characteristics of autism. However, food allergies, coeliac disease and other individual medical conditions require their own assessment and care.

Support can help with communication, daily living skills, learning, mental health and participation in everyday life. Its value should be assessed against specific goals, safety and quality of life, not promises to change someone's identity.

## How can you evaluate a new claim about autism?

Four questions can help:

1. Who is making the claim: a clinical organisation, researchers or someone advertising a service?
2. Is there a link to the original source, rather than just a striking anecdote?
3. Are they presenting one person's experience as a rule for everyone?
4. Are they promising a guaranteed result or a "cure"?

If a message creates fear or pressures you to buy a programme immediately, check it against independent sources first.

## Key takeaway

Autism cannot be reduced to a single behaviour, communication style or prediction about the future. Children benefit from support that responds to their individual characteristics and respects their dignity.
