## What is a meltdown?

**Meltdown** describes an intense response to a situation that has become too difficult to manage. It may involve crying, shouting, vigorous movements or trying to leave the place where distress has built up.

The National Autistic Society describes meltdown as a response to severe overload, not simply “bad behaviour”. However, **not every instance of crying or shouting is a meltdown**. Other possible explanations include pain, tiredness, fear and ordinary frustration.

## What is a shutdown?

**Shutdown** is a term some autistic people and professionals use for a quieter response to overload. A person may stop talking, withdraw from interaction, respond after a long delay, turn away or become noticeably less active.

A lack of visible emotion does not necessarily mean someone feels calm or comfortable. The reverse is also true: **silence alone does not prove that shutdown has occurred**.

Meltdown and shutdown are descriptive terms, not separate diagnoses or substitutes for assessing someone's condition.

## How are these responses different?

| Question | Meltdown | Shutdown |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What may be visible? | Crying, shouting, intense movement or attempting to leave. | Silence, withdrawal, fewer responses or reduced interaction. |
| What may they have in common? | Significant overload and difficulty coping. | Significant overload and difficulty coping. |
| What must not be assumed? | “The child is deliberately having a tantrum to get their way.” | “The child is silent, so they are calm and agree.” |
| What needs consideration? | Safety, circumstances, possible pain and other causes. | Safety, circumstances, possible pain and other causes. |

The same person may respond differently on different occasions. Another person may never experience anything similar. Neither the presence nor appearance of such episodes determines the “severity of autism”.

## What can lead to overload?

Several factors may occur together:

- **Sensory factors:** loud noise, bright light, touch or crowded rooms.
- **Communication barriers:** difficulty asking for a break, expressing pain, refusing or understanding instructions.
- **Changed expectations:** cancellation of a familiar activity, delays or unexpected transitions.
- **Sustained pressure:** constantly trying to meet social expectations.
- **Health and wellbeing:** hunger, tiredness, pain, sleep problems, anxiety or another condition.

NICE recommends considering physical health, communication, anxiety, sensory and social environments and changes in routine when someone experiences significant distress. Attributing everything to autism would be a mistake.

## Meltdown or ordinary frustration: why does context matter?

Imagine a child crying in a shop. This may follow a refusal to buy something, tiredness, loud announcements or pain. **The visible behaviour alone cannot identify a single cause.**

If the child covers their ears and tries to leave a noisy room, consider possible sensory overload. If they suddenly become unresponsive, do not settle on the word “shutdown” without checking their wellbeing.

Labelling the episode too quickly may obscure what the child actually needs.

## What matters during severe distress?

The immediate priorities are **safety and reducing additional demands**. General suggestions described by the National Autistic Society include:

1. Reduce unnecessary noise, bright light and crowds when safe to do so.
2. Avoid asking many questions or giving complex explanations during severe overload.
3. Speak briefly and calmly, allowing enough time for a response.
4. Do not demand eye contact or impose unwanted touch.
5. Make the child's accessible communication methods available, including speech, gestures, pictures or AAC.

These suggestions **are not a personalised crisis protocol**. They do not justify ignoring dangerous symptoms or independently using painful interventions, forceful restraint or punishment.

Where injury is possible, prioritise safety and seek appropriate help. Loss of consciousness, sudden unexplained unresponsiveness, breathing problems, first-time seizure-like episodes or other acute medical symptoms require urgent medical assessment. Do not wait for a supposed “shutdown” to pass if a child is unusually unresponsive or their condition changes abruptly.

## What about after the episode?

Some people need time to recover after overload. Discussing the episode in detail immediately may not be helpful. Later, in a calmer setting, ask for the child's perspective and explore what helped or made things harder.

Accept all accessible forms of communication, including writing, gestures and pictures. Not answering aloud does not prove a lack of understanding.

## What should parents observe?

If similar episodes recur, it may help to record factual observations:

- What happened immediately before the episode?
- Was there unusual noise, waiting, a change of plans or signs of illness?
- How did the child try to communicate?
- Was there a risk of injury?
- What changed when the surroundings became calmer?
- How does the child describe their experience, if they can?

These notes may inform discussions, but **they are not a diagnostic test** and cannot prove a single cause.

## When should professional help be sought?

Discuss the situation with a professional if episodes are frequent, change suddenly, interfere with life, involve self-injury or raise concerns about pain, sleep, anxiety or another health condition.

Assessment may need to consider communication accessibility and the environment as well as behaviour. NICE recommends examining possible medical and social causes, not just outward signs.

## Four common misconceptions

**“If a child is silent, they have calmed down.”** Not necessarily. Silence can accompany overload.

**“A meltdown is a deliberate way to manipulate adults.”** When severely overloaded, a person may lose access to their usual ways of coping.

**“Noise is always the cause.”** No. Pain, anxiety, communication problems and unexpected changes also matter.

**“We must stop the shouting at any cost.”** Quiet behaviour does not guarantee improved wellbeing. Safety and understanding causes come first.
