## Can a medical test determine whether someone is autistic?

Autism is diagnosed from developmental history, clinical observation and diagnostic criteria. There is **no universal blood test, conventional MRI, EEG or genetic test that independently confirms or rules out autism**. The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/diagnosis/index.html) also explains this distinction.

It is useful to separate three questions.

**Clinical autism assessment** asks whether a person's developmental profile meets recognised criteria. **Medical and genetic investigations** can identify co-occurring conditions or help explain particular symptoms. **Scientific research** investigates biological patterns in groups, but an association in a study is not automatically a test for an individual.

We will look at the purposes and limitations of these approaches.

An autism diagnosis cannot be based on a single biological measurement. Autistic people may have different genetic variants or similar-looking conventional MRI images. Conversely, a biological characteristic does not necessarily indicate autism. What matters is whether a test answers a relevant medical question and whether its benefit has been established beyond a research sample.

## How is a clinical autism assessment carried out?

Under [NICE CG128](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg128/chapter/Recommendations), assessment of a child includes developmental history, clinical observation, reports of behaviour at home and in other settings, medical and family history, and consideration of co-occurring conditions.

Professionals consider communication, social interaction, behavioural characteristics, strengths and support needs.

![A conceptual diagram showing developmental history and referral concerns, assessment of communication and behaviour, health context and tests only when indicated, followed by clinical conclusions and support planning.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/research/autism-diagnostic-pathway-evidence-en.svg)

*Figure 1. A simplified outline of the components of clinical assessment. The actual pathway varies according to age, indications and the organisation of healthcare.*

A screening questionnaire may indicate that further assessment is appropriate, but a positive result is not a diagnosis, and a negative result does not necessarily exclude autism. [NICE](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg128/chapter/Recommendations) does not recommend using such instruments alone to confirm or rule out autism.

The history of a characteristic matters as well as whether it is present. A clinical conclusion considers early development, recurring patterns of behaviour and interests, social interaction and alternative possible explanations.

One short appointment may not provide a complete picture. Family accounts and information from different contexts help establish how persistent the characteristics are and how they affect daily living.

Assessment should not be limited to listing difficulties. It should also describe the person's strengths and needs.

## MRI and fMRI: what do brain studies show?

**Structural MRI** shows brain anatomy and may help investigate certain neurological conditions.

Average differences between groups of autistic and non-autistic people often overlap considerably between individuals. They do not create a universal diagnostic sign. The [AAP clinical report](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2019-3447) discusses this limitation.

### When might structural MRI be ordered?

A clinician may consider MRI for specific neurological indications, such as unusual developmental regression, abnormal neurological examination findings, changes in head size or suspected structural disease.

Imaging should address a defined medical question rather than be ordered solely to "check for autism".

Without an indication, MRI can add burdens relating to waiting, sensory discomfort, remaining still and incidental findings requiring further evaluation.

Not being referred for MRI does not, by itself, make an autism assessment incomplete.

### What does functional MRI measure?

**Functional MRI (fMRI)** measures the BOLD signal, indirectly associated with changes in blood flow and oxygenation.

Researchers use it to examine **functional connectivity**, or similarity in the time courses of measured signals between brain regions.

This does not establish a direct anatomical connection or prove the cause of behaviour.

A [2023 *Biological Psychiatry* mega-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2022.12.018) described group-level connectivity differences. A [2019 reproducibility study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31285817/) illustrates the limits of transferring individual findings among samples and research centres.

Even a striking colour-coded brain map is therefore not an individual clinical test. Age, head motion, scanner parameters and analysis methods influence results.

Structural MRI primarily addresses anatomy and certain pathological changes, whereas fMRI investigates signals indirectly related to the activity of brain networks. Neither task is the same as diagnosing a neurodevelopmental condition.

Since scans may reveal incidental abnormalities, findings must always be assessed in clinical context. Investigating young children can also involve practical difficulties. The decision to perform imaging is based on individual clinical indications.

## EEG: when is it needed?

EEG records the brain's electrical activity. It is used for specific neurological questions, especially suspected epileptic seizures.

Group-level EEG findings studied in autism are also affected by age, sleep, co-occurring conditions, medicines and recording methods.

There is **no single EEG pattern that confirms or excludes autism**.

[NICE CG128](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg128/chapter/Recommendations) and the [AAP](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2019-3447) describe possible indications such as suspicious episodic events or certain patterns of unusual regression, including loss of acquired language.

Repetitive movements or a lack of response do not automatically mean epilepsy. A clinician needs to assess the episodes.

Unlike structural MRI, EEG answers questions about electrical activity. A clinician may want to investigate episodes involving reduced awareness of surroundings or unusual involuntary movements. Descriptions of their duration, frequency and accompanying features help guide decisions.

An EEG result does not explain every behaviour. Even when abnormalities are detected, a specialist must determine whether they relate to a suspected neurological condition and whether further investigation is required.

## Blood tests, biomarkers and questionable testing offers

Researchers compare cytokines, metabolites, hormones and other measurements among groups.

A difference in group averages does not make a marker suitable for diagnosing an individual.

Immune and biochemical findings may depend on infections, diet, sleep, medication or co-occurring illness.

The [AAP report](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2019-3447) does not present any such marker as an established universal autism test.

### Why are laboratory tests ordered?

A clinician may investigate anaemia, nutritional deficiencies or other illnesses when symptoms justify it, for example severe dietary restriction or unusual tiredness.

Such results help assess health. They do not measure the "severity" or amount of autism.

### Hair analysis, heavy metals and "toxins"

Commercial proposals to identify the cause of autism through a supposed "toxic burden" require particular caution.

The [AAP](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2019-3447) does not recommend routine broad toxin screening merely because someone is autistic.

Individual tests, such as blood lead testing, can be medically appropriate for specific indications.

Unvalidated tests must not be used to justify self-directed chelation or "detoxification", which can be harmful.

A research biomarker and a clinical diagnostic test are fundamentally different.

To report a research finding, scientists may demonstrate a statistical difference between group means. For clinical use, they need independent validation and evidence of how the test changes decisions.

Medical tests are ordered for specific symptoms, not simply because a person has autism. A severely restricted diet, for example, may raise questions about nutritional deficiencies. Identifying these conditions can be useful, but it cannot establish the presence or degree of autism.

## Genetic testing: CMA, sequencing and FMR1

Different common and rare genetic variants are associated with autism. Some are also present in non-autistic people.

DNA testing may identify a specific genetic condition, but no single variant determines autism in everyone.

### Chromosomal microarray (CMA)

**CMA** can detect some changes in the number of copies of DNA regions, including deletions and duplications.

It does not identify every class of genetic variation.

### Exome and genome sequencing

**Whole-exome sequencing (WES or ES)** primarily examines protein-coding DNA regions.

**Whole-genome sequencing (WGS or GS)** covers a broader range of genetic material.

The methods differ in the kinds of variants they can detect and interpret.

According to [ACMG guidance from 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34211152/), exome or genome sequencing should be considered a first- or second-tier test in certain congenital anomalies, global developmental delay or intellectual disability.

That is not a universal recommendation to sequence every autistic child.

### FMR1 testing

Fragile X syndrome can occur together with autistic characteristics.

Detecting certain repeat expansions in the **FMR1** gene requires a specialised method. Ordinary CMA and exome sequencing do not replace it in all cases.

[AAP guidance from 2025](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/156/1/e2025072219/202230/Genetic-Evaluation-of-the-Child-With-Intellectual) discusses genetic assessment for children with intellectual disability or global developmental delay.

The appropriate test depends on clinical characteristics and family history.

The methods also differ in what they can detect. CMA examines particular copy-number changes. Sequencing reads nucleotide sequences, but not every structural variant, repeat expansion or technically challenging region is necessarily identified reliably.

Thus a negative result from one method does not exclude every possible genetic explanation. Additional methods may be useful for particular indications, but no universal test package is suitable for every family.

Genetic results can also matter for relatives. In some cases, counselling helps explain inheritance and decisions about further testing. Such questions should be discussed with a qualified professional rather than inferred from the name of a gene in a report.

## What do real-world studies show about the yield of genetic tests?

In a [2015 *JAMA* study by Tammimies and colleagues](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2432161), researchers evaluated **258 autistic children**.

CMA identified a molecular diagnosis in **24 of 258 children (9.3%)**.

Exome sequencing was performed only in a subgroup of **95 children**, where it yielded a molecular diagnosis in **8 children (8.4%)**.

The yield depended on additional clinical features. Children with more complex medical and physical findings were more likely to have an identifiable genetic condition.

**This does not mean** that CMA "detects autism in 9.3% of cases" or that exome sequencing "misses everybody else".

The study measured the frequency of an **additional molecular diagnosis**, not the accuracy of detecting autism itself.

These percentages should not automatically be applied to every child in Kazakhstan, because results depend on the sample and criteria for ordering tests.

## How should genetic test results be interpreted?

A **pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant** may help establish a specific molecular diagnosis and guide medical monitoring or genetic counselling.

A **negative result** means the test used did not identify an explanatory change. It does not exclude autism or genetic contributions.

A **variant of uncertain significance (VUS)** cannot currently be confidently linked to disease.

Variant classifications can change as evidence accumulates. [MedlinePlus Genetics](https://medlineplus.gov/genetics/understanding/testing/interpretingresults/) provides an explanation.

A variant in SHANK3, SCN2A, CHD8 or another investigated gene does not by itself establish clinical significance or predict speech, intellectual abilities or future independence.

In some situations a genetic finding can help identify which organs may need additional medical assessment, clarify inheritance patterns, recommend specialist consultations or avoid unnecessary testing.

The meaning and limitations of a particular result should be discussed with a clinical geneticist or similarly qualified professional.

The same category of result may have different practical consequences. A clinically significant variant may clarify a syndrome or guide follow-up. A VUS usually requires cautious interpretation and should not independently determine medical management.

Before testing, ask what the test is intended to find, what its limits are and what an uncertain result would mean.

This is particularly important for exome and genome sequencing, which may identify findings beyond the initial diagnostic question.

## Summary table: what are different investigations for?

| Test or method | When it may be useful | What it cannot establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structural MRI | Some neurological signs or suspected structural disease | That a scan confirms or rules out autism |
| Functional MRI | Research on brain networks and signals indirectly related to activity | That a connectivity measurement is a clinical autism test |
| EEG | Suspected seizures, epilepsy or certain unusual regressions | That one EEG pattern necessarily means autism |
| Blood tests | Symptoms suggesting a particular deficiency, disease or health concern | That an immune or biochemical marker diagnoses autism |
| CMA | Detecting certain DNA copy-number variants | That a negative result rules out genetic contributions or autism |
| Exome or genome | Identifying particular molecular diagnoses when clinically indicated | That every autistic person has one detectable mutation |
| Targeted FMR1 test | Clinical suspicion of a particular genetic condition | That FMR1 testing diagnoses autism |
| Hearing and vision assessment | Investigating co-occurring hearing or visual impairment | That these results replace neurodevelopmental assessment |

## Artificial intelligence and digital diagnostic aids

Research models analyse video, speech, eye movements, questionnaires, EEG or brain imaging.

Strong performance in one dataset does not establish that the system works well in another clinic.

Independent validation, representative age and clinical groups, error analysis and evidence that findings improve actual decisions are essential.

In the United States, the [FDA authorised a specific digital diagnostic aid in 2021](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USFDA/bulletins/2e250cd).

[Canvas Dx documentation from 2025](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf24/K243558.pdf) describes the use of questionnaires, videos and clinical information.

The **EarliPoint** device uses eye tracking within a defined intended use. Authorisation documents cover a [2023 version](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfpmn/pmn.cfm?ID=K230337) and a [version cleared on 5 March 2026](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfpmn/pmn.cfm?ID=K253442).

Regulatory authorisation applies to a specified device, intended population, conditions of use and jurisdiction.

It does not mean that every camera application or algorithm can establish an independent diagnosis. Such tools are considered in the context of professional assessment, not as universal tests based on blood, DNA or MRI.

Digital aids have different purposes. Some collect structured behavioural information, others analyse video or eye-movement signals.

Intended clinical populations, acquisition conditions and interpretation procedures must be specified.

When assessing a new AI system, ask whether it has been tested beyond its training dataset and among people with similar developmental or medical conditions.

**Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value and overall accuracy are different metrics**, not interchangeable descriptions of quality.

## Why can a news headline about "90% accuracy" mislead?

Imagine a hypothetical test with **90% sensitivity and 90% specificity**.

That sounds impressive. But the value of a positive result depends partly on how often the condition occurs among the people receiving the test.

Suppose the condition occurs in **10% of 1,000 people** being assessed.

- Of the 100 people with the condition, about **90** test positive.
- Of the 900 people without the condition, about **90** receive false-positive results.
- About **180** people test positive overall, but only **90** are true positives.

Thus **approximately half of the positive results are false positives** in this example, despite sensitivity and specificity both being 90%.

**These are not results from an autism test.** This is an illustration of test statistics.

Real-world performance depends on the population, how people are selected and the study design.

"90% accuracy" without these details cannot tell a family how useful a result will be.

The illustration with 1,000 people also shows why identical sensitivity and specificity can lead to different meanings of a positive result when the condition is more or less common.

That matters especially for tools proposed outside specialist clinics. It is necessary to understand who the test is for, what other conditions may be present and what happens after a positive result.

## What should families ask before agreeing to investigations?

If a clinic offers "autism diagnosis by laboratory tests", ask what specific medical question each investigation addresses, which symptoms justify it and how the findings would change the next steps.

Also ask about limits of accuracy, alternatives, costs, incidental findings and who will interpret results, especially genetic findings.

A research publication or advertising claim does not demonstrate clinical usefulness on its own.

If anything is unclear, request an explanation from the referring clinician or an independent relevant specialist.

### How can a questionable diagnostic offer be recognised?

Be cautious of promises to diagnose autism from one blood sample, brain map or hair analysis, particularly where the clinic cannot explain the validated reference standard used.

Ask whether diagnostic accuracy has been assessed independently and for which age group the test is intended.

Request written information about indications, expected benefits and limitations.

Before genetic testing, discuss uncertain or incidental findings and possible implications for relatives.

If tests are offered only as part of an expensive package without a clear medical question, a second professional opinion is reasonable.

Declining an unnecessary test is not the same as rejecting medical care. Appropriate investigations may still be justified by specific symptoms, whether or not the person is autistic.

## Must families wait for test results before supporting their child?

Not necessarily.

When communication, sleep, eating or sensory discomfort is difficult, support options can be discussed alongside clinical evaluation.

A qualified professional can help identify practical goals and ways to make communication or the environment more accessible.

Investigating a possible genetic explanation and supporting current needs are separate tasks that need not delay each other without good reason.

A child experiencing specific difficulties should not automatically have to wait for completion of genetic testing before support is considered.

Work on communication, environmental adaptations and co-occurring health concerns can proceed alongside assessment.

That does not mean prescribing medications, procedures or tests independently. Medical interventions require professional evaluation of indications and risks.

## Main conclusions

Clinical autism assessment and investigations for other medical indications have different purposes.

Structural MRI, EEG, laboratory tests and genetic studies may help address particular health questions, but none replaces an assessment of development.

The important issue is not the number of tests ordered. It is whether each test has a reason and whether the family receives a clear plan for next steps.
