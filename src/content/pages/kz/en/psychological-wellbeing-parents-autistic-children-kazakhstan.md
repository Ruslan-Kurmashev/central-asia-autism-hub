## Why is parents' psychological wellbeing a separate research question?

Most information a family receives about autism focuses on the child: communication, education, daily skills and forms of support. Research also examines the circumstances of the adults who support the child.

**Psychological wellbeing** includes emotional health, rest, social connections, a sense of meaning, satisfaction with life and the possibility of seeking help. It does not mean never struggling or always feeling positive. Parents are not a uniform group. Someone can face barriers to services and still enjoy life with their child. Another parent may have family support but struggle with finances or employment.

Our article about [difficulties faced by families in Kazakhstan (Russian)](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/ru/research/trudnosti-semey-detey-s-autizmom-v-kazahstane/) discusses costs and access. Here we focus on emotional wellbeing, ways researchers measure it and the limits of what studies tell us.

## What exactly do researchers measure?

Stress, anxiety, depressive symptoms, wellbeing, social support and resilience are related but distinct.

| Concept | Research meaning | What not to assume |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Stress** | Tension and responses to everyday demands | Stress is not itself a psychiatric diagnosis |
| **Anxiety symptoms** | Worry, bodily anxiety and alertness | Questionnaire scores describe symptoms during a particular period |
| **Depressive symptoms** | Changes in mood, motivation, energy and interest | A positive screening score is not a diagnosis of depression |
| **Wellbeing** | A broad view of health, life, relationships and parenting | Positive and negative experiences may coexist |
| **Social support** | Help and acceptance the person feels are available | Perceived support is not simply the number of friends or benefits |
| **Resilience** | Adapting to challenges with internal and external resources | Parents should not be expected to cope entirely alone |

These distinctions are essential for interpreting the research from Kazakhstan.

## Study 1. Stress, anxiety and depressive symptoms in Kazakhstan

In 2022, **Raushan Alibekova and colleagues**, including researchers at Nazarbayev University, published a study in *Global Mental Health*. Data had been collected in **April-December 2018**. The researchers recruited **146 parents**, mainly in Astana, through an educational event, a parents' organisation and the National Children's Rehabilitation Centre. This was a convenience sample, not a random nationwide sample.

Participants answered voluntarily in Russian or Kazakh. The **DASS-21** questionnaire assessed symptoms, and a separate scale assessed perceived social support. Symptom calculations were available for **136 complete responses**.

### What did the findings show?

![Three bars comparing reported stress, anxiety and depressive symptoms among 136 parents.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/research/kazakhstan-autism-caregiver-mental-health-2018-en.svg)

*Figure 1. AutismHub chart based on Alibekova et al. (2022). Categories cover symptoms from mild to extremely severe, may overlap and do not constitute clinical diagnoses.*

| DASS-21 symptom category | Share of 136 complete responses |
| --- | ---: |
| Stress, from mild to extremely severe | **61.0%** |
| Anxiety, from mild to extremely severe | **52.9%** |
| Depressive symptoms, from mild to extremely severe | **53.7%** |

[Read the original study](https://doi.org/10.1017/gmh.2022.51).

### Why these numbers are not clinical diagnoses

DASS-21 is a standardised self-report instrument. It calculates scores from a person's answers about experiences during a period of time. **A high anxiety score does not mean that a clinician diagnosed an anxiety disorder.** Diagnosis requires a separate assessment.

One respondent could report stress and anxiety simultaneously, so the percentages **cannot be added to reach 100%**. The participants were recruited through services and organisations, so families outside these networks might be underrepresented.

The supported interpretation is that emotional symptoms were common in the group studied and merit attention in services. It would be wrong to conclude that 61% of all parents of autistic children in Kazakhstan have a psychiatric disorder.

## Which circumstances were linked to emotional wellbeing?

The researchers also compared responses with features of family life. These findings are associations, not tests of interventions.

### Perceived social acceptance

Parents who reported insufficient public acceptance of their child's needs tended to report stronger stress, anxiety and depressive symptoms. This can inform respectful services, but **the cross-sectional design cannot prove** that low acceptance caused those symptoms. Other factors or influences in both directions may be involved.

### Family, friends and close relationships

Higher **perceived** social support was statistically associated with fewer emotional symptoms. The perception matters: a large family does not guarantee useful help, while a small network might offer dependable assistance. The study did not assign families to support programmes and cannot estimate how much a specific action would lower anxiety.

### Employment and sharing responsibilities

In some analyses, employed parents reported fewer symptoms than non-employed parents. This **does not demonstrate** that employment treats stress or depression, or that reducing paid work caused distress. Income, caregiving responsibilities, rest and access to services are interconnected.

### Can a particular therapy be blamed for parental stress?

An association between a type of child intervention and parental symptoms appeared in an initial comparison, but did not persist after adjustment for some other factors. The study therefore cannot show that this intervention caused or prevented parental distress. A suitable comparative longitudinal design would be needed.

## Study 2. Wellbeing in 327 parents and the experiences of 10 psychologists

A second study was published online on **28 July 2024** in *Archives of Medical Science* by **Nazgul Akhtayeva, Lyazzat Kosherbayeva, Aziza Imamatdinova and Kastytis Šmigelskas**. Researchers were associated with SDU University, Asfendiyarov Kazakh National Medical University, Al-Farabi Kazakh National University and the Lithuanian University of Health Sciences.

They used two methods:

1. A wellbeing **survey of 327 parents** of autistic children.
2. **Interviews with 10 primary healthcare psychologists** about their professional experience.

The survey describes responses within its sample. Interviews offer detail but cannot measure the prevalence of an opinion across the national workforce.

### Wellbeing cannot be reduced to negative feelings

The mean total wellbeing scale score was **52.6, with a standard deviation of 15.65**. It is not an overall rating of the lives of parents in Kazakhstan. Proper interpretation depends on the instrument's range, validation and sample.

Some **positive** domains, including enjoyment of the parent-child relationship and subjective quality of life, had high mean scores. Difficulties do not make the entire parenting experience negative, and positive feelings do not mean support is unnecessary.

[Read Akhtayeva et al.](https://doi.org/10.5114/aoms/190772).

### What did the psychologists say?

The authors interpreted interviews as showing that some participating psychologists lacked preparation for systematic support of parents of autistic children and would benefit from further training. **Ten interviews are not a national audit** of psychologists' competence.

### Practical implications

Services should consider:

- the parent's own wellbeing and needs
- the family's strengths and positive relationships
- ways to ask for help without stigma
- the availability of trained professionals
- coordination of psychological and family support.

These are considerations for developing support systems, **not the demonstrated efficacy of a new psychological programme**. The study was not a randomised treatment trial.

## What do more recent international studies add?

A June 2026 *PLOS ONE* systematic review by **Md Salim and colleagues** included **13 studies from 2019-2025** on resilience among parents of autistic children. Factors were grouped into socioeconomic circumstances, the child's needs, parental emotional wellbeing, coping strategies and interpersonal relationships.

Many studies were cross-sectional and used different instruments. They show recurring associations but **do not guarantee** that any individual resource will improve mental health.

### What does resilience mean?

Resilience does not mean never feeling tired or having unlimited willpower. It concerns the capacity to adapt with **personal and external resources**, such as clear information, help from others, shared tasks, accessible services and appropriate professional care.

The need for extra help does not imply a personal failure to be resilient. Circumstances affect what coping is possible.

[Read the systematic review](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0351969).

## What forms of support does the World Health Organization describe?

WHO developed **Caregiver Skills Training (CST)** for families of children with developmental delays or disabilities, including autism. It is an educational approach covering everyday communication, problem-solving and caregiver wellbeing. **CST is not a cure for autism**, and it does not substitute for individual psychological or psychiatric care when symptoms are substantial.

### What happened in Kazakhstan?

According to WHO, training of CST trainers began in Kazakhstan with national partners on **15 February 2021**.

That announcement **does not prove** that sessions were available in every city, language or community in October 2026. A training launch is not a nationwide clinical trial demonstrating effectiveness.

[WHO announcement about Kazakhstan, 2021](https://www.who.int/europe/news/item/05-03-2021-who-europe-launches-caregiver-skills-training-in-kazakhstan-to-support-children-with-neurodevelopmental-delays).

### New WHO caregiver wellbeing resources

On **7 May 2026**, WHO released caregiver wellbeing guides designed for **three group sessions**. They can be incorporated into CST or used as a separate educational module. Their existence illustrates a focus on caregivers' needs, but does not prove efficacy for every individual or availability in a particular region of Kazakhstan.

[WHO materials, 2026](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240120983).

## Practical section: how can parents use this information?

These are ways to organise conversations about support, **not a therapeutic protocol**.

### 1. Separate the needs of the child and the adults

Before a consultation, write down two short lists.

**Child's needs:** communication, play, learning, rest, participation, situations that cause discomfort and available accommodations.

**Parents' needs:** sleep and rest, practical help, a clear route through services and their own psychological support.

The purpose is not to pit family members' interests against one another, but to clarify what **the whole family** needs.

### 2. Ask for specific help

"We need support" may be too general. A family member might be able to provide transport to a consultation, help discuss school options or share practical household tasks for a few hours.

Support should fit the actual person's request, not consist of unsolicited advice. **Different families need different forms of help.**

### 3. Prepare questions for a psychologist

Questions may include:

- What experience do you have supporting parents of children with developmental differences?
- How will my goals and preferences be considered?
- How will you assess my emotional wellbeing and any need for further care?
- How is my personal information protected?
- How can we tell whether the meetings are helping?
- When might referral to another professional be appropriate?

**Counselling, psychotherapy, psychiatric evaluation and educational sessions have different purposes.**

### 4. Do not use research percentages for self-diagnosis

A finding that 61% of respondents had elevated stress scores does not describe an individual parent. If emotional or physical symptoms substantially affect sleep, work, relationships or daily activities, discuss them with a qualified healthcare or psychological professional.

**This article and its chart are not tests for depression, anxiety disorders or burnout.**

### 5. Distinguish support from commercial promises

An expensive "parental resilience recovery" programme is not evidence-based simply because its advertising uses medical words or cites stress research. Before paying, examine qualifications, goals, limitations, cost and independent evidence about the specific service.

## What research is still needed on family wellbeing?

The two Kazakhstani studies involved **selected groups of parents**, so broader evidence is needed for national services.

### More diverse participant groups

Researchers should include fathers, grandparents, other caregivers, rural families and parents who do not attend established support centres. Samples dominated by mothers cannot represent every caregiver's needs.

### Longitudinal observation

Single-time-point surveys cannot show how wellbeing changes as children move through preschool, school, adolescence and adulthood. Repeated observation would help.

### Testing specific forms of psychological support

To assess whether an intervention reduces stress, studies need predefined outcomes, before-and-after measures, suitable comparisons and methods to address bias.

### Regional access to professionals

Access research should assess actual waiting time, cost, language availability, confidentiality and family satisfaction, rather than simply count professionals.

### Autistic people's own wellbeing

Parental research cannot substitute for research on the subjective wellbeing of autistic children, adolescents and adults. Their own experiences and communication methods matter.

## What is known and what cannot be claimed?

**Supported:** the two Kazakhstani studies found substantial emotional difficulties among some participants alongside positive aspects of parenting. The authors discussed perceived support and professional training.

**Supported with limitations:** international research links support, living circumstances and coping with resilience, but much of it is observational.

**Not established:** national rates of clinically diagnosed anxiety or depression among parents of autistic children, causal effects of a particular family support action in these surveys or universal efficacy of a psychological programme.

**Conclusion:** research and services should pay attention to parents' **real emotional needs, strengths, resources and social circumstances**, not just the difficulties their children face.
