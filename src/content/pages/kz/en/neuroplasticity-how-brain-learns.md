## Why does the brain change when we learn?

A child tries to fasten their coat for the first time. At first, holding the fabric, positioning the hands and bringing the parts together is difficult. With experience, movements may become more confident. Something that once required help can gradually become familiar.

Learning is not limited to remembering words or solving problems. It also involves movement, communication, recognising sounds, using objects and many other skills.

The nervous system participates in all these processes. As a person develops and gains experience, the functioning of neural networks changes, and sometimes their structure changes too.

This capacity of the nervous system to change is called **neuroplasticity**.

Neuroplasticity is not a single process that can simply be switched on or off. The term encompasses different mechanisms operating in nerve cells, their connections and larger brain networks.

For example, some connections may become more effective at transmitting signals. Others weaken, and certain structures of nerve cells may change.

Not every brain change represents a useful new skill. Plasticity plays a part in ordinary development, adaptation to the environment and some pathological processes.

To understand its connection with learning, first consider changes between nerve cells.

## What happens between neurons during learning?

Neurons communicate through specialised contacts called synapses.

In chemical synapses, the ending of one neuron releases signalling molecules, and another neuron detects them using receptors.

The structure of synapses is explained in greater detail in the article on how neurons and synapses connect.

Here the important point is that a synapse does not necessarily transmit signals with the same efficiency throughout life.

### How do nerve connections become stronger?

Under some experimental conditions, neural activity can produce an increase in the efficiency of signal transmission that lasts for a period of time.

One such process is called **long-term potentiation (LTP)**.

In 1973, [Bliss and Lømo](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4727084/) studied neural signal transmission in a region of the hippocampus in rabbits.

After particular sequences of electrical stimulation, they observed a sustained increase in the recorded neural response.

This study became an important experimental foundation for investigating long-lasting changes in communication between neurons.

It does not show that every repeated action automatically produces LTP or that LTP alone explains all human learning.

Different brain regions have different forms of plasticity, with distinct cellular and molecular mechanisms.

### Why can neural connections become weaker?

Researchers also study **long-term depression of synaptic transmission (LTD)**.

Under certain conditions, the efficiency of signal transmission through a synapse can decrease in a sustained way.

Here the word "depression" refers to reduced physiological transmission, not a person's emotional state.

Synaptic weakening is not necessarily harmful. It can contribute to rearranging neural networks and changing how incoming information is processed.

A [2004 review by Malenka and Bear in *Neuron*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2004.09.012) describes multiple LTP and LTD mechanisms.

The authors emphasised that these are not two universal processes operating identically in all neurons.

The phrase "learning strengthens connections" can be helpful as an introduction, but it does not capture the full biological complexity.

## How do neural networks change?

Changes in the strength of signal transmission are only one aspect of neuroplasticity.

Development and experience can also affect the structure of individual neurons, the number of some connections and interactions between groups of nerve cells.

### Formation and remodelling of contacts

The developing brain forms a very large number of neural connections.

Some remain, some change and others gradually disappear.

The selective removal of synaptic contacts is called **synaptic pruning**.

This is a normal part of nervous system development.

The brain does not, however, remove contacts according to a simple rule of "keep useful connections, discard useless ones". Remodelling depends on age, brain region, neural activity and interactions among different cell types.

For example, [Paolicelli and colleagues (2011) in *Science*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21778362/) reported that microglia engulfed synaptic material and participated in remodelling contacts in the developing mouse brain.

These findings do not mean that microglia control all learning or that pruning is necessarily impaired in autistic children.

The role of brain immune cells is discussed further in the separate microglia article.

### Does the adult brain change?

Yes. The nervous system does not lose all capacity to adapt after childhood.

A well-known example was published by [Draganski and colleagues in *Nature* in 2004](https://doi.org/10.1038/427311a).

The researchers followed adults learning to juggle. Serial MRI scans showed changes in measures of grey matter structure in regions involved in visual motion processing.

Some of these changes later decreased.

The study demonstrated that learning a new skill in adulthood can be accompanied by changes detectable through brain imaging.

MRI, however, could not count newly formed synapses directly or determine the precise cellular mechanism of those changes.

The researchers did not literally observe the formation of particular synapses during training.

## What are sensitive and critical periods of development?

The brain changes throughout life, but its sensitivity to different kinds of experience is not constant.

Certain neural systems are especially responsive to particular experiences during specific stages of development.

These stages are called **sensitive periods**.

Early visual experience, for example, is important in the development of some visual-processing mechanisms.

A [2004 review by Eric Knudsen in the *Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15509387/) explains the distinction between sensitive and critical periods.

During a sensitive period, experience has a particularly strong influence on a neural system.

The term **critical period** is used for more restrictive cases in which certain experience is required within a limited developmental window for a particular function to develop normally. Its absence can lead to lasting consequences.

### Does this mean it is too late to learn after a particular age?

No.

A common myth claims that brain development is completely finished by age three, five or seven.

Different functions actually follow different developmental timelines.

Some visual and other sensory processes do have well-studied periods of heightened sensitivity.

But there is no single age limit for acquiring speech, communication, independence or other complex skills that can be applied to every child.

A [2005 review by Takao Hensch in *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn1787) describes mechanisms of critical periods, studied particularly extensively in visual systems.

Much of the mechanistic evidence comes from animal studies and particular sensory systems.

Such findings cannot be turned into a claim that a child loses the capacity to learn new skills after a certain birthday.

Early access to appropriate support matters because it provides opportunities to recognise a child's needs and create suitable learning experiences sooner.

That does not mean support during school age, adolescence or adulthood is pointless.

## How is neuroplasticity studied in autism?

Some genetic variants associated with autism affect proteins involved in synaptic function, gene regulation and nerve-cell development.

Researchers therefore investigate synaptic plasticity as one possible biological mechanism relevant to certain aspects of neurodevelopment.

In a [large 2020 study by Satterstrom and colleagues in *Cell*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31981491/), genetic data were analysed from **35,584 participants**, including **11,986 autistic participants**.

The authors identified **102 genes** associated with autism at the selected statistical threshold (**FDR <= 0.1**).

This is a group-level genetic finding, not a list of genes from which a particular child's developmental path can be predicted.

Many of these genes contribute to the regulation of other genes or to neuronal communication.

This helps scientists study nervous system development.

It does not prove that all autistic people have the same impairment of neuroplasticity. Different genetic changes can affect different cells and processes.

### What do human studies show?

A [2025 systematic review by Laguna and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40469240/) examined **11 studies** involving a total of **1,943 autistic participants**.

Researchers used different methods, including measurements of biological molecules, genetic studies and techniques investigating brain activity.

Differences were described in some measures, including certain proteins and genes involved in nervous system functioning.

However, results were heterogeneous and the measurement methods differed considerably.

A change in the concentration of a protein in blood is not a direct measurement of the number or strength of synapses in the brain.

These findings therefore do not provide a single clinical test of neuroplasticity in autism.

### Is learning itself different in autistic people?

This is a separate question that cannot be answered by studying cells alone.

Researchers investigate different forms of learning, including the ability to notice repeating patterns in sounds, images and sequences of actions.

This ability is called **statistical learning**.

A [systematic review by Bell and colleagues, published online on 24 December 2025 in *Molecular Autism*, volume 17 (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13229-025-00697-7), considered **37 studies**.

Across many behavioural tasks, the authors found few consistent differences between autistic and non-autistic participants, although reaction times differed in some conditions.

Brain-activity findings were less consistent.

The reviewers also highlighted the underrepresentation of people with substantial language difficulties or intellectual disability and the diversity of study methods.

These findings do not support the simple idea that all autistic people have an impaired ability to learn.

Neither do they mean that every autistic child will acquire every skill at the same speed or in the same way as their peers.

The evidence indicates diverse forms and outcomes of learning, not one identical "autistic brain type".

## Can a medical examination measure a child's neuroplasticity?

Researchers use several techniques to examine the brain.

**Electroencephalography (EEG)** records the combined electrical activity of large populations of nerve cells.

**Functional MRI** examines changes in signals related to blood flow during tasks or at rest.

**Structural MRI** can assess particular aspects of brain structure.

These methods may be useful for research and for specific clinical purposes when indicated.

None directly measures all changes taking place at individual synapses at once.

An ordinary MRI or EEG result cannot reliably determine whether an individual child will learn to speak, read, communicate or perform everyday activities.

There is also no accepted clinical test measuring a "percentage of neuroplasticity" for autism diagnosis.

Commercial claims to "activate neuroplasticity", "restore brain connections" or "restart a critical period" therefore deserve particular caution.

The benefits of an intervention should be demonstrated through good-quality clinical research, not inferred solely from a theory about its effect on neurons.

## What does neuroplasticity mean for parents?

Parents do not need to study the molecular mechanisms of LTP and LTD to help a child develop everyday skills.

What matters in practice is recognising that learning can occur gradually and that the conditions in which a child experiences an activity influence their opportunity to participate.

Imagine a child who has difficulty telling an adult that they want a drink.

The child might use speech, a gesture, a picture card, a communication device or another accessible form of expression.

If adults notice communication attempts and respond to them consistently, the child has opportunities to practise functional communication.

This is an example of accessible learning, not proof that a particular synapse in the child's brain has become stronger.

### Why does one child need more time?

Learning can be influenced by perception, communication, movement, attention, health and environmental conditions.

A child might find it hard to complete a task in a noisy room but understand an adult more easily in a quiet setting.

In another case, noise is not the problem. Perhaps the instruction is too long or assumes a skill the child has not yet acquired.

A lack of rapid progress does not demonstrate that the child's brain is "unable to change".

The first questions should be whether the learning activity is accessible and the task matches the child's present abilities.

### What is useful when teaching skills?

A specific daily-life goal is often a better starting point than the abstract aim to "develop the brain".

A family might, for example, focus on helping a child independently communicate that they need a break.

They can then identify an accessible communication method, situations in which the child already expresses that need and support that helps others understand them.

Progress should be assessed through observable changes: does the child request a break more often without prompting, need less help or use the skill in different settings?

Such changes have practical meaning whether or not brain activity has been measured.

Learning must not rely on coercion, sensory overload or promises to eliminate autism.

The purpose of support is to expand a person's opportunities, accessible communication, independence and wellbeing.

### Why does neuroplasticity not guarantee a result?

It is sometimes claimed that repeating an exercise often enough will necessarily produce the desired brain connections.

That is an oversimplification.

Learning depends on more than repetition. The nature of the task, previous experiences, attention, health, accessible communication and other factors matter.

Neuroplasticity explains why the nervous system can change. It does not, by itself, establish that a particular intervention works or guarantee an outcome for every child.

Support should be chosen according to the person's needs and evidence for the specific approach.

## What is well established and what remains under investigation?

### What is reasonably well established?

The brain can change how neural connections function in response to activity and experience.

Long-term strengthening and weakening of synaptic transmission have been studied in detail in experimental models.

Connections between neurons form and are remodelled during brain development.

Certain neural systems have periods of heightened sensitivity to experience, but learning does not stop after early childhood.

### Which hypotheses are still being investigated?

Researchers examine how different autism-associated genetic variants may influence the development and function of synapses.

They also study whether particular forms of plasticity differ in specific neural systems in some groups of autistic people.

Another area of interest is how differences in perception, attention and information processing interact with learning.

These findings cannot yet be combined into one model that explains autism in every person.

### What cannot currently be concluded?

There is no scientific basis for claiming that neuroplasticity is uniformly decreased or increased in all autistic people.

A blood test, MRI or EEG measure cannot determine an individual child's capacity to learn.

There is no proven universal age after which an autistic person can no longer acquire communication or other skills.

Nor does neuroplasticity alone establish the effectiveness of programmes promising to "reprogramme the brain" or "remove autism".

The central conclusion is that the brain's capacity to change is important for development and learning. But a person's real abilities cannot be inferred from one proposed biological mechanism. Families benefit more from understanding accessible ways for a child to learn, creating suitable conditions and choosing support according to actual needs.
