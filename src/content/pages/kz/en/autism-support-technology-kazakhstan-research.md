## Why discuss support technology separately from autism treatment?

Reports about new developments may show a robot playing with a child, an exercise app, a tablet with picture symbols or software analysing video. These may look like a single category of 'high-tech support', but they address different needs.

**Communication aids** help a child express thoughts. **Assistive technology** can improve access to learning. **Robots and virtual reality** are often used to examine specific play, social or learning activities. **Artificial intelligence** may support an interface or research analysis, but its presence does not itself establish clinical effectiveness.

Attractiveness is not the same as scientifically demonstrated benefit. A child watching a robot closely does not prove that their everyday communication improved. A device that helps a child report pain or decline an activity may solve a genuine problem without changing their autistic characteristics.

This article examines Kazakhstan research and development, international reviews and questions families can ask before choosing digital support.

## Which technologies are being studied, and for what purposes?

![Original conceptual diagram comparing AAC, educational assistive technology, social robots, apps and virtual reality.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/research/kazakhstan-autism-support-technologies-map-en.svg)

*Figure 1. An AutismHub editorial diagram. It does not compare the scientific effectiveness of technologies or recommend buying any particular device.*

| Technology | Main purpose | Why individual evaluation is needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Augmentative and alternative communication (AAC)** | Expressing requests, choices, ideas, refusals and other messages | Whether communication is usable with different people matters |
| **Educational assistive technology (AT)** | Access to tasks, school environments and educational materials | Device availability alone does not ensure classroom participation |
| **Social robots** | Studying interaction, play scenarios, attention and engagement | Interest in a robot is not the same as improved long-term outcomes |
| **Apps, video prompts and VR** | Practising particular situations or learning activities | Completing a task on a screen does not prove the skill transfers to daily life |

Not every autistic child needs all these tools. Sometimes a simple visual instruction or a convenient communication board is more practical than expensive equipment.

## Kazakhstan's 2019 study: designing NAO robot activities

In 2019, **Anara Sandygulova, Zhanel Zhexenova, Bolat Tleubayev and colleagues** from Nazarbayev University and the National Children's Rehabilitation Center published *Interaction design and methodology of robot-assisted therapy for children with severe ASD and ADHD* in *Paladyn*.

The study included **14 children**. Researchers observed activities involving a humanoid NAO robot, collected feedback from families and specialists, and gradually modified exercises and the robot's spoken and physical responses.

### What activities did the researchers examine?

The activities included simple games, movement to music, imitation and interaction with pictures of emotions. The authors considered what participants enjoyed, what caused difficulties and what should be adapted to different needs.

Some robot behaviours had to be revised or removed because of children's reactions and technical constraints. This illustrates why technology development must consider not only appeal but also **usability, safety and the option to stop participating**.

### What the study does not prove

The primary focus was **interaction design**, not comparative clinical effectiveness. A small observational sample cannot establish that a robot improves communication better than a specialist or that any effect persists for months.

The phrase *robot-assisted therapy* in the source title does not mean the new method's efficacy has already been demonstrated.

[First Nazarbayev University study, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1515/pjbr-2019-0027).

## The 2021 study: what longer engagement showed

In June 2021, **Nazerke Rakhymbayeva, Aida Amirova and Anara Sandygulova** published a study of repeated robot sessions in *Frontiers in Robotics and AI*.

It involved **11 autistic children aged 4-11**, seven of whom also had ADHD. Sessions took place at the National Children's Rehabilitation Center. The researchers examined engagement, emotional responses and differences between familiar and unfamiliar activities.

### Main findings

Many children remained interested in the robot across repeated visits. The researchers found greater engagement with **activities familiar to the participant** than with new ones.

However, they did not find a statistically significant overall increase in time spent engaged between the first and last sessions.

**What this may mean:** familiar activities that appeal to a particular child may inform better robot design. **What it does not mean:** proven improvements in communication outside the study, or superiority over conventional support.

There was no suitable control group. The findings therefore cannot isolate the robot's effect from contributions by human practitioners, other activities, familiarity with the setting or the child's individual characteristics.

[Rakhymbayeva and colleagues, 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2021.669972).

## APRIL: distinguishing project development from proven effectiveness

In July 2025, **Nazarbayev University** and the **Bulat Utemuratov Foundation** announced a joint initiative called **APRIL (AI-Powered Solutions for Robotic Autism Therapy)**, led by researcher **Anara Sandygulova**.

The university reported ongoing tests and plans to expand the robotic system. At a relevant conference in April 2026, the team demonstrated the APRIL digital platform, which combines social robotics with AI-related tools.

This is a concrete technology development effort in Kazakhstan.

But university announcements are **project updates and demonstrations**, not independent results of a large comparative clinical trial. Individual changes in a child's behaviour described during a demonstration cannot be generalised to future users.

As of October 2026, it was reasonable to say **APRIL was being developed and studied**. Claims of universally proven benefit or replacement of a professional were not justified.

[Nazarbayev University project announcement, 2025](https://nu.edu.kz/news-en/nu-and-the-bulat-utemuratov-foundation-launch-joint-project-to-support-children-with-autism/), [APRIL demonstration, April 2026](https://nu.edu.kz/news-en/nu-showcases-robot-assisted-therapy-technologies-for-children-with-autism/).

## AAC: technology that first enables a child to express themselves

**Augmentative and alternative communication** is not limited to digital devices. It may involve gestures, picture cards, symbol boards, written language, communication books or apps that speak aloud.

Its principal aim is **to provide an accessible way for someone to convey what they want to say**. That might be a request, a refusal, a story, a question or a joke.

For example, if a child finds it hard to say 'I need a break', a suitable communication board may let them make their choice clear. That is valuable in itself and should not be described as 'correcting autism'.

### What systematic reviews found

In 2025, **Therrien and colleagues** reviewed **20 studies** of speech-generating devices among autistic preschoolers.

Of these, **14** focused mainly on teaching requests, and **six** on a broader range of communication functions. Eleven studies reported moderate or large effects on their chosen measures.

However, the studies varied in their participants, instructional methods and devices. Fewer than half fully met ecological-validity criteria, meaning researchers did not always sufficiently examine whether outcomes translated into natural settings.

The question is therefore not only whether a child can press a particular button, but whether they can **communicate with people in everyday life**.

[Therrien and colleagues, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/07434618.2025.2479768).

### Does AAC prevent spoken-language development?

The evidence does not justify that conclusion. A review by **Pope, Light and Laubscher** examined naturalistic developmental behavioural approaches combined with AAC for minimally speaking autistic children and reported benefits on some communication and language measures.

These findings **do not justify withholding accessible communication** in an attempt to induce spoken language. At the same time, no AAC system guarantees that any particular child will develop speech.

Individual needs, the support of people around the child and opportunities to use the system across settings are essential when choosing communication supports.

[Pope and colleagues' systematic review, published online in 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10803-024-06382-7).

## What has been implemented in Kazakhstan: UNICEF's programme

In December 2025, UNICEF Kazakhstan and government partners published an assessment of the assistive-technology ecosystem for inclusive education.

Its primary focus was **access to education and communication**, not an autism treatment. The programme involved children with different special educational needs.

Researchers and practitioners identified inadequate equipment, insufficient language adaptation and limited preparation among teachers for alternative communication tools.

[UNICEF assistive technology ecosystem report, 2025](https://www.unicef.org/kazakhstan/en/reports/assistive-technologies-childs-needs-assessment-and-ecosystem-kazakhstan).

### Results from March to December 2025

UNICEF's detailed digital-inclusion report, **dated December 2025**, summarised programme activities from March through December 2025:

| Reported measure | Result |
| --- | ---: |
| Children individually assessed for technology needs | **34** |
| Children who received individually selected devices | **34** |
| Schools receiving equipment resource kits | **11** |
| Teachers completing in-person cascade training | **403** |
| Reported online course completions | **12,000** |

*Source: [UNICEF Kazakhstan, Digital Inclusion in Primary Schools](https://www.unicef.org/kazakhstan/en/media/15801/file/Analytical_Mission_Report_Kazakhstan_2025.pdf.pdf). Activities took place in 2025.*

The units must not be confused. **Thirty-four children does not mean 34 autistic children**, since multiple types of support needs were included. **Twelve thousand course completions** are not independent measurements of educators' practical skills after training.

Nor does the number of devices delivered **automatically demonstrate** improved learning outcomes or quality of life. That would require follow-up assessment of participation, communication access and continued equipment use.

### Why the right technology depends on the environment

Even a carefully chosen communication app may be of little use when it cannot be configured in an accessible language, teachers do not know how to respond to the child, the device cannot be used in class or no one is available to maintain it.

Scientific evaluation therefore considers not only **technical functionality** but also how ready the surrounding environment is to support the tool.

## Virtual reality and apps: what international reviews show

VR, games, video modelling and mobile apps can support practice of specific actions or simulated situations. Yet one VR programme may differ as much from another in purpose as ordinary classroom lessons do.

A 2024 meta-analysis included **six randomised trials** of immersive VR for autistic children and adolescents. Differences were reported on some social, emotional and cognitive outcomes. The limited number of trials reduces confidence about how reproducible the findings are.

A 2025 systematic review of **14 VR studies** also noted small samples, differing activities and outcome measures, making it difficult to combine the results into one dependable estimate.

[VR meta-analysis, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ridd.2024.104771), [VR review, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11840372/).

Another 2025 integrative review considered **11 studies** of digital tools for communication and collaboration. Evidence was heterogeneous, so the authors could not establish that any one type of digital technology was superior under all circumstances.

**The central question about an app or VR programme:** does the skill persist outside the software? Successful completion of a task in a simulated classroom does not necessarily mean that a child will find the real school environment easier.

[Kauts and Rather, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.reia.2025.202625).

## Artificial intelligence: the limits of justified conclusions

AI may help vary an exercise's difficulty, analyse research data, identify measurable features of an interaction or provide a different user interface.

But three separate uses need to be distinguished:

| AI application | Question being addressed | What must not be promised |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Adapting exercises** | Is the current level of difficulty suitable? | That the algorithm automatically knows all of a child's needs |
| **Analysing video or speech for research** | Which measurable features occur in the collected data? | That a statistical model reliably explains the causes of behaviour |
| **Proposed diagnostic support** | Might a new model help a professional assessment? | That autism can be diagnosed from a single video or glance without separate clinical validation |

**The word AI on a product does not establish clinical accuracy or effectiveness.** Such claims require independent trials with suitable samples, error analyses and clearly reported limitations.

### Protecting children's data

Photographs and facial videos, speech recordings, medical information and school observations can be sensitive personal data.

Ask why the data are collected, how long they are retained, who can access them and whether processing can be declined. Avoid uploading a child's records or videos to unknown services to obtain a 'quick diagnosis'.

UNICEF's [report on governance of educational technology data](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/reports/data-governance-edtech) discusses privacy breaches, inaccurate profiling and commercial use of children's information.

## Practical guidance: how families can assess a technology offer

The following questions are educational prompts for discussion with a professional. They are not substitutes for individual assessment or formal equipment procurement procedures.

### Identify the actual need first

Instead of asking 'which tablet is best?', start with 'what messages does the child struggle to communicate in an accessible way?'.

Instead of asking 'should we try robot sessions?', consider 'what everyday goal are we trying to support, and why was a robot chosen?'.

Technology should serve a need, not require a child to adapt to an expensive demonstration.

### Five questions before buying

| Area to check | Example question |
| --- | --- |
| **Goal** | What specific difficulty or need is the device intended to address? |
| **Evidence** | Are there independent studies of this product or approach? |
| **Accessibility** | Can it use a suitable language and control method? |
| **Outcomes** | How will benefit be assessed outside the session or app? |
| **Safety** | How are data stored, can participation be declined and can the equipment be returned? |

When a vendor claims 'proven effectiveness', ask for **the full original research**, not just a promotional video. Consider whether the study involved children with comparable needs, included a suitable comparison group and assessed quality of life.

### Do not turn interaction into coercion

Kazakhstan robot studies highlighted the need to consider children's reactions and willingness to participate. A technology should allow a child to pause or decline an activity.

The length of time a child watches a screen or robot without moving **is not, by itself, evidence of communication quality or wellbeing**.

### Is the most expensive technology necessary?

No universal rule makes a digital tool better than a simple one. One child may find a photograph board easiest, another may prefer a speech-generating device, and another may benefit from accessible written instructions.

Choice depends on the child's real goals and capacities, their environment and the people communicating with them.

## What Kazakhstan research still needs to investigate

**Transfer into everyday life.** Examine not only interest in a robot during a session, but communication, learning and daily participation weeks and months later.

**Accessibility in Kazakh and Russian.** Evaluate linguistic and cultural adaptation of communication systems, rather than only translating interface text.

**Regional access.** Measure real availability of equipment, professionals, repairs and teacher training, particularly outside major cities.

**Autistic people as research participants.** Include users' own experiences and preferences, including those who communicate through AAC.

**Benefit relative to costs.** Determine whether spending leads to sustained improvements in specific abilities, not just more devices installed.

These are proposed research questions reflecting evidence gaps, not findings from completed national trials.

## What is established and what remains unproven?

**Established:** Kazakhstan has published studies of children's interactions with social robots and implemented programmes providing communication and educational access technologies.

**Established with limitations:** international studies suggest benefits of some AAC and digital approaches for particular skills. Results vary with participant characteristics, methods and settings.

**Not established:** that APRIL, any app, VR system or AI universally produces lasting improvements for most autistic children in Kazakhstan or can replace professionals.

**Main conclusion:** **technology's scientific and practical value depends not on novelty but on whether it helps a particular child communicate, learn and take part in life safely, accessibly and with autonomy.**
