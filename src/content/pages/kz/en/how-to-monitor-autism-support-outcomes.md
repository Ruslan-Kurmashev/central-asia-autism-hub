To tell whether chosen support is helping, do not rely only on a general impression after sessions or on one assessment scale.

Before starting, identify 1-3 meaningful outcomes, record a baseline, set a review date and track not only improvements but also distress, adverse effects, demands and whether a skill transfers to everyday life.

## 1. Define what should change

"The method helps" is too vague.

Identify the specific outcome you expect. For example:

- the child can ask for a break
- it takes less time to fall asleep
- the child can eat a wider range of safe foods
- fewer prompts are needed for dressing
- pain occurs less often
- the child can participate in a lesson without severe overload.

Without a clear goal, the outcome will also be difficult to assess.

## 2. Record the baseline before beginning

The baseline is the starting point for comparison.

Record what happens now, before introducing new support.

Measures can include frequency, duration, the number of prompts needed, successful attempts, independence, participation, sleep, pain, comfort or family demands.

### Example

If the goal is to ask for help independently:

**over five school days, the child asked for help independently in 2 out of 20 situations where help was needed.**

If the goal concerns sleep:

**falling asleep takes 55 minutes on average.**

If the goal is dressing:

**the complete sequence requires approximately six prompts.**

This is much more useful than writing **"usually everything goes badly."**

## 3. Choose just 1-3 main indicators

You do not have to measure everything.

A few genuinely important outcomes are usually sufficient for one goal.

These may include:

- **frequency** - how often an action or situation occurs
- **duration** - how long falling asleep, marked distress or another state lasts
- **independence** - how much help is needed
- **communication** - whether the person can convey the intended message
- **participation** - whether they can take part in a valued activity
- **comfort or quality of life** - whether daily situations have become easier
- **family demands** - the time, resources and stress associated with the programme.

## 4. Do not measure only "autism symptoms"

A lower total score on a questionnaire measuring autism-related characteristics does not necessarily mean someone's life has improved.

More important questions concern functional communication, independence, participation, sleep, pain, comfort, relationships and quality of life.

If a scale is used, interpret it alongside meaningful changes in everyday life.

## 5. Track the amount of assistance as well as success

A skill can appear improved simply because adults provide more prompts.

Record whether the person completes the action independently, with a visual or spoken prompt, after a demonstration or with physical assistance.

The amount and type of assistance are themselves part of the outcome.

## 6. Check whether a skill transfers to other settings

A skill that appears only with one professional, in one room, within an app or after a special prompt may not help in daily life.

Observe whether it is used at home, in school, with different adults, with peers and in new situations.

Success during a session and functional success outside it are not the same thing.

## 7. Check whether the benefit lasts

Some changes are visible only during an intensive programme.

If sustained improvement matters for the goal, check again later whether it remains after support is reduced or ends.

## 8. Track marked distress and adverse effects

Unwanted effects matter as much as positive changes.

Watch for pain, poorer sleep, changes in eating, fear, avoidance, injuries, adverse effects of medicines or devices, lost interest in valued activities, severe tiredness and persistent marked distress.

If a programme improves one measure but significantly worsens another important aspect of wellbeing, both must inform the decision.

## 9. Consider the child's own views

Support must not be evaluated from adults' perspectives alone.

Consider how the person communicates assent, refusal, tiredness, fear, discomfort, a wish for a break or a preference for another option.

Persistent refusal of optional support should not automatically become a new goal to **"eliminate refusal."**

## 10. Consider the demands on the family

A programme may offer a small gain while requiring many hours of sessions, daily home exercises, long journeys, major expenses or less time for sleep and rest.

These effects count as outcomes too.

## 11. Avoid changing too many things at once

Starting several new methods while changing diet, adding supplements and changing school programmes makes it very hard to understand what contributed to a result.

When safe and feasible, document what changed and when.

This principle must not delay necessary treatment or urgent medical care.

## 12. Set a review date before starting

Agree on the review date before support begins.

Review earlier if adverse effects or concerning medical signs arise.

## What to discuss at the review

Compare the outcomes with the baseline and answer six questions:

1. Has there been a meaningful improvement?
2. Does it transfer to everyday life?
3. Does it last?
4. Are there adverse effects?
5. Are the demands acceptable?
6. Is the goal still important?

## When continuing makes sense

Continuing can be reasonable if an important goal is improving, the change is evident in daily life, significant adverse effects are absent, the child is not experiencing unacceptable distress and demands remain proportionate.

## When to modify the plan

Sometimes the intensity, length, method of delivery, prompts, environment, goal, professional or outcome measures should be adjusted.

A partial benefit may mean the plan needs adaptation.

## When to pause or stop

Reasons can include:

- no meaningful benefit after an adequate period
- significant adverse effects
- persistent severe distress
- unacceptable demands
- persistent refusal of optional support
- financial harm
- gains that do not transfer into everyday life
- the original goal no longer matters.

Stopping support that does not provide sufficient benefit is a reasonable decision.

## When medical assessment is needed

Separate medical assessment is important for:

- new seizures or unusual periods of unresponsiveness
- loss of previously acquired skills
- significant pain
- major weight loss
- dehydration
- choking or swallowing difficulties
- sudden changes in sleep
- new severe self-injury
- other serious physical changes.

## Improvement after support begins does not prove causation

If things improve after a new method begins, that matters. But timing alone does not establish that the method caused the improvement.

Natural development, school, other support, changes in sleep, treatment of another illness, medication and environmental changes may all influence the outcome.

## Deterioration must not be ignored either

Worsening after a programme begins does not automatically prove that the programme caused it.

However, if the change is marked, dangerous or associated with pain, eating, sleep, mental health or safety, investigate possible harm or medical causes first.

Do not continue simply to "finish the course."

## Example 1. AAC

Goal: **the child can independently communicate "help" or "break".**

Baseline: **one independent message in 15 relevant situations in a week.**

After a period of support, track independent messages, prompts needed, use at home and school, marked distress and the system's ease of use.

## Example 2. Sleep

Goal: **reduce the time needed to fall asleep.**

Baseline: **an average of 70 minutes over seven evenings.**

After changes to the plan, record sleep-onset time, night waking, daytime sleepiness, marked distress and potential adverse effects.

Significant snoring, breathing difficulties, pain or suspected seizures require a separate medical pathway.

## Example 3. Sensory support

Goal: **the child can participate in a school lesson with less overload.**

Measure comfortable participation time, needing to leave the classroom, distress and environmental adjustments.

If a quiet place, reduced noise or other environmental changes resolve the problem, that is a successful support outcome too.

## Example 4. Behavioural support

Goal: **reduce self-injury through accessible functional communication and removing triggers.**

At review, examine not only reduced self-injury but also a safe way to express needs, distress, medical causes and transfer of the skill to different settings.

## A simple observation diary

Five items are enough for each entry:

1. **Date and situation**
2. **What happened**
3. **What support or prompting was used**
4. **The outcome**
5. **Any marked distress, adverse effects or extra demands**

Keep records accessible only to family members and professionals who need them. Do not post medical reports, the child's full name or other personal health data in public chats. Use a de-identified example when that is sufficient.

## Do not turn monitoring into constant testing

Measurements should support decisions, not turn the child's life into an endless experiment.

You do not need to count everything. Choose a few meaningful outcomes and collect only the information necessary for decisions.

## What to take to a medical or professional appointment

Prepare a short summary: the main difficulty, when it started, whether skills were lost, any pain or seizures, changes to sleep or eating, medicines and supplements being taken, how the person expresses discomfort and what has been tried.

## Brief review checklist

1. **What changed compared with baseline?**
2. **Is the change large enough to matter?**
3. **Does the improvement appear outside sessions?**
4. **Is there harm or marked distress?**
5. **Are demands acceptable?**
6. **Does the person want to continue?**
7. **Is the goal still relevant?**
8. **Should we continue, change, stop or seek medical assessment?**
