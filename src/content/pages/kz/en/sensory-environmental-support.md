A child refuses to enter the school dining hall. Other children are talking, cutlery is clattering, food smells are strong and people stand close together in the queue. Rather than trying to make the child sit at a table for longer, first find out what is preventing them from eating comfortably.

Some autistic children are particularly sensitive to sounds, lights, smells, touch or movement. Others may notice certain sensations less or actively seek them. None of these differences alone establishes a need for specialist sensory therapy.

Often, changing the environment is a useful first step. The aim is not to "normalise the sensory system" but to make everyday activities more accessible.

## Which sensory differences affect everyday life?

One child covers their ears at the sound of a hairdryer, another dislikes clothing seams, and a third enjoys jumping, rocking or handling objects. Sensitivity to some sensations may coexist with seeking others. Responses also vary with fatigue, health and circumstances.

Sensory experiences may involve:

- sounds, such as school bells, household equipment or traffic
- bright or flickering lights and visually busy environments
- touch, clothing, haircuts, washing hair and personal space
- smells, tastes, temperature and food textures
- movement, balance, body position and the sense of effort.

An unusual reaction is not inherently misbehaviour and cannot establish a diagnosis on its own. What matters is whether the situation affects eating, learning, sleep, hygiene, rest or an activity the child wants to join.

## How to recognise overload without overlooking other causes

Sensory overload describes a situation in which several sensations or their intensity become too much for a person. A child may cover their ears, leave, cry, stop speaking, respond less often or seek somewhere quieter. These are possible signs of distress, not a required checklist or a separate diagnosis.

The same action can have several explanations. Covering an ear could reflect loud noise, ear pain, infection, headache, fear or anxiety. Avoiding a classroom could be related to a confusing task, conflict, fatigue or too many demands.

Observe what happened before the difficulty, what the child may be communicating and whether reducing sensory demands makes a difference. Do not assume every behaviour is explained by sensory differences.

## Try adapting the environment first

Adjustments do not require a child to learn to tolerate distress beforehand. Options include reducing noise, changing lighting, choosing comfortable clothing, warning about a loud event, providing a quieter place or allowing a break.

[NICE CG170](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations) advises considering the physical environment, including noise and lighting, when supporting autistic children. This does not mean every specific adjustment has been established as effective by randomised trials.

### Example: a school dining hall

The following four steps are a way of thinking through the situation, not a universal treatment protocol.

1. **Identify the difficulty.** Clattering dishes, queues, smells or crowding might contribute. Do not assume only one cause.
2. **Choose an adjustment.** Try a seat near the edge of the room, a different lunch time, suitable hearing protection or a quieter space. Discuss options with the child in a way they can use.
3. **Check participation.** Can the child eat and communicate discomfort? Do not measure success only by how long they remain motionless.
4. **Review the choice.** Keep a helpful adjustment. If distress persists, investigate other causes and involve a professional when needed.

The same process can be used for classroom lighting, touch during personal care or a break in a shop. An adjustment does not have to be a temporary exercise in "getting used to it". Headphones or access to quiet space need not automatically be withdrawn.

For more examples, see the companion article [Sensory overload: adapting the environment](/kz/en/parents/sensory-overload-environmental-adaptations/).

## Environmental adjustments are not the same as sensory therapy

### Changing conditions for participation

Reducing noise, adapting lighting, wearing comfortable clothing, following a predictable routine, having somewhere to rest and respecting personal space can make an activity accessible. The focus is on conditions for participation, not necessarily changing sensory processing itself.

### Individual sensory strategies

Headphones, movement breaks, fidget objects, seating choices and certain types of tactile input may be used for specific goals. But enjoying a fidget does not prove improved attention, and wearing a weighted vest does not mean the child will learn better. Findings about one tool cannot be transferred automatically to another.

### Ayres Sensory Integration

Ayres Sensory Integration (ASI) is a distinct approach with defined principles and requirements. It is not the same as free play in a sensory room, massage or ordinary swinging. When considering a programme, ask about the professional's preparation, assessment methods and measurable goals.

[Read about Ayres Sensory Integration](/kz/en/parents/ayres-sensory-integration/).

### Activity schedules and listening programmes

A planned series of movement and sensory activities throughout the day is sometimes called a "sensory diet". Some studies use the term *sensory activity schedule*. These terms overlap, but neither names one standardised programme.

Auditory integration and commercial listening programmes are also different from ordinary hearing protection. Claims about better speech, behaviour or "brain functioning" need direct evidence for the particular intervention.

[See the Russian-language guide to listening programmes](/kz/ru/parents/auditory-integration-listening-programs/).

## What does research show?

Reviews cover different interventions and different outcomes. Positive findings for one goal should not become promises of improvement across every developmental area.

### ASI: individual goals and limitations

The [systematic review by Acuña and colleagues (2025)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40193295/) included nine randomised trials with 344 children younger than 12. Six studies enrolled autistic children, while three enrolled other populations. The authors reported positive findings for achievement of individualised goals among autistic children, but not for reducing outcomes such as irritability or noncompliance.

The UK [SenITA study by Randell and colleagues (2022)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35766242/) randomised 138 autistic children with sensory processing difficulties to sensory integration therapy or usual care. It found no statistically significant overall benefit on the main measured outcomes at 6 or 12 months. This does not prove no individual child could benefit for a specific goal, but it does not support broad promises.

[RCOT (2026)](https://www.rcot.co.uk/explore-resources/children-young-people-families/sensory-approaches) recommends weighing potential benefits against demands, cost and simpler options. This is UK professional guidance, not a legal requirement in Kazakhstan.

### Deep pressure and other strategies

The [review by Piller and colleagues (2025)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41321460/) included 21 studies of various sensory-based interventions for children and young people with sensory processing difficulties. The authors rated support for certain forms of deep tactile pressure and caregiver training as strong under their own framework. This is not equivalent to high-certainty GRADE evidence or proof that any massage, blanket or vest helps every child.

The same review found moderate-strength evidence that alternative seating does not improve attention. Individual studies of fidget objects also do not justify recommending them automatically to improve concentration.

[Read about weighted products and their safety](/kz/en/parents/weighted-blankets-vests-safety/).

### Sensory activity schedules

The [review by Latifi and colleagues (2026)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41335028/) included nine studies of sensory activity schedules, mostly in schools. Overall evidence strength was low. Possible benefits for particular school outcomes do not establish effectiveness for sleep or behaviour at home.

A [study by Dehghani and colleagues (2025)](https://publish.kne-publishing.com/index.php/JMR/article/view/17507) involving 32 autistic children compared in-person with remotely supported delivery. Both groups received sensory activities, so the study does not determine whether the programme works better than not receiving it.

## Safety and when medical assessment is needed

Sensory support must not become coercion. A child must be able to refuse massage, hugs, deep pressure, swinging or any other activity, and stop when uncomfortable. Pressure and weighted products must never be used for punishment, restraint or restricted movement.

Noise-reducing headphones can be useful but are not automatically necessary for every child. Consider whether the child can hear essential warnings and use the chosen equipment safely.

For necessary activities such as brushing teeth or medical examinations, it may be helpful to increase predictability and comfort gradually. First investigate pain or fear, offer support and make stopping possible. The objective is not to make a child endure any discomfort.

Seek medical assessment if sensitivity appears suddenly, there is pain, new hearing or balance difficulties, problems chewing or swallowing, a sudden major change in eating or loss of previously acquired skills. A child may cover an ear because of an infection rather than overload. Do not postpone necessary medical care in favour of experimental sensory sessions.

## How to tell whether support is helping

"Normalise sensory processing" is too vague a goal. Describe the everyday situation and what improvement would mean:

- the child can eat at school with suitable adjustments without severe discomfort
- they can request less noise or a break through an accessible communication method
- getting dressed, visiting necessary places or joining chosen activities becomes easier.

Distinguish independent participation from actions requiring prompts and situations where an adult has achieved only outward quietness. No crying or movement does not, on its own, demonstrate comfort.

Agree with a professional when outcomes will be reviewed, what observations matter and what to do if there is no benefit or distress increases. A useful adjustment does not automatically need to be faded out.

## Questions for a professional in Kazakhstan

Centres may describe games, massage, movement sessions or ASI under the general name "sensory integration". These are not equivalent solely because they share a label. A sensory room or a brief-course certificate does not establish professional competence or ASI fidelity.

Before starting, ask:

1. Which specific everyday difficulty is the intervention meant to address?
2. Could conditions at home, nursery or school be changed first?
3. How was the child assessed, and were their interests and preferences considered?
4. What is the exact method and what are the professional's base qualifications, competencies and additional training?
5. What evidence applies to this method, the child's age and this goal?
6. How will participation, comfort and independence be assessed, when will the plan be reviewed and when should it stop?

Check professional education, role and any applicable local service requirements separately. International studies and UK professional guidance do not replace Kazakhstan law.
