Functional Communication Training (FCT) teaches a more accessible way to communicate a need when a person has previously expressed that need through behaviour others find difficult to manage. For example, a child may shout to end an activity because they do not yet have an effective way to request a break.

FCT is not intended to make a child more compliant or to suppress every unusual behaviour. The first task is to understand what is happening, then help the person communicate their need in a way others can recognise and respond to.

## Where to start

### First, check for other possible causes

Changes in behaviour may be linked to pain, sleep problems, digestive problems, anxiety or another condition needing medical attention. Lighting, noise, sensory overload, task difficulty, changes to routines and relationships also matter. Where relevant, consider the possibility of abuse or exploitation.

This is not a box to tick and skip. The [NICE guidance on supporting autistic children](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations) recommends considering medical, psychological, communication and environmental factors and changing conditions that contribute to distress. Teaching a new request must not replace treatment for pain, accessibility adjustments or necessary support.

### Then investigate the function of the behaviour

The *function* of behaviour refers to what it helps a person obtain or avoid. Depending on the circumstances, this might involve access to an object, attention, a break from a demand or a sensory consequence. Its function cannot be inferred reliably from how the behaviour looks.

Functional assessment can include conversations with family members and teachers, observation in different contexts and documentation of what happened before and after an incident. The professional considers these observations alongside medical and environmental factors, develops a testable hypothesis and agrees on a support goal with the family. This is not the same as deliberately provoking dangerous behaviour as an experiment.

## Example: helping a child ask for a break

During a noisy group activity, a child shouts and lies on the floor. The reason is not known in advance. The room may be too loud, instructions may be unclear, continuing may be difficult or the child may be in pain.

Adults should first check the child's wellbeing and adjust the environment, perhaps reducing noise or offering a quieter place. If assessment indicates that the child cannot easily communicate a need for a break, they can be supported to use the word "break", a gesture, a picture or a button on a communication device. "Too loud" may also be a useful message during sensory overload.

The new communication method must be available when it is needed. If every request for a break is met with "Finish first", the message does not have the intended effect. Communication becomes useful when partners recognise the request and adjust the situation reasonably. This does not mean every wish can always be met immediately.

## Choosing an alternative way to communicate

FCT does not require spoken language. A short word, gesture, picture, communication board or speech-generating device might work. The choice depends on what the person understands, can use independently and can access at home, school and elsewhere.

FCT and [augmentative and alternative communication (AAC)](/kz/en/parents/aac/) are compatible. AAC may provide the means for expressing the new message. [PECS](/kz/en/parents/pecs/) is one particular picture-exchange approach, but completing PECS is not a prerequisite for other types of AAC.

Existing communication must also be recognised. If someone clearly communicates pain, refusal or a need through a gesture, word or behaviour, adults should not ignore it and insist on a particular card. The goal is accessible, independent communication rather than a prescribed response format.

## What does the evidence say about FCT?

A [2025 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41464031/) pooled 34 single-case experimental studies involving 79 autistic children aged 2-8. The authors reported a large combined effect on behaviours of concern (Tau-BC = 0.97) and a moderate-to-large effect on alternative behaviour (Tau-BC = 0.78).

Tau-BC is a statistical measure of change in single-case designs. Values of 0.97 and 0.78 do **not** mean FCT helps 97% and 78% of children respectively, and they do not predict the outcome for an individual child.

There were important limitations. Only 29.4% of the included studies met the What Works Clearinghouse (WWC) standards, either fully or with reservations. Reporting on treatment fidelity, family relevance, maintenance and generalisation of skills was often insufficient.

A [2021 review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32929757/) also distinguishes efficacy under controlled conditions from lasting benefit in everyday life. A [2024 systematic analysis of school studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39056488/) found that experimental results are not always matched by convincing evidence of FCT's feasibility in ordinary school settings. A skill learned with a professional cannot be assumed to persist at home or in class.

## What if a request cannot be met immediately?

Sometimes adults cannot provide a desired item or start an activity straight away. Some FCT programmes teach waiting after a person has learned to make a request. Extending the waiting period can, however, be accompanied by the return of previously reduced behaviour, as described in a [systematic review of delayed reinforcement](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35164521/).

Such decisions require individual assessment. In particular, adults should not artificially create intolerable conditions to elicit a request. Never withhold water, toilet access, medical care, necessary rest or AAC until a child uses the "correct" picture or gesture.

## Consent and potential adverse effects

Not every repetitive or unusual behaviour needs to be reduced. Intervention goals should address a genuine difficulty involving safety, pain, communication, participation or quality of life, rather than outward conformity with others' expectations.

Respect refusal and signs of discomfort, including non-speaking signals. If sessions increase panic, self-injury, avoidance or dependence on prompts, or restrict independent communication, stop or reconsider the programme with appropriately qualified professionals.

Self-injury, aggression and other immediate risks need professional assessment and a safety plan. Parents should not carry out experimental functional analyses of dangerous behaviour themselves or deliberately induce a crisis to test a hypothesis.

## Measuring outcomes in everyday life

A single measure, such as fewer episodes of shouting during a session, does not tell the whole story. Compare baseline and later observations across several domains:

- **Behaviour and safety.** How frequently does a difficult situation arise, how long does it last, how distressed is the person and are new risks emerging?
- **Independent communication.** Can the child request a break or help, refuse or report pain without constant prompting, using an accessible method?
- **Participation and quality of life.** Is it easier to take part in meaningful activities, while communication remains freely available?
- **Generalisation.** Does the new communication method work with different people, at home, in school and beyond the therapy room?

Using a picture only after a professional prompts the child does not yet demonstrate an independent, lasting skill. See the [Russian-language guide to evaluating support](/kz/ru/parents/kak-ponyat-pomogaet-li-podderzhka/) for further information.

## When to review the plan

Reassessment is needed if the child appears more distressed, develops new medical symptoms, requests go unanswered or prompting is not reduced. Other reasons include a lack of use outside sessions, excessive burden on the family or observations that do not support the original functional hypothesis.

Sometimes no behaviour-reduction programme is needed at all. Changing the environment, arranging medical care or recognising an existing communication method may be enough. There is no need to continue solely to complete scheduled programme stages.

## Questions to ask the professional

1. What observations support the proposed function of the behaviour? Were medical, sensory and environmental causes investigated?
2. Why was this communication method chosen? Which other AAC options were considered and what does the child prefer?
3. How will adults respond to the request, and what will change in everyday situations?
4. How will safety, independence, adverse effects and quality of life be measured?
5. How will skill use at home and school be assessed, and what would prompt a change or discontinuation of the programme?

## Considerations in Kazakhstan

FCT elements may be used in behavioural, psychological, educational, speech-language and developmental support. Calling someone an "FCT specialist" does not establish their professional qualifications or authorisation to provide a regulated service. Check their foundational qualification, relevant competencies and the requirements applying to the specific service.

## In brief

FCT may be helpful when assessment identifies a need for a more accessible way to communicate and other people are prepared to understand and respond to the message. Success should be assessed through independence, safety, the ability to refuse and everyday quality of life, not only reductions in behaviour of concern.
