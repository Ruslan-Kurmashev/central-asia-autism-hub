## 1. A scientific paper is not a news story about research

A media article might say that the cause of autism has been found, or that scientists have debunked a myth. The research paper itself is usually more cautious: it has a specific objective, methods, results and limitations.

For practice, we will use a real publication: Hviid A., Hansen J.V., Frisch M., Melbye M. (2019), *Measles, Mumps, Rubella Vaccination and Autism: A Nationwide Cohort Study*, published in *Annals of Internal Medicine*, DOI: [10.7326/M18-2101](https://doi.org/10.7326/M18-2101).

It is a useful example because the paper clearly describes the hypothesis, cohort design, national data, participants, numerical result and limitations.

## 2. Where to find the paper

A DOI is a persistent digital identifier for a publication. A link such as https://doi.org/10.7326/M18-2101 takes you to the publisher's page. In [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30831578/), you can check the authors, journal, year, abstract and bibliographic details.

Make sure you are reading the original paper rather than an advertising summary. Distinguish a research article from an editorial, a letter to the editor or a commentary on findings.

## 3. Start with the research question

The authors examined whether receiving the combined measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine was associated with an increased likelihood of a subsequent autism diagnosis, including in specific subgroups and time periods after vaccination.

Note that this was not a study of the effectiveness of all vaccines or a description of the biological mechanisms of autism. Careful reading begins by identifying the precise question.

## 4. Look at the design: this is a cohort study

The researchers used data from Danish national registries. They did not randomly allocate children to receive or not receive vaccination. Instead, they examined records of actual vaccinations and subsequently registered diagnoses.

The study was therefore a population-based observational cohort study, not an RCT. An observational design requires particular attention to possible confounding factors and the quality of registry data.

## 5. Who took part, and how long were they followed?

The cohort included 657,461 children born in Denmark between 1999 and 2010. Follow-up began at the age of one and continued within the study's eligibility and follow-up rules until the end of August 2013.

During follow-up, 6,517 autism diagnoses were recorded over 5,025,754 person-years. A person-year is not one person. If a child is followed for four years, that represents four person-years. The five million figure therefore does not mean five million separate participants.

## 6. What was compared with what?

The researchers used records of MMR vaccination and autism diagnoses. The analysis adjusted for age, birth year, sex, other vaccinations, family history of autism and a set of recorded risk factors.

Statistical adjustment is useful but cannot guarantee the absence of every unmeasured difference. Read which variables were actually included, rather than relying on the word "adjusted" alone.

## 7. How to read the main result

The authors obtained an adjusted hazard ratio (HR) of 0.93, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.85-1.02, when comparing children who received MMR with those who did not.

*AutismHub illustrates the published numbers in an original diagram rather than copying the journal's figure.*

An HR of 1 usually represents no difference in the event rate under consideration. The HR estimate of 0.93 is below 1, but the interval includes 1. These data do not establish a protective effect of MMR. The study found no increased risk of autism following MMR vaccination. It does not establish that every possible risk is exactly zero for every person under every circumstance.

## 8. What do these numbers mean in ordinary language?

The number 0.93 does not mean that the vaccine protects against autism by 7%, nor does it mean that the risk for a particular child decreases by exactly 7%. It is the adjusted ratio of the hazards of a recorded diagnosis in this cohort analysis.

The full interval and the study's limitations matter. Distinguishing the point estimate from uncertainty protects readers from oversimplified headlines.

## 9. How were alternative explanations assessed?

The authors conducted additional comparisons across subgroups and time windows after vaccination. They found no consistent increase in risk in the groups they studied. However, estimates in small subgroups are usually less precise and cannot be interpreted without their confidence intervals.

Do not search for a single chance "significant" row among many tests while ignoring the overall research question specified in advance.

## 10. What limitations were reported?

One limitation identified by the authors was that the individual medical charts were not reviewed. The accuracy of registration depends on the completeness of national data and the criteria used in the registries.

Furthermore, a Danish cohort is not identical to the population of every other country, and an observational design cannot remove every possible confounding factor. Nevertheless, the scale of the study, registry data and analyses across different circumstances strengthen its contribution to the evidence.

## 11. What can and cannot be concluded?

| A justified statement | What one paper does not establish |
| --- | --- |
| This large cohort found no increased risk of autism following MMR vaccination | All medical risks of every vaccine in every country are zero |
| The estimate relates to particular years, a particular country and groups | Participants were randomly allocated to receive vaccination |
| The authors examined several potential alternatives and subgroups | Every third factor was completely ruled out |
| The finding is consistent with no increased risk in the comparison examined | HR 0.93 proves that vaccination prevents autism |

Read a single study alongside independent studies and systematic reviews. Questions about an individual vaccination schedule should be discussed with a qualified healthcare professional.

## 12. Read six parts of a paper in the right order

1. **Title / Abstract:** what question does the paper aim to answer?
2. **Introduction:** why was the question still open?
3. **Methods:** participants, design, outcomes, analysis and limitations.
4. **Results:** actual numbers, intervals and missing data.
5. **Discussion:** what do the authors explain, and what do they not claim?
6. **Declarations:** funding, interests, data and protocol availability.

It is fine to skim the abstract first, but assess the conclusion using the methods and results, not just the abstract's final sentence.

## 13. Warning signs to look for in any paper

- The sample and eligibility criteria are described incompletely.
- The headline asserts causation, while the method establishes only an association.
- Information on participants who dropped out is missing.
- Only percentages are reported, without absolute numbers.
- Intervals and opposing findings are ignored.
- The conclusion extends beyond the population and outcome studied.
- Commercial interests are hidden or unclearly described.

These are reasons to ask questions, not to accuse researchers of fabrication automatically.

## 14. Using the downloadable worksheet

Complete the AutismHub worksheet in order: DOI and publication type, question, design, participants, the outcome measured, main estimate and interval, alternative explanations, limitations and relevance to your own question.

Do not assign an overall reliability score by counting ticks. Not every study needs to be an RCT, and a large sample cannot correct biased recruitment.

## 15. Five main points to remember

1. Understanding the original question matters more than remembering a dramatic headline.
2. The study design limits causal claims.
3. Participant numbers do not substitute for measurement quality and an appropriate comparison.
4. A confidence interval reflects statistical uncertainty, not a guarantee for a family.
5. Scientific knowledge is more reliable when independent high-quality studies agree.

Conclusion: you can learn to read scientific papers carefully even without a medical degree. The key is asking the right questions, checking the numbers and separating the authors' findings from advertising claims.
