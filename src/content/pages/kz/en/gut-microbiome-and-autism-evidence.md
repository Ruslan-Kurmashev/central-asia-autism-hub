## What is the gut microbiome?

The human gut contains many microorganisms, including bacteria, archaea, fungi and viruses. Together, they form a complex community whose composition changes throughout life.

This community is called the **gut microbiota**. The term **microbiome** is often used to refer to the microorganisms, their genes and their associated functions.

Some gut bacteria help break down food components that the human body cannot digest completely on its own. They produce metabolites that contribute to gut function and other physiological processes.

For example, microorganisms can produce short-chain fatty acids when they process certain dietary fibres. These include butyrate, acetate and propionate.

This does not mean that every microorganism is beneficial or that everyone needs exactly the same set of bacteria to stay healthy.

Age, diet, medication, infections and many other factors influence gut microbial communities. Even two healthy people can have substantially different microbiota.

### Why is the microbiome studied in autism?

In some studies, researchers have found differences in gut microbial communities between autistic and non-autistic participants.

Some autistic children also experience constipation, abdominal pain, selective eating or other circumstances that can influence the microbiota.

Researchers want to understand whether these observations are related and what might explain the differences.

Three distinct questions need to be considered:

1. Does the microbiome differ between the groups studied?
2. What causes any differences that are found?
3. Can these findings improve diagnosis or healthcare?

A positive answer to the first question does not provide an answer to the other two.

## How are the gut and brain connected?

The digestive system and nervous system interact in both directions.

Stress, for example, can affect digestion. Conversely, abdominal pain, constipation or nausea can disrupt sleep, concentration and everyday activities.

Researchers often describe these interactions as the **gut-brain axis**.

This is not a separate organ or one nerve connecting the gut to the brain. It is a shorthand for several interacting processes, including neural, immune, hormonal and metabolic signalling.

One pathway under investigation involves the **vagus nerve**. Other pathways involve substances produced by the body's cells or during metabolism. Gut microorganisms can also contribute to some of these processes.

But these observations do not show that changing a particular bacterium will necessarily change a person's behaviour.

For example, a microbial metabolite may affect nervous-system cells in a laboratory experiment. That does not establish that the metabolite causes autism in children.

A plausible laboratory mechanism is very different from a demonstrated cause of a human condition.

**The existence of the gut-brain axis is not evidence that the gut microbiome causes autism.**

## What have autism studies found?

Findings are inconsistent. Some studies identify differences in the gut microbiota of autistic and non-autistic participants. In others, these differences are small or change after researchers account for diet, age, family environment and other factors.

Several studies help illustrate why this topic is complex.

### Cell (2021): what role does diet play?

[Yap and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34767757/) analysed data from **247 participants** in a study published in *Cell* in 2021.

They examined stool samples, dietary patterns and other characteristics that might be associated with the microbiome.

After accounting for several factors, they found very limited direct microbiome differences associated with an autism diagnosis. The association between restricted dietary variety and gut microbial characteristics was more substantial.

The authors proposed a possible explanation: food preferences can influence what a person eats, and diet in turn can influence microbial communities.

This does not prove that diet explains every microbiome difference associated with autism. It does show why any observed bacterial difference should not automatically be interpreted as a cause of autism.

In an [official correction published in 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2023.12.001), the authors reported a data-conversion error affecting particular estimates in a figure and a supplementary table. Some numerical values changed, but the authors stated that the main conclusions were unchanged.

### Nature Neuroscience (2023): some differences were detected

[Morton and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-023-01361-0) combined data from several independent studies of gut microbiota and other biological measures.

They detected certain microbial and molecular differences between the groups examined. However, the results depended substantially on the dataset and participant characteristics.

This limitation matters. A pattern found in one research group may not appear in children living in another country, at a different age or with different diets.

### Nature Microbiology (2024): what does AUC 0.91 mean?

[Su and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41564-024-01739-1) analysed stool samples from **1,627 children aged 1 to 13 years**.

They investigated not only bacteria but also other microorganisms, including archaea, fungi and viruses, alongside certain microbial functions.

Using **31 markers**, they developed a machine-learning model that achieved an **AUC of 0.91** in their research programme.

This is a high measure of statistical discrimination between the groups studied. But **an AUC of 0.91 does not mean that a stool test correctly diagnoses autism in 91% of children**.

AUC describes how well a model ranks people from different groups over a range of classification thresholds.

Clinical usefulness requires additional evidence, including independent validation, sensitivity and specificity at selected thresholds, the consequences of errors, and evidence that using the test benefits patients.

Two official corrections followed publication. In [2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41564-024-01900-w), the authors added information about access to participant data. A [2026 correction](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41564-026-02496-z) expanded the disclosure of potential conflicts of interest.

These corrections are relevant to research transparency. They did not report recalculation of the original AUC estimate.

### What did a critical analysis in 2026 conclude?

In a [paper published in *Neuron*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2025.10.006), Mitchell, Dahly and Bishop critically examined research commonly used to argue that the gut microbiome causes autism.

They considered three areas: human observational studies, animal experiments and clinical intervention trials.

The authors identified conceptual and methodological weaknesses that, in their assessment, prevent the available evidence from establishing a causal role for the microbiome.

Potential confounding, reproducibility and the difficulty of translating experimental findings to people were among the major concerns.

This was a critical analysis of the literature. It was not proof that gut microorganisms cannot interact with the nervous system. Its value lies in showing why causal claims need careful evaluation.

The appropriate conclusion at present is that some statistical associations have been identified, while their origins, reliability and clinical importance remain under investigation.

## How does diet affect the microbiome?

Consider a child who prefers a narrow range of foods. They might eat particular cereals, bread and a few familiar meals while avoiding many fruits and vegetables because of their taste, smell or texture.

Their diet may differ from that of a child who regularly eats a broader variety of foods. This affects the dietary fibres and other substances available to intestinal microorganisms.

Over time, differences in diet may be associated with differences in the composition and functions of the microbiota.

Two conclusions would nevertheless be incorrect. First, that a different microbiome must have caused autism. Second, that eating more varied foods will eliminate autism. Studies do not support either claim.

The microbiome is also influenced by medication, including antibiotics, digestive health, age and environmental conditions.

Family circumstances matter too. Children living together often share dietary habits and aspects of their environment.

This is why some studies compare autistic children with their non-autistic siblings. Such comparisons help account for certain shared family factors, although they do not remove every possible source of bias.

### Should we aim for the highest possible microbial diversity?

There is no universal rule that a greater number of bacterial types always means better health.

Microbial diversity is one research measure. Its meaning depends on age, health status and how it was measured.

A stool sample also cannot fully reveal the processes occurring throughout the digestive tract.

For families, it is more useful to assess a child's diet and wellbeing than to aim for a particular microbial diversity score.

## Why can't a stool test diagnose autism?

Some research teams use machine learning to identify differences between stool samples from autistic and non-autistic people.

This can be useful for studying microbial communities. It is not an established clinical method for confirming or ruling out autism.

There are several reasons. First, microbiome composition depends strongly on diet, age, medication and digestive health.

Second, the findings can vary with laboratory methods and data-analysis procedures.

Third, a model that performs well on research data may make mistakes in other groups of children.

Finally, even a statistically significant difference between groups does not establish that the result will help a clinician assess an individual child's development.

### What about commercial microbiome tests?

Some companies sell stool analyses that claim to identify “dysbiosis”, a lack of beneficial bacteria or the need for particular supplements.

Such results cannot establish the cause of autism. Relative measurements of bacterial abundance do not reliably identify a universal probiotic or a treatment that will change the core characteristics of autism.

A clinician may recommend appropriate investigations if a child has constipation, diarrhoea, pain, a restricted diet or other symptoms.

Investigating digestive symptoms and assessing autism are different clinical tasks.

## Do probiotics and special diets help?

**Probiotics** are live microorganisms that may provide a health benefit when administered in adequate amounts under suitable conditions.

Products contain different species and strains, so findings about one preparation do not automatically apply to another.

### What have clinical trials shown?

A [systematic review and meta-analysis by Kotowska and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39173907/) in 2024 included **12 randomised trials with 630 participants**.

The authors detected a small statistical signal in some pooled measures of core autism characteristics. However, many studies had substantial limitations. **Nine were assessed as having a high risk of bias**, and other analyses found no convincing difference between groups.

The review concluded that the evidence did not provide high-quality support for using probiotics to change core autism characteristics in children.

This does not mean probiotics have no possible role in any gastrointestinal disorder. Indications for digestive conditions need to be considered separately from attempts to treat autism.

### What are prebiotics and synbiotics?

**Prebiotics** are particular substances that microorganisms selectively use in ways that can benefit health.

**Synbiotics** combine live microorganisms with substances intended to be used by them.

These ingredients alone are not evidence that a product is effective for autism. Each intervention requires studies of its particular composition, dose, population and clinical outcomes.

### Should gluten or dairy products be excluded?

Some families are advised to remove gluten, casein or whole food groups to change core autism characteristics.

However, [NICE guideline CG170](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations) does not recommend exclusion diets for that purpose.

The situation differs if a child has coeliac disease, a confirmed food allergy or another medical condition that requires a special diet. Dietary changes should then follow the relevant clinical indications.

Additional restrictions in a child who already eats very few foods may increase the risk of nutritional deficiencies.

Food choices should therefore reflect the child's health and nutritional needs, not a promise to “fix” the microbiome.

## What is known about faecal microbiota transplantation?

**Faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)** is a medical procedure in which specially prepared donor gut microbial material is administered to a recipient.

Related approaches are used for selected gastrointestinal conditions. For example, the [American Gastroenterological Association's 2024 guidance](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/fecal-microbiota-based-therapies-for-select-gastrointestinal-diseases/) considers their use for certain adults with recurrent *Clostridioides difficile* infection.

This is not evidence that FMT is an effective autism treatment.

### Why were improvements reported?

An [open study by Kang and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28122648/) in 2017 included **18 autistic children with gastrointestinal symptoms**.

The authors reported changes in some gastrointestinal and behavioural outcomes following a multicomponent intervention that included microbiota transfer.

They later published a [two-year follow-up](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30967657/).

These studies were open-label and lacked an appropriate control group. During extended follow-up, diet, medication and supplement use also changed for some participants.

It is therefore not possible to reliably attribute the changes to the transplantation procedure rather than to other factors.

Such studies may justify further investigation. They do not prove treatment effectiveness.

### What are the risks?

FMT may transmit infectious agents and cause other complications.

In 2020, the [US Food and Drug Administration](https://www.fda.gov/safety/medical-product-safety-information/fecal-microbiota-transplantation-safety-alert-risk-serious-adverse-events-likely-due-transmission) issued a safety warning about serious infections after the use of donor material.

This warning concerns procedural safety. It does not imply that every country has identical regulatory rules.

**FMT is not an established treatment for autism.** Attempting the procedure independently, or seeking it solely to change autistic characteristics, is unsafe.

## What should parents do about digestive problems?

A child may experience pain or discomfort without being able to explain it in words.

Sometimes this appears as changes in sleep, appetite, usual activity or behaviour. For example, a child may become distressed at mealtimes, avoid certain situations or find familiar activities harder to tolerate.

Such changes do not prove that a particular bowel condition is present, but they may be a reason to consider the child's health more carefully.

If constipation, diarrhoea, abdominal pain or substantial feeding difficulties occur repeatedly, discuss them with a clinician.

Before the appointment, it may help to note:

- When the symptoms began, how often they occur and any apparent patterns.
- Foods the child usually eats and avoids.
- Changes in weight, appetite and daily routines.
- Medicines and dietary supplements the child takes.
- Whether discomfort changes after eating or using the toilet.

Where food choices are very restricted, appropriate professionals may need to assess growth, nutritional intake and possible deficiencies.

This is consistent with [NICE guidance](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations) on feeding and nutritional difficulties in autistic children and young people.

Blood in the stool, severe or increasing pain, repeated substantial vomiting, signs of dehydration, noticeable weight loss or a sudden deterioration in general health require timely medical assessment.

These symptoms should not be attributed solely to autism or to presumed microbiome changes.

### What should families take away from the research?

The gut microbiome contributes to normal bodily processes. Some studies report statistical differences between microbial communities in autistic and non-autistic groups.

But findings do not always replicate, and the direction of any causal relationship remains uncertain.

There is currently no generally accepted stool test that diagnoses autism. Nor is there sufficiently reliable evidence to recommend probiotics, exclusion diets or FMT for every autistic child in order to change core autism characteristics.

None of this reduces the importance of treating gastrointestinal illness. A child with constipation, pain, diarrhoea or feeding difficulties needs care directed at those specific problems.
