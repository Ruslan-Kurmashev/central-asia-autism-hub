## 1. What the document covers

The report brings together identification of ASD, diagnostic assessment and subsequent management. It is not a catalogue of one therapeutic method. It considers child development, everyday functioning, co-occurring conditions, family needs and care arrangements. The authors updated previous AAP documents from 2007 in one publication.

It is important to distinguish **a clinical report from a professional society** from a mandatory regulatory instrument. Even within the US, AAP explicitly states that its recommendations do not define the only acceptable standard of medical care and should account for individual circumstances.

## 2. Developmental surveillance and screening

The US model provides for two distinct processes:

| Process | Purpose | What it does not do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Developmental surveillance | Regularly consider child development, family concerns and professional observations | Is not a one-off test and does not confirm a diagnosis |
| Standardised screening | Identify children who need additional assessment | Does not independently establish or rule out ASD |

AAP recommends **ASD-specific screening at 18 and 24 months**. In its separate developmental screening report, AAP also describes general standardised developmental screening at 9, 18 and 30 months. These are **different activities**. Ages 9 and 30 months should not be presented as mandatory ages for autism-specific screening.

When reasonable concerns arise outside the scheduled ages, consider further action rather than waiting for the next routine visit.

## 3. What happens after a concerning result

In the clinical approach described by the report:

1. Discuss the result with the family in understandable language and clarify developmental history.
2. Consider referral for further comprehensive assessment where indicated.
3. Arrange access to early support for identified developmental difficulties, without allowing the wait for a definitive diagnosis to cause an unjustified delay in support.
4. Continue surveillance because the child's needs change over time.

**Screening is not a diagnostic conclusion.** The report cannot be used to interpret an individual child's result without clinical assessment.

## 4. Diagnostic assessment and co-occurring conditions

AAP stresses that assessment must extend beyond ASD diagnostic criteria. Professional assessment considers developmental history, communication, functioning, health and other possible explanations or co-occurring conditions.

The report draws attention to sleep, nutrition, gastrointestinal symptoms, seizures, anxiety, ADHD, safety and other characteristics that may affect quality of life. These require separate professional assessment where indicated. Having ASD does not automatically explain every symptom.

Observation and testing tools may supplement clinical assessment, but cannot replace professional competence. This review does not establish a list of mandatory investigations or medicines for every child.

### Clinical diagnosis and investigation of possible causes

AAP distinguishes assessment of clinical autistic characteristics from investigation to clarify aetiology. Genetic testing may identify an associated condition and influence subsequent medical surveillance or genetic counselling, but the result alone neither establishes nor rules out autism. The report recommends discussing aetiological assessment with the family and involving a genetics specialist when necessary. Additional metabolic investigations, EEG and neuroimaging depend on history, examination and clinical indications. For example, EEG is not routinely ordered in the absence of suspected seizures, atypical regression or other relevant neurological signs.

Assessment of communication and daily functioning considers language, cognitive and adaptive skills, with hearing assessment when relevant concerns arise. Support for identified needs should not be delayed until a genetic cause is established.

## 5. Supporting the child and making decisions

The report proposes considering support as coordinated work across health care, education, other services and the family. The aim is to improve functioning, participation, communication and quality of life in ways meaningful to the person, not to promise elimination of autism.

The choice of support approaches should take account of goals, risks, available resources and family preferences. The suitability of a particular method depends on its evidence for the intended goal. No approach should be prescribed solely because of a diagnosis.

When referring to early support, check whether the family has reached the relevant service and agree further surveillance. AAP referral and early intervention recommendations concern the US system. The specific pathway in Kazakhstan is determined separately.
