## Why compare autism registration statistics across regions?

When families want to know how many autistic children live in their city or region, a natural question arises: why are official figures much higher in some parts of Kazakhstan than in others?

It may seem that autism must be more common wherever the numbers are higher. However, **registration statistics cannot support that conclusion**. The number of children known to the health system depends not only on how many children have autistic developmental characteristics but also on who was assessed, received a diagnosis and was entered into the register.

This article distinguishes **officially registered diagnoses** from **the actual prevalence of autism in the population**, examines the most recent regional figures available at the time of review, and explains what scientific studies can and cannot tell us.

## What do regional data for 2025 show?

On 23 April 2026, Factcheck.kz published a review of statements made by members of parliament about autism. The article reported information supplied by Kazakhstan's Ministry of Health. The nationwide registration indicator for 2025 was **243.6 per 100,000 children**. The Ministry also named the three regions with the highest reported figures.

| Region | Children registered with ASD, 2025 | Per 100,000 children |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Astana | 4,001 | **712.9** |
| Kostanay Region | 956 | **492.8** |
| East Kazakhstan Region | 780 | **450.0** |

*Source: [Factcheck.kz, 23 April 2026](https://factcheck.kz/articles/faktchek-deputaty-o-roste-chisla-detey-s-autizmom-v-kazahstane), citing the Ministry of Health of the Republic of Kazakhstan. That report does not provide figures for the remaining regions. The precise calendar date of the 2025 data snapshot was not specified.*

![Comparison of three regional ASD registration indicators in 2025, per 100,000 children.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/research/kazakhstan-regional-autism-registration-2025-en.svg)

*Figure 1. The three highest regional registration indicators from the published Ministry of Health response. This is neither a map of biological risk nor a ranking of all regions in the country. AutismHub prepared the chart using the reported figures.*

### What does 712.9 per 100,000 mean?

Imagine a hypothetical group of 100,000 children living in Astana. An indicator of 712.9 means that there are approximately **713 registered cases** per 100,000 children according to the method used for the Ministry's data.

It **does not mean** that independently assessing any random group of 100,000 children would necessarily identify exactly that many autistic children. Some children may not yet have a diagnosis, while registration itself depends on the rules of the medical reporting system.

The rate can also be expressed as approximately 0.713% of children registered in Astana. This is another way to represent the same administrative indicator, **not an independent estimate of the true prevalence of ASD**.

### Why are absolute counts alone misleading?

Astana had 4,001 registered children, compared with 956 in Kostanay Region. But the regions have different child populations. Comparing counts alone may tell us more about population size or completeness of registration than about the frequency of identification.

Regional statistics therefore often use a measure per 100,000 children:

**Number of children registered with ASD / number of children in the corresponding age group × 100,000.**

This formula permits comparisons between places of different sizes. But even a correctly calculated denominator does not eliminate differences in access to assessment, age composition, diagnostic classification or registration rules.

## New 2015-2025 study: first registrations and specialist availability

On 14 November 2026, [Mansharipova and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare14223910) published a study in *Healthcare*. They analysed annual reports from Kazakhstan's psychiatric services (Form 10-f) covering **2015-2025**. Their analysis concerned **first-time registered cases** under ICD-10 codes F84.0, F84.1 and F84.5 among children aged **0-17 years**, alongside the number of child psychiatrists.

**This is a different measure from the total number of children with ASD on a register.** For example, the sources report different values for Astana in 2025.

| Source | What is counted | Astana, per 100,000 children |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Ministry of Health response reported by Factcheck.kz | Children registered with ASD under the Ministry's reporting definition | **712.9** |
| Mansharipova and colleagues, 2026 | Cases first registered during the year under the specified ICD-10 codes | **221.4** |

The two figures must not be subtracted from each other, added together or treated as successive points in a time-series chart. They measure different things and come from different administrative sources.

For 2025, the new study reported **119.5 first registrations per 100,000 children in Akmola Region** and **28.7 in Karaganda Region combined with Ulytau**. Astana had the highest rate among the territories assessed. The authors used **16 territorial units based on historical boundaries**, not separate figures for every modern region.

At the national level, the availability of child psychiatrists decreased from **2.06 to 1.43 per 100,000 children** over the study period, while the first-registration rate increased from **3.9 to 63.5 per 100,000 children**. These trends matter for planning services, but **the study did not show** that the change in psychiatrist numbers caused registration rates to rise. Aggregate data cannot tell us the actual diagnostic workload of each individual clinician.

The researchers also identified problems with the denominators used for regional rates. In 2015-2022, the sum of regional child-population figures was **7-9% below the national total**. The authors made additional calculations for regions whose boundaries changed after 2022. Sensitivity analyses did not remove all the uncertainty, so regional estimates, particularly for earlier years, should be regarded as approximate.

The study adds important evidence about regional registration. However, it **does not measure the true population prevalence of autism** or establish a single cause for regional differences.

## What did research from 2016-2022 find?

In June 2025, researchers from Karaganda Medical University published a paper in *Frontiers in Psychiatry* about trends in autism and other neurodevelopmental conditions in Kazakhstan.

The authors were Karina Nukeshtayeva, Zhanerke Bolatova, Aza Galayeva, Nurzhamal Shintayeva and Gulmira Zhanalina.

They analysed annual mental-health service statistics for children aged **0-17 years** over **2016-2022**. The study considered childhood autism and atypical autism as separate ICD-10 diagnostic categories.

That distinction matters: **figures for childhood autism and atypical autism cannot automatically be equated with a modern combined ASD registration indicator**.

### Which regional differences were reported in 2022?

| Region or city | Published indicator for 2022 | Diagnostic category |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Astana | 263.7 per 100,000 | Childhood autism |
| Akmola Region | 112.7 per 100,000 | Childhood autism |
| North Kazakhstan Region | 142.8 per 100,000 | Atypical autism |

*Source: [Nukeshtayeva and colleagues, 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1520460). The figures refer to children aged 0-17 and to different diagnostic categories. They illustrate selected study findings, not a single regional ranking.*

The researchers described higher registered indicators for particular forms of autism in some northern regions than in southern ones, as well as considerable differences between large cities. In some territories, the indicator increased significantly over the seven-year period.

But the study **did not demonstrate** that living in a particular region itself changes the probability of autism. A comparison of administrative rates describes a statistical pattern, not an individual's cause of autism.

### Why should figures from 2022 and 2025 not be placed on one scale?

The 2022 paper distinguished childhood autism from atypical autism according to ICD-10 categories, whereas the Ministry's published 2025 response concerned an aggregate ASD registration measure. The periods, sources, administrative procedures and potentially the groups of children counted also differ.

It would therefore be incorrect to subtract 263.7 from 712.9 and claim that the resulting difference represents the increase in Astana's ASD registration rate under a single method. These are **different statistical definitions**, even though the units look the same.

## What did the 2014-2024 national registry study add?

In July 2026, *Frontiers in Psychiatry* published a study by Amir Baikatov, Ayat Assemov, Dias Alimbetov and Almira Kustubayeva. The authors analysed national electronic medical-register data for children **under 16 years of age** for 2014-2024.

The dataset contained **214,904 registry records** across several neurodevelopmental diagnostic groups. Importantly, the unit of analysis was a **child-year**, not a unique child over the entire eleven-year period. The total number of records must not be presented as the same number of individual children.

The study found that the national registered ASD indicator increased from **16.8 to 190.5 per 100,000 children** between 2014 and 2024. The authors identified substantial variation across regions, with rates often higher in major cities and in the north and east of the country.

An especially important point for regional comparisons is that administrative rates also reflect the performance of the diagnostic system. The authors discuss the availability of specialists, referral pathways, changing diagnostic practices and administrative rules as possible influences.

## Why might regional rates differ?

The mechanisms below **may** contribute to some of the variation. They are not separately established causal explanations for each region of Kazakhstan.

### 1. Access to specialists

Where there are more child psychiatrists and specialist assessment services, a family may be able to move from initial developmental concerns to a confirmed diagnosis sooner. In an area with fewer services, some children may remain without a diagnostic record for longer.

### 2. Referral and registration procedures

A child must pass through a particular pathway in the health system and be registered before appearing in official statistics. Changes to reporting rules or institutional responsibilities can alter counts, even when the underlying need for support changes much less.

### 3. Awareness among families and professionals

When parents and professionals are more familiar with neurodevelopmental differences and know where to seek help, more children may receive an assessment. Increased awareness alone does not establish that the true frequency of autism has risen.

### 4. Population age structure

The proportion of children in different age groups varies between regions. A study covering ages 0-17, another including children under 16 and a third focusing on preschool children cannot be compared directly without accounting for their age composition.

### 5. Diagnostic classification

It matters whether a source combines childhood autism, atypical autism and other diagnostic categories into a single ASD measure. Changes in classification or habitual coding can create the appearance of sudden regional shifts.

### Can the data show an effect of environmental pollution?

No. These regional comparisons do not establish that air pollution, industrial activity, climate or any other regional exposure **caused** the differences observed. Establishing a causal effect would require studies with individual-level data, measured exposures, control of confounding factors and an appropriate design.

**A high registration rate in a region is not evidence of a correspondingly high biological risk of autism.**

## Why is there still no comparable map for today's regions?

A truly comparable map of all regions for a single year would require, for each territory, the number of registered children, the exact definition of ASD used, age group, child-population denominator, date of the data snapshot and a description of the method.

The Ministry of Health response reported by Factcheck.kz contains only three regional figures for children on the register. Mansharipova and colleagues (2026) add first-registration data for 16 historical territorial units. Those data do not form a comparable map of all children on the register in each modern region. Different definitions and uncertainty about denominators prevent us from filling in missing values mechanically.

We deliberately do not fill gaps with assumptions, interpolation or approximate recalculations combining different studies.

## What do regional statistics mean for parents?

The practical value of these data lies not in estimating an individual child's likelihood of autism but in discussing **equal access to assessment and support**. Useful questions for society and services include whether qualified professionals are available in smaller cities, whether assessment pathways are clear and how long families wait for necessary services.

A high regional indicator does not mean a particular child needs a special treatment method. Nor is a low indicator a reason to ignore developmental concerns or delay consultation.

When parents notice developmental differences or have concerns, it is more useful to discuss **the individual child's characteristics** with a qualified professional than to rely on the region's position in a statistical table.

For information about initial steps, see [Support in Kazakhstan](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/en/help-kazakhstan/). The same general principle of seeking an appropriate developmental assessment applies regardless of a region's rank in the statistics.

## What is established, and what remains uncertain?

**Supported by the published data:** recorded autism indicators vary substantially between Kazakhstan's regions. Official reports and scientific studies describe clear differences between territories and over time.

**Plausible but not measured as an individual causal effect for each region:** contributions from access to specialists, referral pathways, diagnostic rules, awareness and infrastructure.

**Not established by these sources:** the true population prevalence of autism in each region, the cause of autism for any individual or a causal effect of a specific environmental exposure on autism risk.

An important next step in regional research is to gather standardised information for all regions using comparable definitions of age, registration and diagnosis.
