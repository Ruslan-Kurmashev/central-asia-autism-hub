## Two children, one diagnosis: why can they be so different?

Two children may both receive an autism diagnosis while having very different speech, everyday skills and sensory needs.

One child might speak at length about favourite interests but find unexpected changes and noise difficult. Another may use little spoken language, communicate through gestures or pictures, enjoy familiar activities and need assistance with daily care.

These differences cannot be reduced to a single “severity” score. Each child may need different kinds of support in different areas.

Why? **Because autism is not one simple brain characteristic that appears in the same way in everyone.** The diagnosis groups people with certain neurodevelopmental characteristics, but superficially similar features may arise alongside different combinations of genetic, cellular and neural-network processes.

The World Health Organization describes autism as a **diverse group of conditions related to brain development** and notes that autistic people's abilities and needs may change over time ([WHO, 2025](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/autism-spectrum-disorders-(asd))).

This article explores why characteristics differ, what scientists have found about the underlying biology and how this knowledge can guide individualised support without reducing someone to a fixed label.

## What does “spectrum” actually mean?

Autism is sometimes presented as a straight line, with “mild autism” at one end and “severe autism” at the other. This picture is too simple.

One person may communicate fluently in some situations but struggle significantly with daily tasks. Another may use little spoken language while showing strengths in visual search, memory or particular activities. A third person's most noticeable needs may involve sensory overload, anxiety and a predictable environment.

**An autistic person's profile is multidimensional.** Different areas need to be considered separately rather than replaced by one overall score.

### What can't one overall score tell us?

Questionnaires and standardised measures can be useful within a comprehensive assessment, but each measures particular characteristics.

Two people with similar questionnaire scores may have very different self-care skills, communication access needs or medical concerns.

Conversely, different scores do not necessarily indicate who will be happier, develop a particular skill or benefit from a specific form of support.

Clinical systems may describe the level of support a person currently needs. These descriptions can be informative, but **they do not replace a detailed individual assessment** or predict everything about the future.

## Which aspects of development may differ?

### Communication and speech

Some autistic people use extended spoken language. Others use short phrases, speak very little or do not use speech as their main means of communication.

**Not speaking does not mean a person lacks thoughts, emotions, understanding or a desire to communicate.** They may use gestures, writing, pictures or augmentative and alternative communication (AAC).

Producing speech, understanding speech, social communication and having a way to express a choice are different abilities and needs. They may require different support.

### Sensory experience

A person may avoid certain sounds, seek movement or respond differently to lighting and touch.

Sensory experience is also multidimensional. The same person may be highly responsive to some stimuli and less responsive to others. This is discussed in the article **“Why might sounds, light and touch feel different?”** (*a related English research translation has not yet been published*).

### Interests and repetitive actions

Interests can be focused, deep and meaningful. Repetitive movements or activities may help someone regulate themselves or simply be enjoyable. In other situations, particular repetitions may interfere with important activities or accompany distress.

What matters is not just how noticeable a behaviour is to other people, but **its function, safety and significance for the person**.

### Learning and attention

People differ in how quickly they learn skills, which tasks interest them, how they remember information, organise attention and need information explained.

Autism does not automatically imply intellectual disability or unusually high intelligence. Some autistic people also have an intellectual disability, while others do not. Individual strengths and support needs remain important in either case.

### Everyday independence

**Adaptive skills** are abilities used in daily life, such as dressing, eating, following steps, using transport or asking for help.

A person may need substantial assistance in one activity and little in another. Independence also depends on accessible communication, health, environmental safety and the demands of a particular situation.

## Why isn't the biology of autism the same for everyone?

Differences begin long before speech and daily behaviour can be described.

As explained in the earlier articles in this series, brain function depends on the interaction of many genes, molecular programmes, nerve cells and networks. **Different genetic changes may affect partly overlapping biological processes**, but not always in the same way.

### Common variants and some rare variants

Everyone carries many genetic variants. Individual common variants often have very small statistical effects. Certain rare variants can have a larger influence on development, although their effects vary and are not always predictable.

Variants can be inherited or arise for the first time. Therefore, the absence of other autistic people in a family does not rule out a genetic contribution.

Major studies by [Fu and colleagues, *Nature Genetics* (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-022-01104-0) and [Antaki and colleagues, *Nature Genetics* (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-022-01064-5) linked rare and common genomic variation with different aspects of neurodevelopment and autistic characteristics.

This **does not mean** that a single identified variant can predict a child's future speech, intellectual functioning or independence. Its consequences may depend on the particular variant, other genes and developmental factors.

### Different cellular processes

Some genetic factors help regulate gene activity. Others affect the development of certain neurons, synapses or communication between cells.

This helps explain why research discusses several biological mechanisms with varying levels of evidence across people.

But the opposite oversimplification must also be avoided: **a different pattern of behaviour does not prove that every person has a completely separate biological “type of autism”.** Development involves interacting processes that are still not fully understood.

## What did the 2022 study of genetic heterogeneity show?

[Warrier and colleagues, *Nature Genetics* (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-022-01072-5) combined information about genetic variants and different characteristics of autistic participants.

Rather than treating every autistic characteristic as a single dimension, they examined separate aspects of communication, repetitive characteristics and co-occurring development.

The study found that certain **common genetic variants were statistically associated with particular dimensions of phenotype**, while the patterns for other types of genetic variation could differ.

**What this means:** genetic contributions are not identical across all observable autistic characteristics, and detailed phenotypic descriptions matter in research.

**What it does not mean:** a commercial genetic test can accurately predict one child's future personality, speech, behaviour or support needs. The study examined group-level statistical associations, not reliable individual predictions.

## What do the four statistical groups from the 2025 study mean?

**The four groups are not four official diagnostic types of autism.**

[Litman and colleagues, *Nature Genetics* (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-025-02224-z) analysed detailed questionnaires and genetic data from **5,392 children in the SPARK research cohort**.

They applied a statistical model that grouped participants according to combinations of many measured characteristics. The selected model identified **four groups** with differing patterns of communication, behaviour, developmental delay and co-occurring difficulties.

The groups also showed differences in genetic patterns and the activity of particular biological programmes. Parts of the findings were examined in an independent dataset.

Comparing **whole profiles** with genetic factors is a valuable research approach. But clustering depends on the measurements and mathematical model and does not, on its own, demonstrate fixed natural subtypes.

Important limitations include:

- The number of groups depends on which characteristics were measured, participants' ages, questionnaire reporting and the chosen algorithm.
- Statistical group boundaries need not correspond to sharp biological divisions between people.
- The study did not establish four new official medical diagnoses.
- The model has not been shown to predict an individual child's future or identify an effective intervention.
- Results cannot automatically be generalised to every age group or country.

**Conclusion:** the study found measurable statistical and biological components of autism's heterogeneity. “Scientists have conclusively proved four types of autism” would be an overstatement.

## Why is genetics not the only source of differences?

Two questions should be kept separate: **“Which biological factors contribute to autism?”** and **“Why is this person facing a particular difficulty or needing support in this situation?”**

A person's current profile is shaped not only by neurodevelopmental processes but also by health, accessible communication methods, environmental demands, age, acquired skills and experience.

For example, someone who is very sensitive to noise may manage daily tasks much better in a quiet, predictable environment. Their autistic characteristics have not disappeared. The activity has become more accessible.

Similarly, an appropriate AAC system may allow a child to express needs more effectively. This **does not prove that their neurodevelopmental condition was “cured”**. It shows that part of the communication barrier was related to access to a suitable tool.

## Why do co-occurring conditions matter so much?

Autistic people can also experience other medical or mental health conditions. Some substantially affect sleep, behaviour, concentration, comfort and independence.

These include **ADHD, anxiety disorders, epilepsy, sleep problems, gastrointestinal conditions, motor coordination difficulties and intellectual disability**. They do not occur in everyone and are not mandatory “parts of autism”.

In a large systematic review, [Micai and colleagues (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2023.105436) examined **340 publications involving approximately 590,000 participants**. They found considerable variation in the reported prevalence of co-occurring conditions depending on age, sample characteristics and the condition assessed.

For families, this means that **new changes in behaviour should not automatically be attributed to autism**. Increased irritability, for example, may be related to pain, poor sleep or another issue requiring attention.

[NICE CG128](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg128/chapter/recommendations) recommends considering co-occurring conditions during assessment, including ADHD, anxiety, hearing and vision problems, epilepsy and sleep disturbances.

### Example: epilepsy

Epilepsy occurs in some autistic people, but **it is not a required characteristic of autism**. Estimates vary markedly between populations and age groups in [systematic reviews and meta-analyses](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34510916/).

Not every repetitive movement is an epileptic seizure. Conversely, possible episodes of unresponsiveness or unusual sudden events should not be dismissed as autism without medical assessment.

## Why can development change significantly with age?

Parents sometimes hear claims such as “if a child has not begun speaking by a certain age, nothing will change” or “development is largely over after preschool”. These are unreliable generalisations.

**Development continues throughout childhood, adolescence and adulthood.** Communication, daily-living skills, self-regulation, interests and support needs can all change.

Development is not necessarily even across areas. One skill may improve quickly while another changes more slowly. Moving school, illness or increased demands may temporarily make some tasks harder.

### What did a longitudinal study of 406 children find?

[Chen and colleagues, *JCPP Advances* (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcv2.12212) followed **406 children** diagnosed with autism between ages 2 and 5, measuring adaptive functioning repeatedly into adolescence.

They identified several **statistical trajectories**, which differed in starting skill levels and in patterns of change with age. Communication, everyday skills and social adaptation did not all follow the same course.

**Main finding:** adaptive development is heterogeneous, and meaningful changes can occur at different ages.

**What it cannot tell us:** that a two-year-old can be permanently assigned to one of four trajectories or that their later life can be predicted precisely. These trajectories were produced by statistical analysis of a particular sample, not inevitable life paths.

## Why isn't the need for support fixed?

Imagine a teenager who completes homework independently in a calm environment. At a new school with noisy corridors, an unfamiliar timetable and greater social demands, they may need much more help.

This does not necessarily mean autism has become worse. **The demands of the environment and their fit with the person's available resources have changed.**

Similar shifts can happen in adulthood, during a move, when starting work, taking on new responsibilities or becoming unwell.

A better question is *“What support does this person need for this activity and in these conditions?”*, rather than trying to assign one permanent measure of independence.

[NICE CG170](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations) emphasises individualised support informed by changing needs, physical and mental health, environmental factors and life transitions.

## Why is autism sometimes recognised later in girls and women?

Differences in presentation also affect **which characteristics other people and professionals notice**.

A girl may need predictability, become exhausted by social interaction or experience sensory difficulties without these needs being readily recognised. Some people learn to imitate social patterns deliberately or conceal discomfort.

This is sometimes called **masking or camouflaging**. It is incorrect to assume that every autistic girl masks, or that boys never do. Masking occurs among people of different genders and ages and is difficult to measure accurately.

[NICE CG128](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg128/chapter/recommendations) warns that autism may be under-recognised in girls and that coping strategies and supportive environments can conceal difficulties.

A [2024 systematic review and meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rasd.2024.102492) found statistical associations between greater camouflaging and higher anxiety, depressive symptoms and poorer wellbeing. Observational evidence cannot establish that camouflaging itself was always the cause of these outcomes.

Appearing to interact successfully does not rule out support needs. Quiet behaviour does not mean social interaction requires little effort.

## How can location and access to support affect what we observe?

Living conditions can influence **when differences are recognised, when a diagnosis is made and which forms of support become available**.

If a region has few professionals familiar with diverse presentations of autism, some people may receive a diagnosis much later. If a child lacks an accessible communication system, people may underestimate their abilities.

This is particularly relevant to Kazakhstan and Central Asia. Much autism genetic research draws predominantly on cohorts from selected countries and populations. **The findings do not necessarily generalise across ethnic backgrounds and healthcare systems.**

Two distinct points matter:

- **Biological factors involved in autism's development** should not be attributed to parenting, culture or a lack of services.
- **How characteristics are identified, described and supported** can depend on language, culture, access to assessment and the environment.

This is not a contradiction. It is the difference between the origins of a neurodevelopmental condition and the circumstances of a person's daily life.

## Can autism be divided into biological subtypes with specific treatments?

Identifying more precise biological subgroups is an active area of research.

If scientists can find groups with reproducible biological mechanisms, this may help them study particular genetic conditions and medical needs.

Moving from a research cluster to a clinically useful category, however, requires evidence that it:

1. Can be identified reliably in independent and diverse cohorts.
2. Retains a meaningful interpretation across ages and circumstances.
3. Reflects biological mechanisms, not only questionnaire patterns.
4. Leads to decisions that produce a meaningful benefit for people.
5. Does not unjustifiably exclude those outside the group from support.

**The four groups found in one study are not four established types of autism.** There is no universal blood test, genetic test or MRI procedure that assigns every autistic person to validated biological categories with a matching individual treatment.

For some rare genetic conditions, clinicians may recommend targeted investigations for associated medical risks. This is not the same as being able to “treat one of four types of autism”.

## What can parents do in practice: a profile instead of a label

Scientific findings about heterogeneity are relevant beyond the laboratory. They help adults ask better questions.

Instead of “How severe is this child's autism?”, it may be more useful to ask: **“Which situations are easy or difficult for this child, how do they communicate, what causes discomfort and what support helps?”**

Five areas may help structure a conversation with professionals.

| Area | What to ask | What this may clarify |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Communication | How does the person express requests, preferences, refusal and discomfort? | Whether support is needed with speech, gestures, writing or AAC |
| Sensory environment | Which sounds, sensations and situations are pleasant or distressing? | Which environmental changes may reduce overload |
| Daily living | What can the person do independently, with prompts or with assistance? | Which goals are genuinely useful |
| Co-occurring health | Are there concerns about sleep, pain, anxiety, attention or other symptoms? | Whether a separate medical assessment is needed |
| Strengths and interests | What does the person enjoy and do well? | How activities can build on their interests |

This is **not a diagnostic test**. It is a way to gather questions for families and professionals rather than assign scores.

Returning to these questions over time is useful. A description from two years ago may not reflect a person's current communication, skills and needs.

## Why might children with the same diagnosis need different programmes?

Even if clinicians use the same diagnostic term, support goals may differ.

One child may most need AAC to express their needs. Another may need help with sleep and sensory overload. A third may benefit from support with daily activities or a more predictable school timetable.

**Research into heterogeneity does not support one universal programme that suits every autistic person.**

This does not mean abandoning evidence-informed approaches. Good support uses reliable methods for **specific goals**, respects safety and the person's preferences, considers quality of life and measures meaningful outcomes.

A better measure of progress is whether someone can communicate more easily, take part in meaningful activities and feel more comfortable, not whether they can be described as “less autistic”.

## What is established, and what remains uncertain?

| Supported by research | Not established as a universal rule |
| --- | --- |
| Autistic people differ in skills, characteristics and needs. | Everyone fits on one severity scale. |
| Common and rare genetic variants contribute in different ways. | One variant completely predicts an individual's development. |
| Studies identify statistical groups with differing profiles and genetic correlates. | Algorithm-defined groups are already established biological types. |
| Co-occurring conditions can affect health and everyday life. | Sleep problems, anxiety or epilepsy are mandatory features of autism. |
| Skills and needs can change with age and living circumstances. | An early score or short assessment can permanently predict independence. |
| Different people need different combinations of support. | One programme or set of exercises works for everyone. |

## Questions about differences between people

### If two children are autistic, why might one speak and the other not?

Communication develops differently and involves several processes. Speech, language comprehension, motor and cognitive abilities, co-occurring conditions and access to support may differ. Not using spoken language does not mean an inability to communicate or understand.

### Does “spectrum” mean autism moves from mild to severe over time?

No. Support needs may change, but differences are multidimensional. Someone may need substantial help in one area and be independent in another.

### Can two autistic siblings be very different?

Yes. Even children in the same family do not have identical combinations of genetic variants, experiences and medical characteristics. Shared familial influences do not mean identical development or skills.

### If a child communicates more and becomes more independent, has their autism gone away?

Improved skills and fewer barriers do not automatically mean autism has disappeared. The practical focus should be meaningful progress and comfort rather than trying to declare a diagnosis “cancelled” after one change.

### Did the 2025 study really discover four types of autism?

The researchers identified four statistical clusters in a particular dataset and associated genetic patterns. This is an important research finding, but not the establishment of four new official diagnostic categories.

### If my child does not have epilepsy, anxiety or intellectual difficulties, can an autism diagnosis still be appropriate?

Yes. Co-occurring conditions affect only some autistic people. They are not required diagnostic criteria. Assessment relies on established neurodevelopmental characteristics and developmental history.

### Why should professionals ask about strengths as well as difficulties?

Strengths, interests and preferences help identify meaningful ways to communicate, learn and participate. They do not erase real difficulties, but they contribute to a more accurate individual profile.

## The main conclusion about differences in autism

**Autism differs between people because nervous-system development has many dimensions, and genetic and biological contributions are heterogeneous.** These interact with health conditions, age, acquired skills and environmental accessibility.

Research can now identify some statistical clusters and genetic programmes, but there is not enough evidence for a final classification of biological autism types or for precise predictions about one child's future.

For families, the most useful conclusion is to focus not on a permanent label but on **the person's communication, strengths, difficulties, health and support needs in specific circumstances**. Support should fit the person, not an average description of a diagnosis.
