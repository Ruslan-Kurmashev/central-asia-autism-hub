A child is trying to open a box of toys, but the lid will not move. An adult notices the difficulty and offers help. The child may ask using a word, gesture, pointing to the box or selecting a message on an augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) system. The adult responds and helps open the box.

This is one everyday opportunity to support communication. Naturalistic Intervention (NI) involves learning specific skills within familiar situations where they are useful, such as play, dressing, walking, meals or interaction with others. It is not one standardised programme, and it does not mean teaching every minute of the day.

## How naturalistic learning differs from ordinary play

Free play has value in its own right. A child may play alone, choose activities and communicate without an educational target. In a naturalistic learning opportunity, the adult or professional also identifies which skill is being supported, when it is relevant and how to know if it is used in everyday life.

For example, during railway play, the goal might be for the child to request a carriage, invite someone to join or say that they want to stop. Any accessible communication method is valid.

Simply following an adult's instruction should not be the only measure of success. What matters is whether the child can initiate interaction, express preferences or refuse. Not every action has to become a question, exercise or request to imitate.

## An example: a box of toys

Consider a sequence in which a child needs help with an ordinary item. It illustrates the principles but is not a universal session protocol.

![Five successive scenes with the same mother and child. The child cannot open a toy box, the adult notices, the child asks for help, the mother opens the box and later helps with a jacket fastening.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/diagrams/naturalistic-help-five-steps-approved.webp)

1. **An everyday difficulty arises.** The child wants a toy inside a box but cannot open it. An adult is nearby.
2. **The adult notices.** They offer help and give the child an opportunity to communicate the need without requiring eye contact.
3. **The child asks for help.** This may be a word, gesture, pointing, a card or AAC. If asking is difficult, the adult helps without coercion.
4. **Help is given.** The adult opens the box or opens it together with the child. The request has a meaningful outcome, but assistance does not depend on saying the "correct" word.
5. **The skill is tried elsewhere.** The child may need help with a fastener while getting dressed. The adult observes whether they can ask independently. One episode does not establish stable generalisation.

The fifth scene illustrates another opportunity to observe requesting help. It does not show that a skill has been mastered after one successful attempt.

If the child has already asked for help in an accessible way, there is no reason to withhold it to obtain another word, gesture or card. Water, toilet access, necessary rest and AAC remain available regardless of task performance. Essential assistance must not be deliberately withheld to create a teaching opportunity.

## Skills that may be supported in everyday life

Naturalistic approaches can be used for different functional goals. The choice depends on the child's age, abilities, interests and needs. For example:

- During play, a child may request help or select a toy. The useful outcome is being able to communicate a choice accessibly.
- While dressing, they might request assistance with a fastening when needed.
- During a walk, they might indicate where they want to go or ask for a break.
- During a shared activity, they might initiate another turn or finish using speech, gesture or AAC.

"Improve communication" is too broad to assess. A more specific example is "during a favourite activity, the child independently communicates 'more', 'stop' or 'help' in an accessible way". This is an example of goal wording, not a universal plan.

There is no need to create difficulties intentionally if they cause distress. Teaching opportunities should arise within ordinary situations, not through withholding necessary help.

## Child interests, prompts and AAC

If a child enjoys trains, music, letters or water play, these interests can provide a meaningful setting for learning. An adult may join the activity, offer choices, demonstrate an action or allow time for independent initiation.

Prompts can sometimes help, such as showing an action, pointing to a symbol or modelling a message on AAC. However, distinguish a child's independent initiative from a response that occurs only after another reminder. Some prompts may be reduced as a skill develops.

Permanent supports should not automatically be withdrawn. AAC, a visual schedule or a clear checklist may remain useful tools for independence. AAC communication is not inferior to speech and does not require prior spoken-language skills.

See also [AAC](/kz/en/parents/aac-communication-guide/) and the article on prompting and fading prompts in the relevant language section.

## Research and recommendations

Hume and colleagues (2021) classified Naturalistic Intervention as a focused practice that met the NCAEP evidence criteria. The review identified 75 publications, comprising 37 group studies and 38 single-case studies, published from 1990 to 2017.

These figures describe how much research was available and its designs. They do not mean 75 studies tested one identical programme or produce a single effect size for all children, goals and settings. Outcomes must be considered for the specific intervention.

The AFIRM practical guide (2017) explains how to choose a target, identify everyday opportunities, use appropriate strategies and observe progress. It describes an earlier evidence base that should not be added to the NCAEP study count as though it were independent.

NICE CG170 recommends considering specialist social-communication interventions using play-based strategies to support joint attention, engagement and reciprocal communication. These should fit the child's development and involve trained professionals, with parents, educators or peers participating as appropriate. NICE does not establish the effectiveness of every intervention just because it is called "naturalistic" or "play-based".

Naturalistic Intervention and naturalistic developmental behavioural interventions (NDBI) share some principles but are not interchangeable terms. [Read about NDBI](/kz/en/parents/ndbi-naturalistic-developmental-behavioral-interventions/).

## How to tell whether a skill helps in everyday life

Do not assess only performance in a professional's office. It is more useful to ask:

- Does the child use the skill independently or only when prompted?
- Is it used in ordinary activities rather than with one specific toy?
- Does it work with different people and in different places?
- Does it benefit the child in a practical way?
- Can the child still refuse, rest and choose another communication method?

For instance, asking for help only after a professional's reminder differs from asking independently at home. Both can be recorded, but they are not the same outcome.

One successful event does not prove that a skill has been established. Monitor it over time in several suitable situations without creating unnecessary family demands.

## The child's initiative and the limits of teaching during play

The child may decide when they need help and when they would rather play independently. Adults should follow the child's initiatives instead of turning every interaction into a test.

## Respecting boundaries and family capacity

Adults should respond to the child's accessible communication, including refusal, requests for breaks and wishes to play alone. Forced eye contact, holding the child's attention or continually repeating tasks should not be goals merely to obtain behaviour that looks "typical".

Parents can use selected opportunities within activities they already do. They should not become full-time therapists. Free play, rest and ordinary family relationships matter just as much, and play without learning tasks has value of its own.

If every meal, outing or game turns into a skill test, discuss with the responsible professional whether the number of teaching opportunities or the goals can be reduced.

## When to reconsider the approach and questions for a professional

Reconsider the approach if goals are unclear, the child has little choice, refusals are repeatedly ignored, support causes marked distress, success occurs only after ongoing prompts or the skill does not transfer to everyday life. Lack of progress should not be blamed on the child for not trying hard enough.

Six questions can guide a discussion:

1. Which specific skill are we targeting, and for what purpose?
2. Why does this goal matter to the child, and how are their interests considered?
3. What prompts are used, and which ongoing supports will remain?
4. How can the child refuse, take a break or obtain essential help?
5. How is independent use distinguished from prompted performance, and how is generalisation checked?
6. When will the plan be reviewed, and what demands does it place on the family?
