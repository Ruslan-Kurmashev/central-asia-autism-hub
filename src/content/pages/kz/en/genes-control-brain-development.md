## How do genes help the brain develop?

A child's brain begins forming before birth. Billions of nerve cells appear and gradually develop, migrate and establish connections with one another.

How do cells acquire different properties and carry out their tasks? Genes are involved in these processes.

The illustration below shows a simplified way that genetic information can be used by nerve cells.

![Simplified educational diagram: information in DNA is transcribed into RNA and, for many genes, used to produce proteins that support cellular functions. Not all genes encode proteins.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/research/gene-expression-brain-development-en-2026-11-19.svg)

*This illustrates one major pathway of genetic information use. Some genes produce functional RNA without subsequent protein production.*

Genes contain inherited information needed for the body to develop and function. They help cells make molecules required for growth, signalling and interactions with other cells.

Genes do not, however, control development like a predetermined programme specifying a child's future speech, behaviour and abilities.

Brain development is much more complex. It depends on the activity of many genes, interactions among cells and other biological processes.

To understand this, we can begin with a question: why do cells in the same person perform such different functions?

## Why do cells with nearly identical DNA perform different tasks?

Most cells in one person's body contain almost the same DNA, but their functions differ considerably.

Skin cells help protect the body from its environment. Nerve cells receive and transmit signals, allowing the brain to process information and regulate different functions.

Why are they so different if their genetic information is almost identical?

One reason is that different cells use different genes.

Imagine a large recipe book. Each cell holds nearly the same book, but cells rely on different instructions to perform their specific functions.

Some instructions contribute to the development of nerve cells. Others support the work of muscles or skin.

Of course, cells do not consciously read instructions or choose what to do. Their activity is regulated by complex biological processes.

Nevertheless, the analogy illustrates a central idea. Brain development depends not only on which genes are present, but also on how cells use their information.

## What does it mean when a gene is "switched on" or "off"?

Scientific articles often use expressions such as "gene activity", "gene activation" and "gene expression".

They refer to processes by which cells use information from particular genes.

Consider a simple example.

A neuron needs specialised proteins to transmit signals. Proteins are molecules that perform many functions. Some maintain cell structure, others support signalling or regulate additional molecules.

For many proteins, the cell uses information encoded in DNA.

A simplified pathway is:

**DNA → RNA → protein → cell function**

First, information from a DNA region is used to make an RNA molecule, rather like a working copy of an instruction.

The cell can then use that information to produce a protein.

The DNA itself is not consumed in the process. It remains in the cell.

When researchers report that a gene has become more active, they usually mean its information is being used more intensively, for example through increased production of the corresponding RNA.

Reduced gene activity does not necessarily mean that the gene has stopped functioning altogether.

### Why does this matter for brain development?

At different stages of nervous-system development, cells need different proteins.

At some stages, they divide actively. Later, they acquire specialised properties and form connections.

Thus normal development depends not only on which genes are present, but also on when and to what extent they are expressed.

Changes in specific regulatory processes can influence nervous-system development.

That does not mean that every change in gene activity causes a disorder or autism.

## What is epigenetics in simple terms?

Epigenetics studies processes that help regulate gene activity without changing the underlying DNA sequence.

Return to the recipe-book example. Imagine adding bookmarks to particular pages. Some instructions become easier to locate, while others are used less often.

Cells have biological mechanisms that partly resemble this arrangement.

For example, small chemical groups can attach to parts of DNA and influence how actively genetic information is used.

Other mechanisms relate to how DNA is packaged within a cell and how accessible particular regions are.

All these processes contribute to ordinary development.

### Can the environment affect gene activity?

Some environmental conditions can influence biological processes, including gene regulation.

But we need to avoid jumping to conclusions.

If researchers find an association between an exposure and a change in gene expression, this does not demonstrate that the exposure causes autism.

It is particularly incorrect to attribute autism to a lack of parental affection, inappropriate upbringing or ordinary family stress. Epigenetics does not substantiate those explanations.

Changes in gene activity are not necessarily harmful. Many are normal features of development.

## How might gene regulation be related to autism?

Research indicates that some genes associated with autism participate in the development of nerve cells.

Some help control cell growth, others participate in the formation of contacts between neurons, and others regulate the activity of many additional genes.

This does not imply that everyone who is autistic has the same disrupted biological process.

One gene under investigation is **CHD8**.

It helps regulate other genes and is involved in nervous-system development.

Certain rare changes affecting CHD8 can have substantial consequences for development and may be associated with particular neurodevelopmental characteristics, including autism.

In [laboratory studies](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11325375/), researchers investigate what happens to developing nerve cells when CHD8 function changes. They have found changes in certain cellular processes.

These findings cannot automatically be applied to every autistic child.

Most autistic people do not have an identified clinically significant change in CHD8. Moreover, a cell experiment cannot predict how a particular child will speak, communicate or develop skills.

Researchers therefore continue to investigate many genes and processes rather than look for a single mechanism explaining the entire autism spectrum.

## How do researchers investigate gene activity?

Researchers use specialised laboratory techniques to examine which genes are active in cells.

For instance, they can measure the amounts of RNA produced. This helps indicate which genes were more or less active in the sample being studied.

Scientists compare different brain-cell types and examine changes across developmental stages.

Sometimes nerve cells are grown in the laboratory.

More complex models called **organoids** are small three-dimensional structures grown from cells that reproduce certain aspects of developing nervous tissue.

They allow researchers to study processes that would be difficult to observe directly in a developing human brain.

An organoid, however, is not a complete brain. It cannot reproduce the full range of structures, connections and interactions with the rest of the body.

### Why do different studies sometimes disagree?

Imagine one team studying blood cells and another examining neurons.

Even if they investigate the same gene, their results may differ.

Gene activity depends on cell type, age and experimental conditions. An observed difference also does not necessarily cause autism.

Researchers therefore try to replicate findings independently and establish the biological meaning of changes they detect.

## Can autism be identified from gene expression?

There is currently no universal test of gene expression or epigenetics that can, by itself, confirm or exclude autism.

Some studies have found differences in the activity of certain genes between research groups.

These findings are not yet sufficient to provide a universal diagnostic tool.

A blood test, for instance, can measure gene expression in blood cells. That does not directly establish how neurons in a person's brain developed or function.

Before a new clinical test can be used, researchers must demonstrate reliable accuracy across relevant populations and show that its results improve medical decisions.

Commercial offers to determine an "epigenetic cause of autism" or select a universal treatment from such testing should therefore be treated with particular caution.

Autism assessment relies on developmental history, communication and behaviour rather than one laboratory measurement.

## What does this mean for parents?

Knowledge of genes helps explain the complexity of brain development.

It also shows why autistic children can have different characteristics, and why one genetic result cannot determine everything about a person's development.

Gene research does not yet allow us to predict a particular child's future abilities precisely or choose their individual support programme.

If a child has an identified genetic condition, a clinician can explain whether this has implications for additional medical follow-up.

When a specific genetic cause cannot be identified, that does not prevent families from addressing the child's development and health needs.

It is more useful for families to understand how their child communicates, which conditions help them learn, what creates difficulties and which support is needed.

Research into genes continues and contributes to understanding autism biology.

However, a long period often separates a laboratory discovery from a method that can be used safely and effectively in medical practice.

New claims about "switching on the right genes", "correcting epigenetics" or "removing autism at the DNA level" should therefore be assessed against rigorous clinical evidence rather than the persuasiveness of advertising.
