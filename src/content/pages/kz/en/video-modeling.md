## What are modelling and video modelling?

A child may find it easier to learn an action after seeing how it is performed. Modelling means teaching by demonstrating an example. An adult can show how to fold a towel, then invite the child to try. If the example is recorded, this approach is called video modelling.

This is not a distinct autism therapy. The method is used to teach specific actions that are meaningful to the person. Watching a recording does not mean that a skill has been acquired. What matters is whether the action becomes more accessible in everyday life.

## Main formats of learning through modelling

| Format | How the example is presented |
| --- | --- |
| Live modelling | An adult demonstrates an action directly. |
| Video modelling | The child watches a recording of another person performing the entire action, then may try it. |
| Video self-modelling | The child watches a recording of their own successful performance. |
| Point-of-view video | The camera shows hands and objects approximately from the perspective of the person performing the action. |
| Video prompting | The person watches and performs a single step, then watches the next step if necessary. |

Camera perspective and presentation method can be combined. For example, individual steps can be filmed from a first-person perspective. In video self-modelling, an action entirely guided by an adult must not be presented as independent performance.

## How to choose a suitable format

If the child is comfortable watching an adult and repeating a simple action, start with live modelling. It does not require making a recording or using a screen. If an action is difficult to demonstrate repeatedly or it is useful to examine a movement closely, consider video. For a complex sequence, present individual steps if this format is clearer.

Notice whether the child can distinguish the relevant objects and movements on screen. If video distracts, causes discomfort or is inconvenient in a particular situation, try live modelling, static pictures or environmental adaptation. Do not assume that all autistic people learn better from video.

In the [study by Benson and colleagues (2024)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38691580/), video and static pictures were compared in 17 participants aged 9-18. Improvements were observed with both forms of support. This supports individual choice rather than a compulsory switch to video.

## What skills can be taught?

| Skill | Example recording content |
| --- | --- |
| Setting a table | Place a spoon beside an unbreakable plate. |
| Dressing | Demonstrate fastening the zip on an adult's jacket. |
| Organising belongings | Put toys in an accessible container. |
| Handwashing | Show the usual sequence with water at a safe temperature. |
| Communication | Show how to ask for help or a break using an accessible method. |
| Getting ready for school | Place necessary items in a schoolbag. |
| Work skills | Sort safe materials or organise a workspace. |

Choose an action that genuinely helps the child participate in family life, learn, communicate or enjoy a preferred activity. Do not use video to impose eye contact, suppress safe stimming or teach masking of autistic traits.

A recording does not replace a safety assessment. Teaching actions involving hot objects, knives, electrical appliances or road environments requires appropriate supervision and professional assessment where necessary.

## How to prepare an instructional video at home

1. Choose one specific goal. For example, fold a small towel in a familiar, comfortable way.
2. Check which actions the child can already perform and what creates a barrier. Change inconvenient conditions before filming.
3. Break the task down into necessary steps. Decide whether one recording or several short clips will be easier to use.
4. Record the example on a phone. For many everyday tasks, an adult's hands, a stable camera and good lighting are sufficient. Show the objects and outcome without unnecessary noise or abrupt transitions.
5. Check the recording before presenting it. Actions should be distinguishable, words clear and objects similar to those the child will use. Accurate captions or a text alternative may be added for important spoken information.
6. Offer the opportunity to watch and try. Assess whether the recording helped rather than counting views.

There is no universal video duration. It should be short enough for the specific action without omitting important steps. Professional editing, music or filming the child is not required.

General planning and monitoring principles are described in [AFIRM methodological materials](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED605910). The examples in this article are original AutismHub educational scenarios, not reproductions of research protocols.

## Practical examples

### Fold a towel

An adult places a small towel on an accessible table, smooths it out, folds it in half and puts it on a shelf. Record the actions so that the hands and fabric edges do not obscure one another. If the child finds it difficult to remember the sequence, offer a recording of an individual step. Allow another comfortable way of folding. Do not correct every movement unnecessarily.

### Pack a schoolbag

Prepare familiar items and show how to put them in the bag. If the child already uses a checklist of necessary items, retain it. Later, assess whether the recording helps the child pack in their usual morning setting. The goal is an accessible way to prepare, not exact imitation of an adult's posture.

### Ask for help

An adult can demonstrate how to ask for help with an object using a gesture, word or their own augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) system. The child may communicate a need in any understandable way. Do not guide the child's hand to select a message or withhold a needed object, water, food or assistance to obtain imitation of the example.

## How to organise viewing and learning

Offer the recording in a suitable setting where objects and familiar communication aids are available. Allow the child to stop the video, repeat a clip or decline to watch. Do not require them to sit still, look into the model's eyes or watch the recording to the end.

After the demonstration, provide an opportunity to perform the action. If assistance is needed, use an accessible, unobtrusive prompt. Help with one difficult step while leaving other steps available for independent performance. If the child refuses or experiences discomfort, stop the teaching attempt and establish what is creating a barrier. Do not punish mistakes.

If video has become a convenient permanent instruction, do not automatically remove it. Independently accessing the video without an additional adult reminder may count as independent performance with an adaptation. Further reading: [prompting and prompt fading (in Russian)](/kz/ru/parents/prompting-and-prompt-fading/).

## Child consent and recording safety

For home learning, the preferred option is to record an adult's actions, hands or objects without showing the child's face or publishing the recording. If the child or another participant needs to appear, establish beforehand who is recording, why, where it will be stored, who has access and when it will be deleted. Comply with applicable permission requirements and consider the child's accessibly expressed consent or refusal. Refusal to be filmed is not a problem to be corrected.

Do not record intimate procedures, including toileting, bathing and changing clothes. Do not share identifiable recordings in group chats or social media without necessary permissions and a separate privacy assessment. Check automatic synchronisation and cloud storage access settings. Do not upload recordings of the child to third-party AI services for skill analysis. If consent is withdrawn, stop using the recording and delete copies under your control unless other mandatory lawful grounds require retention.

The Campbell map describes studies of educational outcomes. It does not establish the legality of filming or the safety of all video programmes. Consent, comfort and privacy are assessed separately.

## How to assess outcomes, generalisation and maintenance

Before starting, define the observable action and usual permanent adaptations. Compare similar situations before and after introducing video. Note who started the recording, whether additional assistance was provided, what the child could do and whether they wanted to continue. Do not create a required number of attempts for the table.

| What to observe | Example entry |
| --- | --- |
| Date and situation | 12:00, familiar towel at home. |
| Video and assistance | Adult started video and pointed to the edge of the fabric once. |
| Outcome | Folded the towel after a gesture. |
| Comfort | Asked to finish after the action. |
| Generalisation | Still needs help with a different towel. |

This is a fictional example, not a developmental benchmark. If the child independently starts an accessible video instruction and performs the action without an adult, record this separately from performance after an adult prompt. If an opportunity did not occur, do not count it as an unsuccessful attempt.

Check whether the skill remains available over time and whether it is accessible with small changes in the setting. Do so in natural situations without depriving the child of necessary support. Generalisation and maintenance are not guaranteed by one successful performance.

If video does not help the child notice important steps, more prompts are required or the child refuses, reconsider the format and conditions. Seek appropriate professional advice for pain or significant new difficulties.

## What does the research say?

In the [review by Hume and colleagues (2021)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8510990/), video modelling is included among 28 practices meeting the authors' criteria. It lists 97 studies from 1990-2017, 95 single-case and 2 group studies. This does not mean video is suitable for every goal.

The [map by McConnell and colleagues (2024)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38707947/) covered 438 studies of video-based interventions for autistic children and adolescents aged 3-18. The search ended in May 2021. A single-case design was used in 394 studies. The chart presents the number of studies by format.

![Number of studies: video modelling 273, video self-modelling 82, point-of-view video 61, video prompting 57, video feedback 12, computer-based video instruction 4. Categories overlap.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/diagrams/video-modeling-evidence-map-en.svg)

Number of studies: 273 - video modelling, 82 - video self-modelling, 61 - point-of-view video, 57 - video prompting, 12 - video feedback, 4 - computer-based video instruction.

Data source: McConnell et al., Campbell Systematic Reviews (2024), [DOI 10.1002/cl2.1405](https://doi.org/10.1002/cl2.1405). A study could assess several formats, so the numbers do not add up to 438. The chart reflects the volume of research, not comparative effectiveness. The map contains little evidence on safety and community participation, and samples are uneven across sex, ages and countries.

### Daily living and work skills

[Hong and colleagues (2016)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27442687/) synthesised 23 single-case studies on functional skills and reported a positive overall effect. [Wertalik and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1177/01626434241298955) reviewed point-of-view video prompting for daily living skills in a review published online in 2024. [Moustaka and colleagues (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1080/20473869.2025.2533936) synthesised 13 studies involving young children and noted incomplete data on session duration and frequency. These findings do not establish a single programme for families.

The [meta-analysis by Bross and colleagues (2021)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36601641/) included 20 publications on work skills, of which 11 met the selected quality threshold. The overall Tau-U value for these studies was 0.91. Tau-U describes an effect across repeated measurements, not the percentage of people who acquired a skill. Most studies took place in educational settings, limiting generalisation to ordinary workplaces.

The [randomised study by Zohrabi and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41389166/), published online in December 2025, compared smartphone video with live modelling in 31 children. Parents received remote specialist support. The available information does not allow the effect of watching video alone to be isolated or an advantage of unsupported home video to be promised.

### Communication and AAC

The [review by Mohanan and colleagues (2025)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40279535/) included 17 studies involving 47 children, in which video was combined with other components of conversational skills instruction. In the [study by Thirumanickam and colleagues (2018)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30456987/), involving four adolescents who used AAC, the independent effects of video techniques were limited. The effect of the entire programme cannot therefore be attributed to the recording alone. Communication goals should be meaningful to the person rather than aiming for outward resemblance to neurotypical behaviour.

### Can a best format be selected?

The [review by Shaikh and Karkhanis More (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1177/09731342261496607) synthesised 19 single-case studies with 66 participants. In the comparative analysis, point-of-view video and self-modelling had higher values than peer modelling. However, the groups were unequal, with just three participants in the self-modelling group. The authors note the absence of independent double screening and limitations of the statistical measure PND. These findings do not establish superiority of a format for every child or task.
