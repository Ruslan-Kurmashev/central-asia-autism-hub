## What are prompts and why are they used?

A prompt is additional assistance that makes a particular action clearer or more accessible. Prompt fading means reducing assistance that is no longer necessary as the skill is acquired.

For example, a child is learning to put a light, unbreakable cup on an accessible table after drinking. In an everyday situation, the drink is finished and the cup is in front of the child. This is a natural cue for action if the routine is already understood. An adult can additionally point to where the cup belongs or demonstrate the movement.

Not every ordinary request is an unnecessary prompt. If the goal is for the child to respond to a request, the request itself may be the natural cue. If the goal is independent initiation, a repeated adult reminder counts as additional assistance. These goals should be distinguished.

## Main types of prompts

| Type | Example | How to reduce unnecessary assistance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Verbal | Remind the child where an object belongs | Allow time to respond, repeat the reminder less often. |
| Gestural | Point to the correct place | Reduce the additional gesture if the location is already understood. |
| Visual | Show an image of the next action | Remove only redundant support. |
| Modelling | Demonstrate how to fold a towel | Demonstrate an acquired action less often. |
| Video prompting | Show a single step on video | Reduce the need to watch if this is comfortable for the person. |
| Physical assistance | Support an object with the child's consent | Reduce only unnecessary assistance while maintaining safety. |

The table does not establish a hierarchy. One child may understand a picture more readily, another a short phrase or demonstration. Sometimes changing an object or the environment is more useful than an additional instruction.

## How to choose suitable assistance

Start with one achievable task that is meaningful in the child's life. Consider their interests and ability to refuse. Check whether the action is understood, the object is easy to use, communication is accessible and pain, fatigue or sensory discomfort are absent.

Observe which parts of the skill the child already performs independently, which require support and where a barrier arises. Choose the most accessible, unobtrusive support that genuinely facilitates the action. If the child does not initiate a task, this does not always mean a more intensive prompt is needed. They may not understand the purpose, want a rest or prefer another way of completing it.

A specialist can systematically compare accessible prompt types and fading procedures. An example is described by [Cowan and colleagues (2022)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9177132/). SWEEPS is designed for professional selection of strategies, not independent diagnosis or mandatory home assessment of a child.

## Methods of prompt fading

### Most-to-least prompting

Initially provide sufficient assistance for the child to understand or perform an action comfortably. Then reduce additional assistance as the skill develops. For example, an adult demonstrates how to fold a towel and later only points to the fold.

Greater assistance does not necessarily mean physical contact. There is no need to begin with touch if a demonstration or environmental change is sufficient.

### Least-to-most prompting

In a suitable situation, allow the child an opportunity to perform the action independently. If help is needed, offer an understandable prompt. Change the type of support as necessary. If the child asks for help, do not make them wait to test independence.

Do not automatically intensify prompts after refusal or no response. First check whether the task is accessible and whether the child wants to continue.

### Simultaneous prompting and teaching with fewer errors

In some teaching procedures, an additional prompt is provided immediately after the instructional cue to reduce errors. Independent performance is assessed separately under suitable conditions. These procedures do not mean that children are not allowed to make mistakes or that every mistake must be corrected immediately.

The systematic review by [Brown and Cariveau (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10864-022-09481-6) indicates that different ways of organising prompts may produce comparable rates of acquisition of a learning task. This does not establish a single best arrangement for practice at home.

## Time delay

Time delay is a pause between a natural or instructional cue and an additional prompt. It provides an opportunity to process information and initiate an action independently.

Constant time delay uses the same pause within a selected teaching procedure. Progressive time delay changes the pause as the skill develops.

There is no universal rule to wait a particular number of seconds. Timing depends on the person's communication method, motor abilities, usual processing speed and the task itself. If assistance is needed for safety, hygiene or basic needs, do not delay it for teaching purposes. The pause must not become a demand for a correct response at any cost.

## Practical everyday examples

### Put a cup away after drinking

Choose a light, unbreakable cup and an accessible location without hot or sharp objects. Initially, you may demonstrate the action. In another suitable situation, point to the location if this is sufficient. When the child initiates the action independently, allow them to finish without unnecessary commentary.

If the child becomes confused or asks for help when the prompt is reduced, restore accessible support. This sequence is illustrative and not a mandatory training schedule.

### Fasten a jacket

The child can already put on a jacket but finds it difficult to join the two parts of the zip. Assist only with that step if the child accepts the help. They can perform the other actions independently. More accessible fasteners or clothing may be a better permanent solution.

### Wash hands

The child may use a familiar sequence of pictures and complete the actions without verbal reminders. In this case, the visual support promotes independence. It does not have to be removed. If the difficulty relates to water temperature, an inconvenient tap or fear, change the conditions first.

### Ask for help or communicate a need

A child uses augmentative and alternative communication (AAC), but usually initiates communication after an adult asks a question. In everyday situations, create room for initiative, model familiar symbols on your own communication system or provide an accessible explanation. Any understandable form of communication should be acknowledged.

Do not guide the child's hand to select a word or compose a message on their behalf. Helping position a device for accessible use is different from determining the content of someone's message. Water, food, rest, toilet access and necessary assistance remain available regardless of whether the child has used the selected communication method.

## Prompt dependency

Prompt dependency occurs when an action remains tied to an additional adult cue instead of independent use of the skill in a suitable situation. For example, a child may know how to put things away but wait for a repeated instruction even if the usual cue is already familiar.

The reason for waiting is not always clear. Check whether the adult prompts too quickly, whether the same prompt is repeated in every situation, whether the child understands when the action is appropriate and whether the support is actually necessary.

A [2024 study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39336043/) addresses reducing prompt dependency during functional communication training with one 16-year-old autistic participant. Its findings do not establish a universal procedure for all children.

## Physical assistance, consent and safety

Before using a prompt, assess the accessibility of the task, the child's interest and whether support without touch is possible.

If the child pulls away, freezes, tries to free themselves, cries or expresses refusal, stop the physical prompt. Identify the source of discomfort and offer another way of providing support. Do not hold the child's hands, force their body to move or disregard refusal for the sake of correct performance. The absence of resistance does not itself indicate consent.

When personal care, movement or a potentially dangerous action requires assistance, support must respect safety, dignity and the child's needs. If there is an immediate risk of injury, safety takes priority over teaching. New pain, sudden loss of a skill or marked motor difficulties require appropriate professional assessment.

A movement entirely guided by an adult cannot count as independent performance. When teaching communication, distinguish access to the device from the content of the message. [ASHA](https://www.asha.org/slp/fc-and-rpm-faqs/) specifically warns about a helper's influence on message authorship.

## When support should not be removed

The purpose of prompt fading is to reduce unnecessary adult assistance, not deprive a person of necessary aids.

AAC, glasses, hearing aids, adapted clothing, written instructions, visual schedules, timers and other useful devices may be used permanently. If a child independently consults a visual schedule to gather their belongings, this may constitute independent performance with an adaptation.

The [ASHA professional resource on AAC](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/professional-issues/augmentative-and-alternative-communication/) emphasises that communication aids must remain accessible. Do not remove them to encourage spoken language or test a skill.

## How to assess independence and generalisation

Select an observable action and define in advance what counts as independent performance. Record naturally occurring opportunities, rather than requiring a fixed number of trials. Distinguish three outcomes: independent performance, performance after an additional prompt and an action not completed. Record the type of assistance, comfort, refusal and circumstances separately.

### Example diary and chart

Skill: place a light, unbreakable cup in an accessible location after drinking. All figures below are fictional and are provided only to explain the observation method.

| Day | Total opportunities | Independent | Prompted | Not completed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mon | 5 | 1 | 4 | 0 |
| Tue | 5 | 2 | 3 | 0 |
| Wed | 5 | 3 | 2 | 0 |
| Thu | 5 | 4 | 0 | 1 |
| Fri | 5 | 4 | 1 | 0 |

Percentage of independent actions = number of independent performances / number of observed opportunities × 100%. If no suitable opportunities occurred, do not calculate a percentage. Do not include artificially created trials for the sake of completing the table.

The chart is based on fictional table data, not a clinical study. It does not specify a required pace, number of attempts, teaching duration or target percentage. On Thursday, 20% of actions were not completed, so the two lines do not add up to 100%. A prompt was needed once again on Friday. This is normal within the illustrative example and does not mean that a child has lost a skill.

Try reducing unnecessary assistance when the action becomes more accessible and the child participates comfortably. If independence does not increase, or errors, refusal or discomfort arise, reconsider the type of assistance. Do not remove permanent adaptations for the sake of an attractive graph.

### Checking generalisation

Observe whether the action is accessible under other familiar and safe conditions: with another object, at home or at school, or with another adult. Conditions may differ, so another adaptation may be required. Do not insist on performance in all situations or repeatedly test the skill without the child's consent.

## Common errors and questions for a specialist

| Common error | What to change |
| --- | --- |
| Prompt immediately every time | Allow time if this is comfortable for the child. |
| Repeat the same instruction constantly | Check understanding of the task and accessibility of the environment. |
| Complete the entire action for the child | Assist at the difficult step while maintaining necessary care. |
| Increase assistance after refusal | Stop and establish what is creating a barrier. |
| Count all correct responses as independent | Distinguish performance with and without a prompt. |
| Remove AAC or permanent visual support | Preserve necessary aids. |

Ask the specialist what skill has been selected, which ordinary cue will initiate it, why a particular prompt is needed, what observations will determine fading, how refusal and discomfort are considered and how generalisation will be assessed. Ask which devices should remain available.

Prompting is used in various educational and support programmes. Knowledge of the procedure alone does not establish a specialist's professional qualification in Kazakhstan.

## What does the research say?

The [systematic review by Cengher and colleagues (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10882-017-9575-8) included 45 publications and 46 experiments. Different procedures supported acquisition of actions, but comparative effectiveness and learning speed depended on the prompt type and learning task. The review includes people with various developmental disabilities, not only autistic children.

In the [review by Hume and colleagues (2021)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8510990/), prompting was among 28 practices meeting the authors' evidence criteria. The broad review synthesised 972 publications, not 972 studies of prompting. These findings do not justify using any prompt without considering the individual's needs.

In a [2016 study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27606243/), most-to-least prompting was more effective than least-to-most prompting for three children. In [another study published in 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31414492/), the most suitable types of prompts varied, and the least-to-most procedure was more efficient in terms of teaching time for participants in that particular study.

A [2020 randomised study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31347696/) compared three prompting procedures to teach naming of objects and other stimuli to 27 autistic participants. There was no control group without intervention, and few differences were found between the procedures. The findings are limited to the task studied.

In a [comparison of video prompting and least-to-most prompting (2020)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30771131/), outcomes varied among participants. The small study cannot establish one approach for all daily living skills. The [review by Brown and Cariveau (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10864-022-09481-6) included 11 publications comparing simultaneous prompting and time delay. The authors identify a need for further research on the conditions in which dependency on additional assistance decreases.

The most defensible conclusion is to select support individually, track independent performance separately from prompted actions, and consider comfort and generalisation. There is no scientifically established universal sequence or timetable for fading prompts.
