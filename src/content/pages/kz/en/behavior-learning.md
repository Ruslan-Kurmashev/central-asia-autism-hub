A child shouts, walks away from a task, pushes an adult's hand away or refuses to enter a noisy classroom. Similar actions can have very different causes: pain, anxiety, unclear instructions, sensory overload, tiredness or no accessible way to communicate a need. Sometimes the child simply does not want to continue, and that refusal needs to be heard.

Rather than starting with a technique designed to "stop the behaviour", it is more useful to understand what is happening and what might help the child. This guide describes the main approaches, a step-by-step example and questions to discuss with a professional.

## Why behaviour can become difficult

Behaviour occurs in a particular context. The same shout may happen because of pain, an unexpected change of plans or a demand the child does not yet understand. The appearance of an action alone cannot reliably tell us its cause.

Not every unusual behaviour needs to change. Safe repetitive movements and other forms of self-regulation should not be suppressed merely because they look different from what others expect. Support is worth discussing when there is a genuine difficulty, such as a risk of injury, substantial distress, barriers to communication, participation or independence.

The child's perspective matters when setting a goal, including preferences expressed through gestures, refusal or other nonverbal communication. Sitting perfectly still or following every instruction without objection is not, on its own, evidence of a better quality of life.

## What to check first

If the behaviour starts suddenly, becomes much more intense or is accompanied by new pain, signs of illness or a risk of injury, the child's condition needs to be assessed. These changes should not simply be attributed to autism or met with more intensive sessions.

[NICE CG170](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations) recommends looking for possible reasons for behaviour that challenges before choosing an intervention. Depending on the situation, consider:

- **Physical health:** pain, sleep problems, gastrointestinal symptoms, adverse effects of treatment and other medical conditions.
- **Emotional wellbeing:** anxiety, fear, severe stress, possible bullying or abuse.
- **Communication:** whether the child understands the request and can refuse, ask for help or explain pain or a need for a break.
- **Environment:** noise, lighting, crowds, long waits or an overly demanding task.
- **Changes:** a new routine, moving to another class, changes at home or school, and lack of predictability.

Sometimes treatment for an identified health problem or a change to the environment is more useful than a behaviour technique. Lowering the noise, making a task clearer, offering choices or making a break available may be enough. If serious risk persists, professional assessment and a safety plan are needed, not experiments at home that provoke dangerous behaviour.

## Understanding the function of behaviour

Functional behaviour assessment (FBA) explores the circumstances in which an action occurs and what may be maintaining it. Here, *function* means a possible consequence or outcome, such as ending an overly difficult task, getting help or accessing an item. It is a working hypothesis, not a diagnosis or proof of the child's intention.

Assessment considers three elements:

1. **Before the event.** Where and when did it happen? What changed in the child's health, surroundings or demands?
2. **During the event.** What exactly did the child do? How often did it happen, and how long did it last?
3. **After the event.** How did others respond? Did the noise, task, attention or opportunity to rest change?

Observations are then compared across situations, including times when the difficulty is absent. A hypothesis should be revised when the evidence does not support it. The point is not only to count episodes of shouting but to understand what the child is experiencing and what assistance they need.

[NICE CG170](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations) is relevant to autistic children and young people generally. [NICE NG11](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng11/chapter/recommendations) provides further detail on functional assessment, but it concerns people with learning disabilities whose behaviour challenges. Its recommendations should not automatically be applied to every autistic child.

## Approaches that may be used

What does ABA mean? Applied behaviour analysis (ABA) covers different learning principles and procedures, not a single fixed programme. The label "ABA programme" does not tell a family what goals have been set, what will be done or whether it is suitable for their child.

[Hume et al. (2021)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8510990/) reviewed 972 publications and identified 28 focused practices that met the authors' evidence criteria. The review covered studies published up to the end of 2017. It is not a ranking of methods in 2026 and does not establish that any commercial course works. A later [review by Nuske et al. (2024)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37740093/) also discusses preventing difficult situations, emotion regulation, visual support and parental involvement. Outcomes should be evaluated for the particular procedure and goal.

### 1. Functional behaviour assessment (FBA)

This assessment is not meant to suppress a reaction. It helps justify the choice of support. A professional examines the context, observations and possible functions while considering health, communication and the environment. Sometimes the appropriate response is to change the conditions rather than teach the child to react differently.

### 2. Functional communication training (FCT)

The child is supported to express a need in an accessible way, using speech, a gesture, a card, a button or augmentative and alternative communication (AAC). They may learn to communicate "Help", "Break" or "Too loud". Adults need to recognise these messages and respond appropriately. A clear refusal must not be ignored simply because the child did not use the preferred card.

[Read more about FCT](/kz/en/parents/fct-functional-communication-training/)

### 3. Changing conditions before a difficulty arises

Antecedent-based interventions can make situations more accessible. Examples include reducing noise and waiting time, breaking down a task, showing what will happen next, offering a choice or planning rest. The purpose is to address the difficulty, not to force a child to tolerate distressing conditions.

A narrower example is a high-probability request sequence. A [meta-analysis by Cowan et al. (2017)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28236099/) focused mainly on task completion and responding to requests. These findings do not establish improvements in independence or quality of life, and they do not justify pressuring a child to comply.

### 4. Reinforcement

Reinforcement means that a consequence makes an action more likely in the future. When a request for a break leads to an accessible opportunity to rest, the child may become more likely to use that communication. Negative reinforcement is not punishment. Water, food, the toilet, medical care and communication must not become rewards for obedience.

[Read more about reinforcement](/kz/en/parents/reinforcement-parent-guide/)

### 5. Differential reinforcement

These procedures arrange more favourable consequences for some actions than for others. DRA, DRI, DRO and DRL differ in their goals and rules. The label alone does not explain why a goal matters, whether it relates to the function of behaviour or whether the child will be deprived of something important. A safe repetitive movement should not automatically become a target for reduction.

[Read more about differential reinforcement](/kz/en/parents/differential-reinforcement-guide/)

### 6. Prompts and gradually fading them

Verbal, visual and gestural prompts or demonstrations can help when a skill is being learned. Support can then be reduced as the child becomes ready to act independently. Distinguish a skill initiated by the child from an action performed after a prompt. Reliance on continuous prompting is not the same as independent mastery.

[Read more about prompts and prompt fading (in Russian)](/kz/ru/parents/prompting-and-prompt-fading/)

### 7. Breaking a skill into steps

Task analysis identifies the actions involved in an everyday or learning task. When learning to dress, for example, the steps may include choosing clothes, putting them on and managing fasteners. The point is not simply to finish the task but to identify where adaptations or help are useful and gradually increase independence.

[Examples of teaching everyday skills (in Russian)](/kz/ru/parents/obuchenie-povsednevnym-navykam/)

### 8. Demonstration and video modelling

Another person, or the child themselves in a video, demonstrates a complete skill or some of its steps. This may help with a particular daily-life or educational action, but the purpose and format should suit the child's needs, abilities and preferences.

[Read more about video modelling (in Russian)](/kz/ru/parents/video-modeling/)

### 9. Self-management

Children or adolescents learn to choose and use appropriate supports, such as a checklist, timer, schedule, reminder or a way to record completed steps. Self-management does not mean being left without help. People should be able to make decisions and use support whenever they need it.

[Read more about self-management (in Russian)](/kz/ru/parents/self-management/)

**What do we know about effectiveness and safety?** The updated [Project AIM review (Sandbank et al., 2023)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37963634/) pooled 252 studies of non-pharmacological early interventions involving 13,304 children younger than eight. These were not 252 studies of ABA alone. Findings varied by approach and outcome, and stricter criteria for outcome measurement reduced the number of statistically significant findings. Only about 10% of reports mentioned adverse events. This reflects inadequate safety reporting, not proof that all approaches are harmless or harmful.

## Practical example: a child struggles in a group activity

Imagine a group session. Music starts, an adult hands out pencils and invites the children to draw. One child covers their ears, pushes a pencil away and leaves the table. These are observations. They do not tell us that the child "does not want to learn" or "is trying to get their own way".

**What can the adult do?**

1. **Make the situation safe and reduce the burden first.** Do not physically keep the child at the table. Turn down the music, offer a quiet space or allow a short break. New pain, illness or an abrupt substantial change in behaviour calls for medical assessment.
2. **Help the child communicate what they need.** Ask briefly and clearly what is difficult. Accept speech or gestures. Where useful, make messages such as "Too loud", "Help", "I don't understand", "Break" and "No" accessible. Do not insist on a particular card when the child has already clearly refused.
3. **Compare situations without deliberately provoking new episodes.** Is free play in the same room difficult too? What happens when the music is off? Does a shorter instruction help? Observations may suggest the influence of noise, tasks or other factors, but do not replace health assessment.
4. **Choose a change that matches the need.** If noise is the problem, reduce it and make a quiet space available. If the instruction is unclear, demonstrate or divide the task into steps. If asking for a break is difficult, make communication accessible and respond to it. Several factors may be involved at once.
5. **Review the outcome in everyday life.** Is the child calmer? Can they ask for help or rest independently? Can they take part in activities that matter to them without being held in place or constantly prompted? Can they still refuse?

If lowering the noise helps the child return to the activity, it is worth investigating the sensory environment. One event does not prove that noise was the only cause or that the same change will always work.

**Do not:** deliberately increase the noise, keep a child in a distressing situation, ignore a clear refusal or restrict communication to obtain the "correct response". Dangerous behaviour requires a professional safety plan, not experiments at home.

*Five-step overview: notice the difficulty, reduce the burden, accept communication, choose suitable support together and review what changed. Do not restrain the child, intensify the noise or ignore refusal.*

## How to assess progress and when to change the plan

Before starting support, it is useful to agree on 1-3 concrete goals with the child and family, record the starting point and set a review date. "Behave better" is too vague. More meaningful outcomes might include asking for help, taking a break without severe distress or completing an important step independently.

Look at several areas together:

- **Independence:** does the child use the skill independently or only after prompting?
- **Communication:** is it easier to express a need, discomfort or refusal?
- **Participation and comfort:** can the child engage in meaningful activities without unnecessary pressure?
- **Generalisation and maintenance:** does support work at home and at school, with different people, and does the skill last?
- **Safety and burden:** have fear, avoidance, sleep problems, distress or excessive demands on the family appeared?

If the child's condition worsens, the working hypothesis is not supported or improvements occur only in the specialist's office, reconsider the plan. Do not automatically increase demands or session frequency. A programme should not continue merely because it has already started.

## Where to seek help in Kazakhstan and what to ask a professional

Begin with the difficulty the child is experiencing, not a method's name. Sudden changes, pain or signs of illness warrant medical assessment, usually starting with a paediatrician and, where indicated, other clinicians. Difficulties understanding speech or expressing needs may call for assessment by a speech and language professional or someone competent in AAC. Schools and their support staff should be involved when the learning environment is part of the difficulty. Injury risks or complex interacting factors may require coordination between professionals.

Terms such as "ABA specialist", "behaviour specialist" or a certificate from a short course do not by themselves establish professional qualifications. Check the person's basic training, relevant competence, role and service arrangements. NICE guidance from the UK does not set legally required procedures in Kazakhstan.

Ask before starting a programme:

1. What is difficult for this child, and which medical, communication and environmental factors have already been considered?
2. What supports the proposed functional explanation and why was this approach selected?
3. How can the child refuse or ask for help or a break without pressure?
4. Which skill or environmental change should improve their everyday life?
5. What outcomes and possible harms will be monitored, and when will the plan be reviewed?
6. How will results be checked outside the consultation room, and what happens if there is no benefit?

Behaviour support is useful when it helps a person communicate, learn and participate in daily life more safely and freely. Making an observable response less frequent without improving comfort and autonomy is not a sufficient outcome.
