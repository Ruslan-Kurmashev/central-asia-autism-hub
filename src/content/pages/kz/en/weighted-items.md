Weighted blankets, vests and lap pads put additional weight on the body. They are sometimes offered to autistic children to help with relaxation, sleep, attention or "sensory regulation". Some children enjoy the feeling of pressure. Enjoyment, however, is not evidence of a treatment effect.

> **Important safety warning.** Weighted products must not be used for infant sleep. For older children, their use requires an individual risk assessment. Do not cover the head or neck, wrap or restrain a child, obstruct breathing or movement, or leave the child without appropriate supervision. A child must be able to remove the product independently. RCOT advises removing a weighted blanket once the child has fallen asleep, even when it was used to settle before bed. Following the "10% of body weight" rule does not guarantee safety.

These products do not treat autism. Their intended purpose, design and risks must be assessed separately.

## What are weighted products, and how do they differ?

Weighted products include blankets, vests, lap pads and other products containing additional weight. They differ in how they distribute pressure, how long they are used, whether the wearer can move freely and what they are intended to achieve.

- **Blankets** may cover a large part of the body and introduce particular risks around sleep.
- **Vests** are worn while sitting or moving. They may affect comfort and movement, and benefits for attention have not been established.
- **Lap pads** provide more localised pressure while sitting. Evidence about blankets or vests cannot automatically be applied to lap pads.

Some forms of deep tactile pressure have been studied as sensory interventions. Massage, pressure applied by hand and weighted products are not a single standardised procedure. A positive finding for one does not establish benefits for all.

## What does research say about sleep, attention and anxiety?

### Weighted blankets and sleep in autistic children

In the [randomised trial by Gringras and colleagues (2014)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25022743/), children aged 5-16 with autism and significant sleep difficulties used a weighted blanket and a similar-looking ordinary blanket. **73 children were randomised and 67 completed the study and were analysed.** Each condition lasted two weeks.

Actigraphy and other sleep measures found no advantage for the weighted blanket in total sleep time, falling asleep, night waking or sleep efficiency. Children and parents more often preferred the weighted blanket, but preference does not establish improved sleep.

The [American Academy of Neurology guideline (AAN, 2020)](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000009033) does not support weighted blankets as an evidence-based routine treatment for sleep difficulties in autistic children. The absence of serious adverse events in one small, short trial does not establish that all products are safe at home.

### The broader 2024 meta-analysis

The [review by Zhao and colleagues (2024)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39447684/) included eight randomised trials and 426 participants from different psychiatric populations. The authors reported a small average effect on anxiety, while the primary pooled analysis of insomnia severity did not show a statistically significant benefit.

In a sensitivity analysis restricted to two remaining, more homogeneous trials, the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) improved. That finding comes from a limited subset and does not establish a sleep benefit for autistic children.

Half the included trials had a high risk of bias. Evidence from adults and mixed psychiatric groups cannot replace direct studies of sleep among autistic children.

### Weighted vests and attention

A [systematic review by Taylor and colleagues (2017)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1750946717300387) found insufficient evidence to regard weighted vests as an effective practice for autism. Studies were small, often used single-case designs and measured different outcomes.

A [review by Piller and colleagues (2025)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41321460/) found positive results for certain types of deep pressure within a broader class of sensory-based interventions. This does not establish that a particular vest improves attention, learning or behaviour for every child. The authors' strength-of-evidence terminology is not an automatic high-certainty GRADE rating or a universal guarantee.

**Practical implication:** there is no evidence-based reason to buy a weighted product solely because a child sleeps poorly, moves frequently or feels anxious. First consider why the difficulty occurs and whether more direct support would help.

## Safety: breathing, movement and sleep

Weight is only one aspect of risk. Age, mobility, ability to escape, the design of covers and fasteners, temperature, breathing, alertness and coexisting health conditions all matter.

[RCOT guidance (2023)](https://www.rcot.co.uk/media/649/download?attachment=) calls for individual risk assessment, clear instructions, consent, suitable supervision and review of outcomes. [Somerset NHS Foundation Trust](https://www.somersetft.nhs.uk/children-and-young-peoples-therapy-service/guidance-on-the-use-of-weighted-blankets/) provides further practical precautions.

### Safety rules that must not be ignored

- Never cover the head or neck, wrap a child tightly or interfere with breathing.
- Do not use a product when the child cannot independently free themselves without a qualified professional assessment of safety. Being able to remove a product is important but does not eliminate other risks.
- Never use a blanket, vest or pad to hold a child in place, suppress movement, punish them or enforce compliance.
- Do not leave a child under a weighted blanket without the required supervision. When used to settle before sleep, remove it after the child falls asleep. It should not hang over the bed edge.
- Consider respiratory health, body-temperature regulation, skin, medicines that affect alertness and other individual risks.
- Follow the manufacturer's size, weight, material and care instructions. Check for damage and accessible filling.

If there is doubt about safe breathing, movement or stopping use, do not use the product. Discuss safer alternatives with an appropriately qualified professional.

### A documented risk of becoming trapped inside a cover

In December 2022, the US Consumer Product Safety Commission [CPSC announced a recall of about 204,000 specific Pillowfort weighted blankets](https://www.cpsc.gov/Recalls/2023/Target-Recalls-Childrens-Pillowfort-Weighted-Blankets-Due-to-Asphyxiation-Hazard-Two-Fatalities-Reported). According to CPSC, two girls aged 4 and 6 died from asphyxiation after becoming trapped inside a removable blanket cover.

This was a recall of a specific product, not evidence of the rate of such events across all weighted blankets. It illustrates why the design of covers, closures and product recalls must be checked, rather than focusing only on weight.

### Extra precautions during sleep and for infants

The [American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP, 2022)](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/150/1/e2022057990/188750/SIDS-and-Other-Sleep-Related-Infant-Deaths) recommends that weighted blankets, swaddles, sleep sacks and other weighted objects not be placed on or near a sleeping infant.

Night-time use cannot be presumed safe for older children either. RCOT recommends removing a weighted blanket once the child is asleep. Apparent calmness or sleeping for a long time under a product does not demonstrate safety.

### When to remove the product immediately and seek help

Stop using it immediately if the child has difficulty breathing, skin colour changes, excessive heat, pain, dizziness, panic, attempts to escape or any other sign of physical or emotional distress.

If breathing is seriously impaired, the person loses consciousness or the situation is life-threatening, seek **emergency medical assistance** rather than waiting for symptoms to resolve.

## Choosing a product and understanding the 10% rule

Some manufacturers suggest a blanket weighing around 10% of the person's body weight. RCOT notes that manufacturers commonly use this guideline and advises following product instructions alongside an individual assessment of risk.

**10% is not a proven therapeutic dose or a safety guarantee.** Even a lighter product can be unsafe if its design is unsuitable, mobility is restricted, breathing is impaired or the user cannot remove it. Increasing weight in pursuit of a stronger effect is not justified.

Consider the whole product: who will use it, how the cover closes, whether the person can get out, whether its materials are appropriate, whether warnings or recalls exist and whether the goal could be met without adding weight.

UK professional guidance helps inform risk assessment but does not substitute for checking applicable requirements in Kazakhstan. A label such as "sensory" or "for autism" is not proof of effectiveness or safety.

## Voluntary use and the child's right to refuse

The child's wishes matter. They may communicate "no", "take it off", "too hot" or "too heavy" through speech, gestures, augmentative and alternative communication (AAC), facial expression or behaviour. Do not continue merely because they cannot describe discomfort in a full sentence.

Additional weight must not be used to enforce stillness or make a child easier for adults to manage. If a child moves less while wearing a vest, consider whether the vest is simply making movement physically harder.

A preference for pressure may be meaningful for comfort, but it neither turns a product into treatment nor removes the need for safety checks. A change to the environment, a normal break or an activity chosen by the child may be safer alternatives.

## How to assess whether it helps

Before considering a product, identify the specific goal and starting situation. A child might find it hard to settle before bed or take part in a particular activity. Avoid goals such as "fix sensory integration" or "make the child sit still".

Agree with a professional how and when to evaluate outcomes: is the child more comfortable during the relevant activity, do they choose the product, are there unwanted effects and does it offer any benefit over simpler options?

For sleep-related goals, monitoring the time it takes to fall asleep, total sleep and night waking is more informative than saying the child "looks calmer". A trial of a weighted product must not replace assessment of persistent sleep problems.

Do not continue indefinitely without review. No improvement, considerable distress or an inability to ensure safety are reasons not to continue.

## When to investigate another cause first

Difficulty sleeping can be related to pain, anxiety, routine, snoring and sleep apnoea, digestive symptoms, medicines, room temperature or lighting. Persistent or new sleep difficulties warrant assessment by a healthcare professional.

If a child frequently gets up during a lesson, possible reasons include fatigue, an unclear task, need for movement, noise, pain or insufficient communication support. A vest cannot replace investigation of these causes.

If loud surroundings are the main problem, adjusting the environment or providing a break may address the need more directly.

## Questions to ask a professional in Kazakhstan

Before using any weighted product, ask:

1. What everyday difficulty are we trying to address?
2. What evidence supports this exact product for the child's age and goal?
3. Have simpler, less risky alternatives been considered?
4. Are there concerns about breathing, temperature regulation, skin, movement or alertness?
5. Can the child remove it independently, and do they want to use it?
6. Have the cover design, manufacturer's instructions, recalls and materials been checked?
7. How will supervision be arranged, and how will use during sleep or as restraint be prevented?
8. How will benefit be measured, when will the decision be reviewed and what would require immediate discontinuation?

Availability in a shop or use by a centre in Kazakhstan is not proof of safety or effectiveness. Buying special equipment should not be a condition for accessing support.

## Summary and related reading

Some children enjoy weighted products, but evidence does not support them as universal treatments for sleep, attention or behaviour in autism. A trial in autistic children did not demonstrate objective improvements in sleep, and evidence for weighted vests remains insufficient.

Safety, consent, individual risk assessment and a meaningful everyday goal are essential. Do not use weighted products for infant sleep, do not restrain movement and do not leave a weighted blanket on a child after they have fallen asleep. The 10% rule does not replace a risk assessment.
