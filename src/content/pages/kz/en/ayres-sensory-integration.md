A child may enjoy swinging without needing Ayres Sensory Integration. Swings, mats and balls can be ordinary play equipment. What distinguishes a specialist intervention from play is not the equipment but how a professional assesses the child's needs, selects activities and evaluates the outcomes.

Ayres Sensory Integration (ASI) is a particular individualised, play-based approach used in occupational therapy. It may be considered for autistic children when sensory difficulties affect meaningful everyday activities. ASI does not treat or "correct" autism.

## What is ASI and how is it different from ordinary sensory play?

ASI sessions may use suspended swings, platforms, balls, mats, play structures and tactile materials. The child participates actively, while the professional adapts activities to the child's interests, abilities and responses.

Equipment alone does not define a method. A sensory room, massage, movement-based play or a weighted vest is not necessarily ASI.

### A practical distinction

**Equipment:** swings, mats and balls are available for play. Simply using them does not establish that an activity is ASI.

**ASI approach:** the professional has assessment findings, an individual functional goal and relevant preparation. They adapt play according to ASI principles and check whether the goal is met in daily life.

A useful decision sequence is:

1. **Assess:** identify the everyday difficulty, possible sensory influences and alternative explanations.
2. **Agree on a goal:** define a meaningful activity the child wants to do or do more easily.
3. **Choose support:** consider environmental adjustments and other options. If ASI is justified, check adherence to its principles.
4. **Review:** assess participation and comfort outside the therapy room, and reconsider the programme if it does not help.

This is a framework for choosing support, not instructions for delivering ASI at home.

## When might ASI be considered, and what are the alternatives?

An unusual response to sound, light, movement or touch does not automatically require therapy. First ask whether the situation makes eating, dressing, washing, playing, using the toilet, learning or another valued activity difficult.

If a child finds a noisy dining hall overwhelming, options might include quieter seating, a different lunch time, a break or suitable hearing protection. These changes may make eating possible without trying to change the child's underlying sensory responses.

Pain, anxiety, fatigue, communication difficulties and unsuitable demands can affect behaviour as much as sensory stimuli. Sudden sensitivity, pain, new hearing or balance problems or loss of previously acquired skills warrant medical assessment.

ASI is one option to discuss only when assessment identifies a specific functional difficulty, the proposed approach fits the child and the potential benefit is proportionate to the demands and cost.

## Assessment, professional training and ASI fidelity

In AOTA and RCOT research and professional materials, ASI is discussed in the context of occupational therapy. The Ayres Sensory Integration Fidelity Measure was developed to assess how closely an intervention follows ASI principles.

Mentioning the fidelity measure or holding a certificate does not establish the quality of an individual service. Ask about the professional's base qualification, ASI training, assessment method and how outcomes will be measured.

AOTA's Practice Smart recommendation advises against sensory-based interventions without a documented assessment of specific sensory processing or integration difficulties. It does not mean every child with sensory differences should be prescribed ASI.

The key assessment question is: what meaningful activity is currently difficult, and what would enable the child to participate? A sensory questionnaire or profile alone is not proof that a particular therapy is needed.

## What does research from 2022-2026 show?

ASI findings are mixed. Some studies report gains in individual functional goals, while others find no overall advantage over usual support. Age, intervention fidelity, controls, outcome quality and follow-up vary. It would be misleading to reduce these studies to one simple statement that "ASI works" or "ASI does not work".

### SenITA (2022): a large trial without an overall advantage

The UK randomised [SenITA trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35766242/) enrolled 138 autistic children with sensory processing difficulties, **69 per group**. Sensory integration therapy was compared with usual care.

There was no statistically significant overall advantage for the primary irritability outcome at six months. Wider outcomes did not show a convincing overall benefit at six or twelve months. The intervention was not found to be cost-effective relative to usual care.

Individual goals improved within the intervention group, but comparable goals were not measured in the control group. Therefore, those within-group changes cannot establish that the therapy caused the improvement or outperformed usual care.

### Acuña review (2025): individual goals, not universal effects

A [systematic review of randomised trials](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40193295/) included **nine RCTs with 344 participants younger than 12**, six of which involved autistic children. Positive findings mainly concerned individualised goals related to participation and functioning. Benefits for irritability and noncompliance were not established.

This does not mean ASI is guaranteed to improve speech, attention, behaviour or independence for every child.

### A comparative trial and Kishida review (2025-2026)

In a [comparative trial by Schaaf and colleagues (2025)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40815044/), occupational therapy using ASI (OT-ASI), applied behaviour analysis (ABA) and a control condition without either active intervention were compared. Both active intervention groups performed better than control on individual goal attainment, while an advantage in daily living skills did not reach statistical significance.

A [systematic review by Kishida and colleagues (2026)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41903087/) considered ten studies reporting adherence to the revised ASI fidelity measure. Only four were assessed as having a low risk of bias. Findings within these stronger studies were mixed, and the authors raised concerns about Goal Attainment Scaling (GAS) measurement quality.

Current findings therefore do not establish a consistent overall benefit across all programmes adhering to ASI principles.

### New Cangi and Bumin trial (6 October 2026)

A [trial published in BMC Pediatrics](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07777-4) enrolled preschool autistic children aged 48-59 months in Türkiye. **63 were randomised, 60 were analysed**, with 30 in each group. Over 12 weeks, one group received additional ASI while both continued their usual educational programme.

The intervention group had better scores on a daily living skills subscale of VABS-II (**p = 0.042**) and on individual goal attainment GAS (**p < 0.001**). The overall VABS-II score did not show a statistically significant between-group difference (**p = 0.055**).

These are positive results for selected outcomes, but the sample was small, the age range narrow, longer-term follow-up absent and trial registration retrospective. This trial does not overturn SenITA or establish benefits at all ages.

### ASI with CO-OP (11 November 2026): two active approaches compared

In a [trial by Yazıcıoğlu and Bumin](https://doi.org/10.3390/children13111562/), 26 autistic children aged 7-11 received either ASI plus Cognitive Orientation to daily Occupational Performance (CO-OP) or ASI alone for ten weeks.

Some executive-function and daily-occupation outcomes improved in the combined group, but there was **no statistically significant between-group difference on the overall SCOPE participation score**.

The small sample, short follow-up, unequal amounts of ASI and limitations of assessor blinding complicate interpretation. This does not compare ASI with no intervention and cannot isolate the independent contribution of CO-OP.

### Why a broader meta-analysis does not settle the question

[Park and Kim (2026)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41935430/) included 23 randomised trials of a broader category of sensory integration therapy among children with different developmental conditions. Some motor and daily functioning outcomes improved on average, but not every intervention met a strict ASI definition and not every participant was autistic. Those pooled effects cannot automatically be attributed to fidelity-defined ASI for autistic children.

The reviews also overlap in the older studies they include. Their participant numbers and trial counts must not be added together as though the studies were independent.

**Overall assessment:** possible benefits for certain individual goals deserve further investigation. Confidence in broad behavioural and functional effects remains limited. Both "ASI is proven to help everyone" and "ASI can never help anyone" misrepresent the available evidence.

## Meaningful goals and what ASI cannot promise

Goals should describe concrete activities rather than an abstract improvement in "sensory integration". Examples include:

- the child getting dressed in chosen clothing with suitable support
- joining a play activity they value in a playground
- completing an everyday sequence with less help.

Do not attribute every gain to one intervention. Children develop and learn in several settings and may receive other support at the same time. Improvement within one group does not establish superiority to an alternative.

ASI is not an autism cure and cannot promise speech onset, increased intelligence, the disappearance of stimming, elimination of all behavioural difficulties or guaranteed independence.

## Safety, consent and sensory preferences

Enjoying swinging, jumping or repetitive movements does not itself require therapy. If the activity is safe and does not obstruct valued participation, it should not be targeted solely for looking unusual.

During any session, the child needs an accessible way to refuse, ask to stop, take a break and report discomfort. Marked distress should not automatically be interpreted as a reason to continue exposure. Swings and suspended equipment require appropriate safety procedures and qualified supervision.

ASI must not be used to suppress safe stimming or compel a child to experience distressing sensations. If the main barrier is noise, crowding or bright light, adjusting the environment may be more direct than trying to change the child's response.

## Evaluating outcomes and deciding when to change a programme

Describe the starting situation before sessions begin. For example, a child needs full help to get dressed or can join a preferred game only with continuous assistance.

Agree on an observable goal, how it will be measured and when to review the programme. The focus is participation in everyday life, not only performance within the therapy room.

Questions include:

- Is the relevant task easier at home, nursery or school?
- Does the child need less assistance, and are they more comfortable?
- Does a skill carry over from the session to daily activities?
- Is any benefit proportionate to the time, cost and effort involved?
- Could environmental adjustments or another approach offer similar benefits?

Looking calmer immediately after a session does not demonstrate improvement in the functional goal. When GAS is used, ask who set the goals, how achievement was scored and whether the assessor knew which intervention was assigned.

Review the programme if goals are unclear, every child receives identical exercises, the child repeatedly experiences substantial distress, improvements do not transfer beyond the clinic or the provider promises a guaranteed autism treatment. No meaningful benefit within the agreed period is grounds to discuss changing or stopping the approach.

## Questions for professionals in Kazakhstan

Different centres may use the label "sensory integration" for ordinary play, massage, swinging or ASI. These services should not be considered equivalent because they share a name.

Before starting, ask:

1. Is this ASI or another sensory approach? How is adherence to ASI principles checked?
2. What is your base professional qualification and additional ASI training?
3. Which difficulties did the assessment identify, and were pain, anxiety, communication and the environment considered?
4. What meaningful functional goal was agreed with the child and family?
5. What research applies to that goal and age group?
6. How will participation, comfort and independence be measured outside the clinic?
7. How can the child refuse or ask for a break?
8. When will the plan be reviewed, and what would lead to a change or stop?
9. Can a simpler environmental adjustment be tried first?

A sensory room or short-course certificate does not prove ASI fidelity. Professional qualifications and applicable service requirements in Kazakhstan must be checked separately. AOTA and RCOT materials do not define Kazakhstan licensing rules.

## Summary and related materials

ASI is a specific individualised, play-based occupational therapy approach, not a name for all sensory activities. Evidence does not support it as a universal treatment for autism. Potential benefits need evaluation against a concrete functional goal, the child's interests, simpler alternatives and study limitations.

The useful starting question is: **what situation makes participation difficult, and what support could make it safer and more comfortable?**
