**Everyday independence is not about “doing everything without help.”** It is the opportunity to take part in one's own life: choosing clothes, placing a cup on the table, opening an accessible fastening, asking for help, washing hands with assistance or putting away a favourite toy.

Some children acquire everyday skills with little apparent effort. Others need repetition, accessible explanations, alternative positions, adapted objects or ongoing help. **Help does not mean that a child has failed.** The action should be safe, manageable and meaningful to the child.

This guide offers 12 home activities, visual instructions and two free PDF resources. They support participation but are not treatment, diagnosis or a standardised assessment.

## 1. What counts as everyday independence?

It includes:

- **Dressing:** choosing clothes, putting an arm through a sleeve, adjusting a shirt or opening a hook-and-loop fastening.
- **Eating:** using a suitable spoon, setting down a cup or communicating that one is full or needs something.
- **Hygiene:** washing one's face and hands or participating in toothbrushing.
- **Toileting:** recognising and communicating bodily signals and using an accessible toilet with appropriate support.
- **Household tasks:** putting a toy in a box, putting away a book or packing a lightweight bag.
- **Communication:** asking for help or a break, or stopping an unpleasant activity.

These skills need not develop simultaneously. A diagnosis or the absence of speech does not automatically determine what the child can do with their hands, understand in familiar situations or choose independently.

For professional support see [“Independence and daily living skills”](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/ru/parents/samostoyatelnost-i-povsednevnye-navyki/).

## 2. Choosing an appropriate skill without overload

Ask **“What would make one ordinary situation a little more comfortable for the child and family today?”**

For example, mornings may be difficult because clothing seams are uncomfortable and the family is rushing. That does not necessarily mean practising buttons more. Soft clothes prepared the evening before, and dressing while sitting, might help more.

| Situation | What could be adapted | How the child might participate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Putting on a shirt is difficult | Loose clothing, stable support, less rushing. | Pulling down the lower hem. |
| A spoon slips | Accessible handle, nonslip mat, comfortable position. | Holding the spoon or asking for help. |
| Handwashing feels unpleasant | Comfortable water, familiar towel, short instructions. | Taking soap or drying hands. |
| The child is afraid of the toilet | Check pain or constipation, flushing noise, seat height and foot support. | Choose a safe first step without coercion. |
| Tidying leads to distress | One simple action instead of a long instruction. | Choose which toy to put away. |

**Practise without rushing**, when possible. When the child is tired or unwell or the family is late, adult assistance may be the most suitable response.

## 3. The “child completes the last step” principle

NHS paediatric occupational therapy services describe *backward chaining*: the adult carries out most of an action, and the child is invited to complete the last step they can manage.

### Example: putting on a T-shirt

1. An adult helps put on the T-shirt and guide the child's arms into the sleeves.
2. If ready, the child **pulls down the bottom hem independently**.
3. Once comfortable, offer the preceding step of putting an arm through a sleeve.
4. The child may gradually take on more steps. If a step remains unpleasant or difficult, the previous level of help is still acceptable.

This is an **optional** approach to everyday activity, not an individual medical prescription or a protocol suitable for everyone.

Another option is **forward chaining**, where the child starts with the first familiar step and the adult assists with the remainder. Choose according to the child's preferences and which part is easier.

### Important points

- Never hold the child's hands forcefully to practise a movement.
- Do not change their clothing without explanation if they can choose a comfortable fit.
- Do not insist on progressing by a fixed deadline.
- Visual cards and adapted clothing may remain permanent supports.

## 4. Four everyday dressing activities

### Activity 1. Finishing a T-shirt

**Materials:** a soft, loose T-shirt and stable seating.

**Method:** the adult assists with harder stages while the child pulls down the hem. Later, if interested, the child can try placing an arm in a sleeve.

**Easier:** adjust one edge of a shirt already being worn.

**Optional challenge:** guide one arm into a convenient sleeve.

**Safety:** do not pull wrists or force the child to wear uncomfortable fabric.

### Activity 2. Pulling up a sock

**Materials:** a loose sock and stable seating with support.

**Method:** the adult puts the sock over the foot and heel. The child may pull up its top.

**Easier:** take off the sock or adjust its cuff.

**Challenge:** place the sock over the heel independently if positioning permits.

**Safety:** do not demand balancing on one leg or continuing through pain.

### Activity 3. Hook-and-loop shoes

**Materials:** familiar comfortable shoes with broad hook-and-loop fastenings.

**Method:** offer to open the fastening, insert the foot and close it. Assist with difficult actions.

**Easier:** the child closes a strap already in position.

**Challenge:** choose the shoes and open the fastening.

**Safety:** no need to use laces just to match an age expectation.

### Activity 4. A jacket sleeve

**Materials:** a comfortable jacket with sufficiently wide sleeves.

**Method:** the adult positions the jacket accessibly. The child may extend an arm into a sleeve.

**Easier:** pull an arm out of a prepared sleeve.

**Challenge:** choose which arm to begin with.

**Safety:** consider pain, sensory discomfort, tightness and garment labels.

## 5. Three activities around meals

Mealtimes are first and foremost for satisfying hunger, enjoyment and communication. **Do not turn eating into compulsory practice.** Provide needed assistance.

### Activity 5. Preparing a place at the table

**Materials:** a nonslip mat, stable cup and safe spoon.

<figure class="independence-table-setting-figure">
  <img src="/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/diagrams/child-daily-independence-table-setting-user-approved-v3.webp" alt="Four illustrated stages of setting a table: a mother and child place a mat, cup and spoon, with gradually greater participation by the child." width="1448" height="1086" loading="lazy" decoding="async" style="display:block;width:100%;height:auto;border-radius:16px" />
  <figcaption>A table-setting example. Completing one step counts as participation, and help remains available.</figcaption>
</figure>

1. Show the child where their place will be.
2. Offer to lay out the mat or place the cup.
3. If interested, suggest adding the spoon.
4. Recognise completing any chosen step as meaningful participation.

**Easier:** put the cup on a space already prepared.

**Safety:** avoid hot drinks, heavy glassware and unstable objects.

### Activity 6. Using a spoon

**Materials:** a suitable spoon, stable bowl and nonslip mat.

The adult can demonstrate the motion while the child decides whether to try. A thicker spoon handle or just the final part of a movement may be helpful.

**Easier:** hold a spoon or stir familiar food without demanding that it be eaten.

**Challenge:** scoop a small, comfortable amount independently if ready.

**Important:** do not force specific foods or quantities for practice. Recurrent coughing, choking, swallowing difficulties, pain or significant eating concerns need medical assessment.

### Activity 7. Holding a cup

**Materials:** a small cup with easy-to-grip handles or familiar adapted tableware.

Provide a small amount of a safe drink. The child can use both hands while the adult supports as needed.

**Easier:** hold an empty cup.

**Challenge:** reduce assistance while maintaining stability.

**Safety:** no hot liquids. Discuss repeated choking or coughing while drinking with a doctor.

## 6. Hygiene: handwashing, teeth and toileting

### Activity 8. Participating in handwashing

  <figcaption>Handwashing steps. The child can take on manageable parts while the adult helps as needed.</figcaption>
</figure>

**Easier:** the child does only the last step and chooses a towel. The adult ensures the other hygiene steps are completed.

**Challenge:** use a short visual sequence instead of some verbal prompts.

**Safety:** supervise as needed, use comfortable water and prevent slippery floors. Ticking a handwashing box does not necessarily mean hands are adequately clean.

### Activity 9. Participating in toothbrushing

The child may choose, hold and start using the brush, but **this does not mean adult help is no longer necessary**. AAP recommends adult supervision and assistance for young children. Choose age-appropriate toothpaste and quantities according to professional dental advice.

**Easier:** choose a brush, mirror or comfortable position.

**Challenge:** learn the stages while retaining final adult help.

**Safety:** never force the mouth open. Consult a dentist for pain, bleeding, intense fear or continuing difficulties.

### Activity 10. Becoming familiar with toileting

Toileting skills depend on bodily signals, movement, communication, readiness, bowel and bladder functioning, sensory reactions and environmental accessibility.

Begin **not necessarily by seating the child on the toilet**, but perhaps by viewing a toilet picture, learning where the bathroom is, choosing a way to request help or washing hands afterwards.

**Easier:** show a “toilet” card or take a towel after handwashing.

**Challenge:** add stages gradually, only in line with readiness and individual advice.

**Never:** forcibly hold a child on the toilet, punish accidents, shame them, restrict fluids or self-treat constipation. Consult a doctor for painful stools, constipation, urine retention, blood or other concerning symptoms.

## 7. Two simple household tasks

### Activity 11. Putting away one toy

**Materials:** a light, stable, open box at an accessible height and one large safe toy.

Let the child choose a toy to place in the box. The adult can hold the box nearby. One action is enough.

**Easier:** hand the toy to the adult.

**Challenge:** choose a place for a second toy if interested.

**Safety:** ensure the box is stable, light and accessible.

### Activity 12. Packing a bag

**Materials:** a light bag and one large safe item.

Invite the child to choose an item and put it into the bag while the adult holds it open. A second item can be added later if desired.

**Easier:** pass the item to the adult.

**Challenge:** locate the item independently using its photograph.

**Safety:** do not overload the bag or use long cords near the child's neck.

## 8. Adapting activities when movement or instructions are difficult

Independence can be supported through **changes to the environment**, not only movement training.

| Difficulty | Possible adaptation |
| --- | --- |
| Standing while dressing is uncomfortable | Stable support and getting dressed sitting down. |
| Small buttons are difficult | Hook-and-loop straps, larger closures or adult help. |
| Holding a plate is difficult | Nonslip mat and stable dishes. |
| A long instruction is hard to follow | One brief step, demonstration or a familiar photograph. |
| The child speaks little or not at all | Gestures, objects, pictures or AAC. |
| Bright light or bathroom noise causes discomfort | Change lighting or noise where possible. |
| Touch causes severe discomfort | Agree on support, try other materials or take a break. |

Continuing to use mobility aids, adult assistance or AAC is not a failure. Participation, safety and choice are the aims.

## 9. When can visual cues help?

Some children find seeing a sequence easier than remembering spoken instructions:

- **Dressing:** choose clothes -> sit comfortably -> dress with help -> adjust.
- **Hands:** water -> soap -> rinse -> towel.
- **Eating:** mat -> cup -> spoon -> request help.
- **Bag:** choose an item -> open -> put inside -> finished.

Pictures are not automatically meaningful to everyone. Consider **photographs of the family's own items**, familiar gestures or words in Russian, Kazakh or another language the child knows.

Do not require a child to look at every picture or follow all steps in order when doing so is uncomfortable or unsafe. Partial participation is valid.

## 10. Supporting skills without constant training

You can choose one household situation each week. **The weekly PDF plan is only an example, not a required schedule.**

| Day | Optional idea | What to observe |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | Adjust a T-shirt hem. | Were the fabric and sitting position comfortable? |
| Tuesday | Put a spoon on the table. | What helped with choosing a place? |
| Wednesday | Take a towel after handwashing. | Was another towel needed? |
| Thursday | Open a hook-and-loop fastening. | Which fastening works best? |
| Friday | Put a toy in a box. | Did the child want to participate? |
| Saturday | Pull up a sock. | Were the fabric and posture comfortable? |
| Sunday | Rest or choose a favourite step. | What would the family like to keep doing? |

If the child does not wish to participate on a given day, simply help them. **There are no “missed exercises” to make up.**

## 12. Noticing changes without scores

Rather than counting “correct” steps, consider:

- Which step does the child choose?
- What can they do with comfortable support?
- How do they ask for help or refuse?
- Which objects and conditions make participation easier?
- Is there pain, fatigue, fear or another difficulty?
- What would the family like to change?

Example:

> **Situation:** The child needs a jacket before a walk.  
> **What happened:** The adult held the jacket, and the child independently put one arm into the wide sleeve.  
> **Conditions:** The child was seated, the material was soft and nobody rushed.  
> **Child's message:** After the second sleeve, the child gestured “finished.”  
> **Question:** Could another fastening make closing the jacket easier?

This may help discuss real needs. It does not grade autism severity or prove the effectiveness of any method.

## 13. When home support is insufficient

Depending on the concern, consult a paediatrician, dentist or other appropriate professional if:

- The child **loses previously established** dressing, eating, movement or self-care skills.
- Pain, weakness, new obvious asymmetry or movement changes appear.
- There is recurrent coughing or choking while eating or drinking, or suspected swallowing problems.
- Severe constipation, painful stools, urination difficulties or other symptoms persist.
- Toothbrushing causes pain, bleeding or major difficulties.
- Daily participation remains seriously limited despite accessible adaptations.

**Choking, inability to breathe, sudden marked weakness or other acute emergencies** require urgent medical attention rather than home skills practice.

Occupational therapists may suggest adaptations, physiotherapists can assess movement challenges, and speech-language or feeding specialists may address relevant needs. The individual situation determines which consultation is suitable. See [“When to discuss development with a specialist”](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/ru/parents/kogda-obsudit-razvitie-rebenka-so-specialistom/) and [“Observing development at home”](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/ru/parents/kak-nablyudat-za-razvitiem-rebenka-doma/).

## 14. What sources support, and what cannot be promised

NHS paediatric occupational therapists recommend splitting dressing and other daily tasks into steps, adapting the environment and using demonstration and visual supports. CDC describes preschool children's participation in simple chores. AAP emphasises adult oral hygiene support and avoiding pressure around toileting. NICE advises considering individual sensory, adaptive, communication and accessibility needs for autistic children.

The **12 tasks in this article have not been demonstrated to form an effective treatment programme**. They cannot promise identical outcomes for all children or replace professional support. Consider health, age, available aids and personal preferences when observing change.

## Key points in one minute

**Choose one meaningful everyday step -> make the environment more accessible -> invite manageable participation -> help when needed -> respect choice, breaks and refusal.**

Independence is not the absence of assistance. It is the opportunity to participate in everyday life safely and accessibly.
