A child is unsure how to do a task and shows a card that says “Help”. The adult explains how to begin. If, in similar situations, the child gradually starts asking for help more often without prompting, the adult's response may be supporting this new skill. This is an example of how reinforcement can work.

Reinforcement can play a role in learning many different actions. It does not mean handing out prizes for obedience. To choose useful support, we need to understand which skill the child is learning, what happens after they use it, and whether the approach respects their wellbeing and choices.

## What is reinforcement, and how is it different from a reward?

In behavioural science, **reinforcement** is a consequence after which a particular action becomes more likely in similar circumstances. What matters is how behaviour changes over time, not whether the adult intended to reward the child.

A sticker, praise or a toy may be called a reward. But if a child receives stickers and does not start using the intended skill more often in similar situations, there is not yet a basis for calling those stickers effective reinforcement. A pleasant experience or one successful session is not enough to establish that. Conversely, answering a question, providing help or continuing an enjoyable game may support a skill without extra prizes.

Reinforcement is not a stand-alone treatment for autism. It is a learning principle that can be used within different forms of support. It does not replace finding out why a child is struggling, and it does not guarantee a result for every child.

## Positive reinforcement, negative reinforcement and punishment

In behavioural terminology, “positive” and “negative” describe whether something is added after an action or whether something is removed. They do not mean “good” and “bad”.

### Positive reinforcement

A consequence is added after an action, and the action becomes more likely in the future. For example, a child asks a question and an adult answers. If the child starts asking independently for needed information more often, the answer may have served as reinforcement.

Another example is a child requesting a toy through an accessible form of communication and receiving it. This does not mean a separate reward is needed each time. Getting what one has asked for may be a natural result of communication.

![Positive reinforcement in three panels: the child invites his mother to play, the mother joins a game with blocks, and we observe whether he starts inviting her more often over time.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/diagrams/reinforcement-positive-play-2026-11-10-en.webp)

### Negative reinforcement

After an action, an unpleasant or excessively difficult condition is reduced or ends, making that action more likely in the future. For example, a child communicates “It's too loud”, and the adult helps them move somewhere quiet. If the child starts using this understandable request more often, the reduction in noise may be reinforcing their communication.

**Negative reinforcement is not punishment.** If a child feels calmer after a single break, that does not yet show that reinforcement occurred. We need to observe whether they use the accessible request more often in similar situations. A necessary break or protection from sensory overload must not depend on whether the child says a particular word or points to a card. Adults must not deliberately increase noise, anxiety or other unpleasant conditions to produce a desired response.

![Negative reinforcement in three panels: the child reports that the noise is too loud, his mother helps him move somewhere quieter, and we observe whether he communicates discomfort more often in similar situations.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/diagrams/reinforcement-negative-noise-2026-11-10-en.webp)

### How is punishment different?

In the same terminology, *punishment* means a consequence after which a behaviour becomes less likely. This describes an observed effect, not a recommendation that parents use punitive procedures.

A decrease in shouting or refusal does not by itself prove that the child's wellbeing has improved. If someone is in pain, afraid or overwhelmed, the priority is to address the cause and help them communicate what they need. Support should not be aimed simply at suppressing reactions that adults find inconvenient.

## When reinforcement may be appropriate, and what to check first

Before choosing a reward or points system, work out what problem you are trying to solve. A child may not be completing a written task because the instructions are unclear, the task is too long, the room is noisy or they are tired. A more attractive reward will not resolve any of these causes.

[NICE CG170](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations) recommends considering physical and mental health, communication, the environment and changes in routine when responding to behaviour that challenges in autistic children and young people. A sudden change in behaviour or signs of pain or illness warrant medical assessment.

Choose a functional goal that matters to the child, such as requesting help, reporting discomfort, learning a particular action or being able to make a choice. Safe repetitive movements, such as hand movements, do not need to be changed merely because they look unusual.

If a child shouts or moves away from a task, we cannot assume what is maintaining the behaviour. A working explanation can be explored through observation and, where needed, a [functional behaviour assessment](/kz/en/parents/functional-behavior-assessment-guide/). Families should not experiment on their own with withholding help or changing adults' responses to dangerous behaviour.

## Natural consequences, preferences and the child's choices

Where possible, support should be linked to a natural and understandable outcome of the skill. A child asks someone to open a door, and the adult helps them open it. The child asks for an explanation and receives one. They invite someone to play, and that person may agree to join in. None of these events is automatically reinforcement. That depends on whether the particular skill becomes more likely over time.

Additional rewards can sometimes help, but they are not always necessary. One child may enjoy praise while another finds it uncomfortable. Interest in a toy can change depending on the situation. [Kamlowsky et al. (2025)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39498565/) studied five autistic children. Preference for an item did not always predict its reinforcing effect, and social interaction sometimes changed the outcome. This small study cannot establish a universal rule for all children.

Offer accessible choices and respect the child's response. For instance, ask whether they would prefer pencils or picture cards for an activity. Making a choice must not become a condition for receiving necessary support. If the child declines, try to understand why and adjust the activity when appropriate.

A child's interests can also make activities more engaging. If they like trains, trains might be used in counting examples. There is no need to take away favourite objects first so the child can later earn them back by following instructions.

## Tokens, stickers and other additional rewards

A **token economy** works by providing a symbol, such as a sticker or token, after a previously specified action. Accumulated symbols can then be exchanged for an agreed, optional outcome. This may be useful for some learning tasks, but it is not an essential part of support for autistic children.

Parents should check whether the child understands the arrangement, wants to participate and is not becoming distressed by it. They should also consider what is actually changing: a count of stickers earned does not show that a new skill has been learned. If ever more prizes must be promised, the system may not be helping the child use the skill independently.

[Kim et al. (2022)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34784784/) analysed 24 studies of token economies in kindergarten to grade-five classrooms. These included general and special education and were not restricted to autistic pupils, so their findings cannot automatically be transferred to programmes at home. In [Fiske et al. (2020)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31329284/), results differed across four autistic participants: tokens supported responding about as well as direct reinforcing consequences for two participants, but had weaker effects for the other two.

Tokens should not turn daily life into a continuous system of “earning” things. Food, water, toileting, medical attention, safety, sleep, communication and necessary rest must not be conditional on earning tokens.

## Worked example: helping a child ask for help

Imagine that a child receives a long written assignment, pushes the paper away and becomes upset. The adult wants to help the child learn to ask for assistance. This is an example of how to think about support, not an individual treatment programme.

1. **Understand the difficulty.** Check whether the instructions make sense, the task is manageable and there is no fatigue, pain or excessive noise. Adjust conditions when needed without waiting for a particular request.
2. **Make communication accessible.** The child may say “Help”, use a gesture, point to a card or use augmentative and alternative communication (AAC). Understandable requests and refusals should be accepted without demanding one “correct” form.
3. **Respond to the need.** When the child asks for help, the adult actually helps by explaining the first step, demonstrating it or adjusting the task. Assistance must not be withheld merely to practise requesting it.
4. **Observe changes.** Is it becoming easier for the child to communicate difficulties? Is distress decreasing? Can the child use this form of communication with other adults?
5. **Review the support.** If the child is still struggling, do not automatically increase the rewards. Re-examine the initial explanation, task demands and access to communication.

Here, the natural outcome of requesting help is receiving help. If the child increasingly uses an accessible way to ask independently, that outcome may have a reinforcing function. The value lies not in reducing visible behaviour at any cost, but in making communication and participation easier for the child.

For more on this, see [Functional communication training](/kz/en/parents/fct-functional-communication-training/).

## Safety, the right to refuse and assessing outcomes

**Basic needs and communication must never become rewards that are conditional on compliance.** A child must not be denied necessary water, food, toileting, pain treatment, safety or rest to secure task completion. If a tablet or another device is the child's primary means of AAC, it must not be taken away as punishment or provided only for “correct behaviour”.

A break during sensory overload may be an essential accommodation, not a prize. Refusal can signal pain, fear, fatigue, misunderstanding or disagreement. A suitable plan leaves room for the child to say “no”, request an explanation or stop an activity.

Evaluate more than how many tokens were handed out. Ask whether the child's everyday situation has improved:

- Is the child using the intended skill independently more often, such as asking for help?
- Can they express a need without constant prompting?
- Does the skill also appear at home, at school and with different people?
- Is the child finding things easier and feeling calmer? Has anxiety, avoidance or distress increased?
- Can the child still refuse and obtain essential support?

If a skill appears only when a reward is promised, the child feels pressured or the skill does not carry over into daily life, reconsider the plan. When a special reward is no longer needed, it may be faded gradually if natural consequences are already supporting the skill. Decisions should be guided by observation, not simply the number of sessions completed.

Self-injury, dangerous aggression or other serious risks require a professional safety plan. Do not deliberately provoke episodes, withhold necessities or test what happens when signals of distress are ignored.

## What research says and how to choose a professional in Kazakhstan

In [Hume et al. (2021)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33449225/), reinforcement met the review authors' criteria for an evidence-based practice. Their search of work published from 1990 to 2017 identified **106 reinforcement studies, including 104 single-case studies and 2 group studies**. This does not mean that 106 large trials confirmed the effectiveness of every reward system. Participants, methods and skills varied, and the review did not cover studies published after 2017.

[Cividini-Motta, Livingston and Efaw (2024)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38966267/) reviewed 10 articles about differential reinforcement during skill acquisition. In many included studies, more favourable reinforcement for independent responses was associated with more acquired responses or faster learning. This does not mean that children should be denied prompts or help. Any particular procedure must be considered in light of the learning goal and the studies' limitations. For related information, see [Differential reinforcement (Russian-language article)](/kz/ru/parents/differential-reinforcement/).

[NICE CG170](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations) addresses support for autistic children and young people. For behaviour that challenges, it recommends considering possible medical, communication and environmental causes, selecting goals relevant to quality of life, and reviewing outcomes. [NICE NG11](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng11/chapter/recommendations) also discusses reinforcement schedules and timely responses, but it applies to people with learning disabilities (intellectual disabilities) whose behaviour challenges. Its recommendations cannot simply be applied to all autistic children. Both documents are UK guidelines, not Kazakhstan's regulations for delivering support.

In Kazakhstan, reinforcement principles may be used within educational, psychological or behavioural support. The terms “ABA”, “tokens” or “reinforcement” alone do not demonstrate professional qualifications. Ask about a professional's training, experience with the child's specific needs, ability to explain the approach and plan for reviewing outcomes.

Before starting, ask **which skill is being supported, what is making it difficult now, why this particular consequence was chosen, what the child can always access without conditions, and when the plan will be reviewed**. A programme that continually demands compliance, restricts AAC or ignores an understandable refusal needs serious reconsideration.

Reinforcement is best discussed as one way to support a specific skill in a particular situation. It must not replace addressing pain, adapting the environment, providing accessible communication or respecting the child's choices.
