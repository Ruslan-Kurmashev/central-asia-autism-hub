A child's development does not happen only during structured activities. It can be seen in ordinary moments: asking for water, trying to zip a jacket, looking through a book, showing an interesting object or finding a comfortable way to calm down.

Parents often notice meaningful changes before they can describe them clearly. Short notes help preserve specific examples to share with a professional if needed. You do not need to test your child constantly, compare them with other children or turn family life into a series of tasks.

This guide explains **what to observe, how to record what you see and how to use your notes**. A printable observation diary is also available.

## 1. How observation differs from testing

**Observing** means noticing what your child does naturally in everyday situations: how they communicate, play, move, learn and take part in daily activities.

**Testing** involves presenting specific tasks, following defined procedures and assessing the responses. Standardised screening tools and professional diagnostic assessments have their own methods. A home diary cannot replace them.

For example, if your child brings you a cup and points towards water during breakfast, that is a useful observation about communication. You do not need to repeatedly ask them to perform the same action or say a particular word to "prove" they have a skill.

Observation is useful even when there is no particular concern. It can help you notice your child's interests, emerging abilities and the circumstances that make everyday life easier.

## 2. Which areas of development to notice

You do not need to track everything at once. Choose one or two everyday situations that are currently important for your child or family.

| Area | What you might notice in daily life | Example note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Communication and understanding | How your child requests, refuses or shares interests, responds when addressed and uses gestures, words, sounds, pictures or AAC. | "At lunch, led me to the jug and held out a cup. Nodded when I offered water." |
| Play and learning | What interests your child, how they explore objects, copy actions, try something new or invite others to play. | "Made a road from books to roll a toy car along and invited me with a gesture." |
| Movement | How your child moves around, holds objects and uses a spoon, pencil or fasteners. | "Walked upstairs holding the handrail, asked for my hand on the way down." |
| Independence | How your child participates in dressing, eating, tidying and other familiar tasks, and what support they need. | "Put on their hat independently, asked for help with the coat zipper." |
| Emotions and sensory experiences | Which situations cause discomfort and what helps your child settle and return to an activity. | "Moved to a quiet room when music was loud and returned after the volume went down." |

The same ability can be expressed in different ways. A child may request something using a word, gesture, communication card, AAC device or several methods together. What matters is the **purpose of communication**, not one required form.

For general age-related guidance, see [Child development from 2 months to 5 years](../child-development-milestones/). These milestones are not diagnostic tests either.

## 3. How to write a useful observation

A useful note answers four questions:

1. **When and where?** Record the date and familiar setting, such as home, a walk or preschool.
2. **What happened?** Describe exactly what your child did, including actions, words and gestures where possible, rather than a general label.
3. **What were the conditions?** For example, noise, tiredness, an unfamiliar room, a familiar person or assistance offered.
4. **What helped or changed?** Describe how others responded and what happened next.

### A fact and an interpretation are not the same

**Less useful:** "He ignores me and does not want to communicate."

**More useful:** "After we came home from preschool, I called to him twice from the next room. He continued building with blocks. When I came closer and showed him a cup, he looked at it and took it."

The second note does not claim to explain the behaviour. It gives concrete circumstances to discuss. Attention, language understanding, hearing or communication conditions may need consideration. A single event cannot identify the explanation.

**Less useful:** "She cannot play at all."

**More useful:** "On our walk, she spent five minutes sorting pebbles by size and then showed me the largest one. She did not join our ball game today."

This records both the child's interests and a specific point for further observation.

## 4. How to observe without adding unnecessary pressure

There is no mandatory number of notes per day or scientifically established period after which home observations produce a medical conclusion.

For convenience, you could start with this approach:

- choose one familiar situation such as breakfast, a walk or playtime
- record only meaningful moments, emerging skills, recurring difficulties or conditions that help your child
- note at least one strength or successful way of participating alongside any concerns
- review your notes once a week and highlight what you would like to discuss with an educator or healthcare professional
- take a break when needed: a diary should be helpful rather than a source of constant anxiety.

**This is an AutismHub suggestion for organising notes, not an evidence-validated observation protocol.** Families can write less often, more often or not at all.

If your child uses several languages, record communication in each of them. Do not evaluate overall communication only by counting words in one language.

If your child was born prematurely, interpreting milestones may require the use of corrected age. Discuss this with a healthcare professional, as chronological age alone may not always allow an appropriate comparison.

## 5. Three examples from everyday life

### Example 1. A child wants a drink but does not say "water"

**Situation:** At lunch, the child approaches the fridge, takes an adult's hand and leads them to a bottle.

**What you can record:** "Communicated a wish to drink using an action and a gesture. Reached for the glass when the adult offered water."

**What not to conclude:** "The child cannot speak, so they cannot communicate." Communication goes beyond speech. If you have concerns about communication development, discuss all the ways your child expresses needs.

### Example 2. A child joins an activity at preschool but declines a similar one at home

**Situation:** An educator reports that the child completed a jigsaw puzzle. At home, the child turns away from a puzzle and leaves.

**What you can record:** "Completed a task with an educator at preschool. After a busy day, put the puzzle aside at home and chose quiet play."

**What to clarify:** Time of day, tiredness, environment, interest, support and demands in each situation.

**What not to conclude:** "If they did it at preschool, they are deliberately disobeying at home." A child's ability to participate can depend on context.

### Example 3. Dressing independently varies from day to day

**Situation:** On the weekend, the child puts on their shoes without help. On a weekday morning before preschool, they ask for assistance.

**What you can record:** "Put on shoes independently on Saturday. On Monday morning we were in a hurry. Asked for help, then closed the hook-and-loop fastener after a prompt."

**What it shows:** The child has demonstrated the action, but time, demands, conditions and available support can affect participation.

## 7. How to prepare for a conversation with a professional

Notes can help replace a vague statement with a concrete example. Instead of "He never listens", you might say, "He brings his shoes when asked in a quiet room, but often does not respond to a spoken request in a noisy group."

Before an appointment, consider preparing:

- two or three examples of your child's existing strengths
- one to three questions that concern you most
- when you first noticed a change, how often it happens and in which situations
- which communication methods and types of support help your child
- observations from preschool or other adults close to your child, if available
- information about any previously established skills that have been lost.

You can ask directly: "Should we consider standardised developmental screening, a hearing test or another developmental assessment?", "Which additional observations would help?" and "When should we revisit this question?"

Next steps depend on the child's age, general health and individual needs. If your family is in Kazakhstan and is unsure where to begin, see [Where to seek help if you are concerned about your child's development in Kazakhstan](../../help-kazakhstan/child-development-concerns-kazakhstan/).

## 8. When not to wait and keep taking notes

Keeping a diary must not delay medical care.

**Seek medical advice without prolonged delay** if your child stops regularly using words, ways of interacting or movement skills that they previously used reliably. Tell the professional exactly what changed and when you first noticed it. Sudden severe symptoms or a rapid deterioration require urgent medical assessment.

You do not need to collect a week or a month of notes before your concern can be taken seriously. A parent's concern is itself a valid reason to ask about development.

For more detail, see [When should you discuss your child's development with a professional?](../when-to-discuss-child-development/).

## 9. What the diary cannot determine

Home observations cannot establish:

- whether your child's development is "normal" or "abnormal"
- whether they are autistic, have a language delay or another condition
- why your child behaves in a particular way
- whether a particular therapy is needed
- how effective an intervention is compared with alternatives.

These questions may require a medical or multidisciplinary assessment and, in some cases, validated screening tools. Even a detailed diary has no diagnostic accuracy on its own.

## What to remember

The main aim of observation is to **understand your child better and help other adults see specific situations**, not to rate their achievements every day.

A simple sequence is enough: **notice -> describe -> discuss when needed**. Remember to include what interests your child, the ways they already communicate and the conditions that make it easier for them to participate in everyday life.
