## What does the word autism mean?

The World Health Organization (WHO) describes autism as a diverse group of conditions related to the development of the brain. Autism is characterised by differences in social interaction and communication, alongside repetitive or inflexible patterns of behaviour, interests and activities. Some people also have unusual responses to sounds, light, touch, smells or other sensory experiences.

The term *neurodevelopment* refers to the development of the brain and nervous system. It does not describe a person's character, worth or upbringing. According to the WHO, various genetic and environmental factors affecting early brain development may contribute to autism. Researchers continue to investigate how these factors interact. General information cannot establish why a particular person is autistic.

## Why is autism described as a spectrum?

It is more useful to think of the spectrum not as a single line from 'mild' to 'severe', but as different combinations of characteristics, abilities and needs. For example, someone may speak fluently but find unspoken social rules difficult to understand, while another person may communicate in a very different way. Responses to change, sensory sensitivities, interests, learning and everyday independence can also vary considerably.

A person's abilities and needs may change over time and depend on the situation, environmental demands, available support, health and other factors. A brief description or a diagnosis alone therefore cannot give a complete picture of the person or accurately predict their future.

## What might other people notice?

Autistic characteristics may become apparent in different areas:

- communication and reciprocal social interaction
- understanding non-verbal signals or unspoken rules
- a preference for predictability and difficulty with unexpected changes
- repetitive actions, movements or ways of using objects
- particularly intense or focused interests
- unusual sensitivity or responses to sounds, light, touch and other sensations.

This is not a diagnostic checklist. Not every autistic person has all these characteristics, and individual characteristics may also occur for other reasons. NICE recommends considering the possibility of autism in the broader context of a child's development and behaviour. Individual signs do not establish a diagnosis on their own.

## How is autism diagnosed?

Autism cannot be diagnosed from one characteristic, a single test result or a screening questionnaire alone. Screening tools can help organise observations and indicate whether further assessment may be needed. However, a positive screening result is not a diagnosis, and a negative result does not rule out autism.

According to NICE, a comprehensive diagnostic assessment of children and young people usually considers developmental history, observations of communication and behaviour, medical information, possible co-occurring conditions, and the child's strengths, skills and needs. The professionals involved and the referral process vary between healthcare systems.

## What does a diagnosis not tell us?

A diagnosis alone does not determine:

- a person's intellectual functioning or ability to learn
- how a person communicates
- their independence in daily life or the support they need
- the one 'correct' way to teach or support them
- a child's exact future.

To understand an individual's needs, it is important to consider their own profile: what they find easier, what presents difficulties, how they can communicate, which environments help, and what support is actually useful.

## If a family has questions about a child's development

General information on the internet cannot determine whether a child is autistic. If differences in development, communication or behaviour raise ongoing questions, families can discuss them with an appropriately qualified professional who can consider the child's overall development. Having concerns does not automatically mean that the child has a particular diagnosis, because individual signs may have different explanations.

Before an appointment, it may help to write down specific observations: what concerns you, when you first noticed it, and whether it occurs at home and in other settings. Previous assessment records or professional reports can also be brought along. Such notes help explain the child's development but are not a way to make a diagnosis independently.

## What is important to remember?

Autism can look different from one person to another. Rather than looking for one 'typical' image, it is more helpful to get to know the individual: their communication, strengths, difficulties, preferences and needs. A definition can describe a general set of characteristics, but it does not replace an individual assessment or describe the whole person.
