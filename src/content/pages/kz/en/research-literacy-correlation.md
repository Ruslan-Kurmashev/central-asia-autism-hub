## 1. Occurring together does not mean one causes the other

Imagine a hot summer. On hot days, people buy ice cream more often and visit swimming pools more often. Comparing the figures over a month might reveal a correlation. But ice cream does not make people swim. A third factor, air temperature, influences both measures.

Correlation means that two measures are statistically associated. Causation means that changing one factor really brings about a change in another under certain conditions. The two are not automatically equivalent.

## 2. Three explanations for the same association

*The connections in the diagram represent competing hypotheses, not proof.*

| What we observed | Possible explanation | What we need to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A occurs more often alongside B | A influences B | Did A come first, and have alternative explanations been ruled out? |
| A occurs more often alongside B | B influences A | Could the relationship run in the opposite direction? |
| A occurs more often alongside B | C influences both A and B | Was the third factor measured? |
| A occurs more often alongside B | Selection and measurement errors | Is the sample representative, and were the same recording methods used? |

## 3. An example involving alternative communication

A centre notices that children who use AAC are more likely to have substantial difficulties with spoken language. It would be wrong to conclude that AAC causes those difficulties.

A more plausible alternative is that augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) is more often offered to people who already have significant communication needs. The reason for offering a device is related to their starting circumstances, not necessarily to the effect of the device.

This is known as confounding by indication. To assess the effect of AAC, researchers need to consider baseline differences, meaningful communication outcomes and the study design. The association alone does not tell us whether AAC is effective.

## 4. Reverse causation: the arrow points the other way

Suppose that in a large group of families, those who visit specialists more frequently also report more developmental difficulties. A hasty interpretation would be that consultations make the situation worse.

But the order may be different: families seek help more frequently because they face greater difficulties. A similar example is that more fire engines attend larger fires. It does not follow that the fire engines made the fires larger.

## 5. A confounding factor influences both measures

Researchers notice that children who attend additional classes are more likely to receive high school grades. However, attendance may be related to family income, adults' available time, transport, initial skills and school quality.

Even if researchers adjust statistically for some of these differences, unmeasured factors may remain. An observational comparison does not become a randomised trial merely because statistical adjustments have been made.

## 6. Happening after something does not mean happening because of it

A parent may notice that a new reaction appeared after a particular event. But a sequence in time does not rule out other causes, coincidence or the gradual development of a condition.

When a child's health is concerned, a medical cause should not be determined independently just because two events occurred close together in time. New symptoms require appropriate professional assessment.

## 7. Selection bias: who was actually observed?

If a study collected responses only from followers of one centre, the sample may contain more families who are experiencing difficulties and actively seeking help. The finding may not reflect the experiences of all autistic people in Kazakhstan.

Similarly, if only children who have received a diagnosis are counted, regional differences may depend on access to diagnostic services and registration rules rather than the true number of autistic people.

## 8. What does a correlation coefficient mean?

Pearson's correlation coefficient, *r*, ordinarily ranges from -1 to +1. A positive value means that two quantitative variables tend to increase together. A negative value means that as one increases, the other tends to decrease. A value close to zero indicates no strong linear relationship, but does not necessarily mean that there is no relationship of any kind.

Crucially, r = 0.8 does not mean that A causes B in 80% of cases. Nor does the coefficient explain the mechanism, the order of events or the quality of the measurements.

## 9. Why this question matters particularly in autism research

Autism research identifies associations with various genetic variants, developmental characteristics, circumstances during pregnancy and the composition of the microbiome. For many such associations, causal mechanisms have either been established only in specific instances or are still being investigated.

An overall association cannot justify blaming an individual parent for doing something wrong, or promote a paid intervention that supposedly removes the cause of autism.

## 10. How researchers strengthen a causal conclusion

Researchers examine the order of events, gather information on alternative factors, use appropriate comparisons and statistical models, and draw on natural experiments or randomised studies where acceptable. Other research groups then try to reproduce the results.

No single method guarantees a correct causal conclusion. Ethical constraints are particularly important when children and potentially harmful exposures are involved.

## 11. An exercise to check your understanding

A report says that schools with more support specialists have more registered children with special educational needs.

Can we conclude that the specialists create those needs? No. One possible explanation is that schools hire more specialists because there are more children with support needs. Their record-keeping may also be better.

## 12. A reader's checklist

- [ ] Is this an association or an actual tested causal relationship?
- [ ] Which event preceded the other?
- [ ] Could there be reverse causation?
- [ ] What third factors might influence both measures?
- [ ] How were participants recruited and the measures recorded?
- [ ] Have the authors phrased their conclusions cautiously?

The main point: identifying a correlation is a useful first step. Explaining its cause is a separate and considerably more difficult scientific task.
