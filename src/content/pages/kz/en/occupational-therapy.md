## What is occupational therapy?

Occupational therapy (OT) helps people participate in everyday activities that are important or necessary to them. In this context, the word occupation means activity, not just paid work. For a child, this may include dressing, playing, eating, participating in lessons or resting. For an adolescent, it may also include preparing food, shopping and using transport.

An occupational therapist considers the person's abilities, the demands of a particular task, the environment and available support. For example, a child may find it difficult to fasten a jacket. Options include teaching the child to use the fastener, choosing a more accessible zip or changing how the child dresses. The choice depends on why the difficulty occurs and what outcome matters to the child.

Occupational therapy encompasses different interventions. It is a professional field, not a single method of treating autism. Its effectiveness cannot be assessed without specifying the goal and approach used.

## When might support from an occupational therapist be useful?

A consultation may be appropriate when a person has difficulties with self-care, using objects, organising actions, participating in school life or playing. Sometimes a person can perform an activity, but it requires considerable effort, causes discomfort or is only possible with constant adult assistance.

A goal should describe a specific change. Instead of 'improve fine motor skills', a goal might be to use preferred cutlery during a family meal. Instead of 'normalise the sensory system', it is more useful to establish what makes the school dining room difficult for the child to use and what changes could make it accessible.

An autism diagnosis does not automatically mean that someone needs occupational therapy sessions. If existing adaptations and support already enable participation in the activities that matter, an additional programme may not be necessary.

## How does work with the specialist proceed?

The exact process depends on the organisation and goal. It usually includes the following steps.

1. Initial assessment. The specialist discusses everyday difficulties with the child and family, observes activities and assesses the conditions in which they take place. Appropriate functional assessment tools are used when necessary.
2. Goal setting. Together, they select a meaningful task and define what would count as a useful outcome.
3. Selection of support. This may involve teaching an activity, adapting an object or space, using an assistive device, changing the sequence of actions or teaching caregivers.
4. Practice in everyday life. The family and, when necessary, educators agree on accessible ways to provide support without turning the child's entire day into a session.
5. Reassessment. At an agreed time, the specialist compares the outcome with baseline and discusses whether to continue, change or conclude the work.

Assessment of everyday activities does not replace autism diagnosis or medical assessment when new symptoms arise.

## Methods and practical examples

The examples below illustrate possible approaches. They are not an individual treatment plan or a list of mandatory exercises.

| Everyday difficulty | Possible approach |
| --- | --- |
| Difficulty fastening clothing | Identify the movement that causes difficulty and choose accessible clothing or a suitable way of fastening it. |
| Difficulty using a spoon | Assess grip, body position and accessibility of cutlery, and choose a suitable aid. |
| Difficulty writing or working at a desk | Consider body position, desk height, writing tools and accessible alternatives to handwriting. |
| Discomfort when brushing teeth | Rule out a medical cause when there are signs of pain, and choose tolerable equipment and a clear sequence. |
| Difficulty starting or finishing a household task | Organise objects and use a list of steps, a timer or a reminder as needed. |
| Difficulty participating in play | Adapt the accessibility of materials and play conditions to the child's interests. |

Some tasks require practice of sequential actions, whereas others are more readily addressed through adaptation. For example, if a child can dress using clothing without difficult fasteners, such clothing can be used permanently without waiting for the child to master every type of fastener.

Detailed teaching approaches are discussed in [Teaching daily living skills in autism](/kz/en/parents/obuchenie-povsednevnym-navykam/).

## What does the research say?

### AOTA practice guideline, 2024

[Patten and colleagues (2024)](https://doi.org/10.5014/ajot.2024.078301) developed occupational therapy recommendations for autistic people across the lifespan, drawing on systematic reviews that included 98 publications. The authors address participation in meaningful activities, self-determination, environmental accessibility and caregiver education. Recommendations relate to specific interventions and outcomes. The number of publications does not indicate how much an individual child's skills will improve.

The [systematic review brief by Baker and colleagues (2023)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37562056/) focuses on interventions for basic and instrumental daily living skills in children and adolescents. It informs discussion of particular support strategies rather than providing an assessment of the entire profession.

### Umbrella review, 2026

[Marcilla-Jordá and colleagues (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.reia.2026.202866) examined 10 systematic reviews and extracted 43 randomised controlled trials (RCTs). The professional profile was unclear in 54.5% of the RCTs. The authors identified 109 different assessment instruments. This makes it difficult to compare outcomes and attribute all observed effects specifically to occupational therapists' work.

### Family-centred occupational therapy

The [study by Hsieh and colleagues (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1177/19437676261464088) involved 19 autistic children aged 2-6 and their parents in Taiwan. Nine families received a programme including parent training, while ten received usual care at other facilities. After six months, the authors reported greater improvements in certain daily living skill measures and attention during play in the programme group. No significant differences were found in parental stress or family outcomes.

Families chose their groups according to travel convenience, so other factors may also have contributed to differences. The small sample and absence of random allocation prevent confident conclusions about a causal programme effect or generalisation to all families.

A [2026 systematic review of self-care](https://doi.org/10.1111/cch.70285) examines factors influencing self-care and support approaches for children and young people with developmental disabilities. Its population extends beyond autism, so it does not provide a separate assessment of occupational therapy effectiveness for all autistic children.

## Occupational therapy and sensory integration

Ayres Sensory Integration (ASI) is a specific approach with its own requirements for assessment, practitioner training and delivery. Some occupational therapists use ASI, but not all occupational therapy is sensory integration. The presence of swings, a sensory room or exercises with different textures does not in itself demonstrate that ASI is being delivered.

In the [SenITA trial (Randell and colleagues, 2022)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35766242/), 138 children were randomly allocated to sensory integration or usual care. No significant overall advantage of the intervention was detected at 6 or 12 months.

A [systematic review by Acuña and colleagues (2025)](https://doi.org/10.5014/ajot.2025.051023) noted positive results in individual RCTs for achievement of personalised goals. Differences between outcomes and studies require cautious interpretation. Results for specific functional goals do not imply improvement in all aspects of autism.

Environmental adaptation may be useful regardless of whether ASI is provided. For example, if noise in a room is difficult to tolerate, a quieter location or appropriate hearing protection could be considered. The choice depends on the child's preferences and safety. [NICE guideline CG170](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations) recommends accounting for individual sensitivity to environmental conditions.

Further reading: [Ayres Sensory Integration](/kz/en/parents/ayres-sensory-integration/) and [sensory differences and environmental adaptation](/kz/en/parents/sensory-differences-and-environmental-adjustments/).

## How does an occupational therapist differ from other specialists?

| Specialist | Main area of work |
| --- | --- |
| Occupational therapist | Participation in everyday activities, selection of ways to perform tasks and adaptations. |
| Movement rehabilitation specialist | Movement, physical function and mobility. |
| Speech and language therapist | Speech, language and communication. Swallowing work requires relevant training and authorisation. |
| Special education teacher | Educational needs, learning and access to educational activities. |
| Psychologist | Psychological wellbeing, emotional and other psychological difficulties within their qualifications. |

This is a general comparison of areas of work, not a legal list of professional powers in Kazakhstan. Specialists may collaborate on the same task. For example, participating in a school lunch may require accessible cutlery, communication aids and an organised environment. Signs of swallowing difficulties require appropriate medical assessment.

## Safety, communication and consent

Before starting sessions, establish whether pain, intense fear, fatigue or an inaccessible environment is affecting the child. Difficulties should not automatically be attributed to autism or unwillingness to follow adult requests.

If a child uses augmentative and alternative communication (AAC), it must remain accessible. The child needs a reliable way to report pain, refuse, ask for help or request a break. Privacy, consent and the minimum necessary assistance are especially important when dressing, using the toilet and attending to personal hygiene. Physical restraint must not be used to teach a skill, and basic needs must not be withheld as a condition of completing a task.

Refusal of food, frequent choking or coughing during eating, swallowing difficulties, severe constipation, pain during urination, new pain or loss of a previously acquired skill require medical assessment. Sudden weakness, acute breathing difficulties or disturbances of consciousness require emergency medical assistance. Exercises do not replace medical assessment.

Safety must be assessed separately when working with hot water, sharp objects, equipment and potentially dangerous aids. Performing a dangerous activity independently should not become a goal merely to achieve a formal measure of independence.

## How to assess outcomes

It is useful to record baseline performance before sessions begin. For example, a child wants to put on a jacket independently before going outside but asks for help with the zip. Together with the specialist, the family can identify which parts of the activity are already accessible and what specifically needs to change.

| Measure | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Performance | Which parts of the activity the person performs independently. |
| Support | What adult assistance and ongoing adaptations are needed. |
| Comfort | Whether pain, marked discomfort or a desire to stop occurs. |
| Generalisation | Whether the activity is possible in other suitable settings. |
| Maintenance | Whether the outcome remains accessible after an agreed period. |

If a child independently uses visual instructions, adapted clothing or an assistive device, this can be a meaningful outcome in its own right. Useful supports need not be removed simply to achieve a measure of 'without assistance'.

The number of sessions attended or improved performance on a particular exercise does not replace assessment of real everyday activities. If no changes are evident, the barriers, goal and chosen approach should be reconsidered with the specialist.

## Occupational therapy in Kazakhstan

Occupational therapy specialists are listed in Kazakhstan's official [list of regulated professions](https://www.gov.kz/org/enbek/documents/details/146227?lang=ru) in the healthcare sector. Professional functions are described in the [Kinesiotherapy. Occupational therapy professional standard](https://career.enbek.kz/ru/professionalstandart/402) and the [occupational therapist profile](https://career.enbek.kz/ru/professionalstandart/402/2945) of the National Qualifications System. The standard was approved as part of the professional standards under Order No. 46 of 25 January 2024.

Order No. ҚР ДСМ-218/2020 governs the list of specialties and specialisations subject to certification. [Order No. 68 of 24 June 2026](https://adilet.zan.kz/rus/docs/G26RR000068) restated the list. Occupational therapy appears in that list.

The [standard for organising medical rehabilitation](https://adilet.zan.kz/rus/docs/V2300032263) and its [2024 amendments](https://adilet.zan.kz/rus/docs/V2400035511) provide for the participation of occupational therapists with pedagogical education in the rehabilitation team. Therefore, the absence of a medical degree alone does not establish that the work is unlawful. However, an educational degree or private course certificate does not automatically authorise someone to perform any medical procedure. The specialist's qualifications and documents, the nature of the service and applicable organisational requirements must be verified.

Teaching daily living skills in a family or educational organisation should not automatically be equated with medical occupational therapy. International recommendations do not replace national service requirements.

## How to choose a specialist

Before starting work, you can ask the following questions:

- What are your professional qualifications, and which documents establish your authority to provide this service?
- What everyday difficulties do you propose to assess, and how will you establish baseline performance?
- What is the chosen intervention called, why is it appropriate for the goal, and what are the limitations of its evidence?
- How will the child be able to refuse, report pain or ask for a break?
- Which adaptations may remain in place, and what can be done at home without placing excessive demands on the family?
- When and how will we assess outcomes in everyday life, rather than only in the clinic?

Promises to cure autism, the same programme for every child, disregard for refusal or pain, demands to remove AAC, and the absence of a clear goal, baseline assessment or review date are reasons for concern. A specialist's formal qualifications and the effectiveness of a specific intervention require separate assessment.
