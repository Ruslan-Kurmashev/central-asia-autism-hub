## What does toileting involve?

Using the toilet consists of several actions. The child needs to recognise the need, communicate it or go to the toilet, manage clothing, get into a comfortable position, urinate or open their bowels, clean their skin, dress and wash their hands. Not all these actions are acquired at the same time.

For example, one child may use a potty after a reminder but not independently communicate the need. Another can urinate in the toilet but only opens their bowels in a nappy. These situations require different goals.

Start with the action that currently matters most to the child's comfort, health and daily life. Ongoing use of an adapted seat, a communication aid or adult assistance does not diminish the value of an acquired skill.

## When to seek medical advice first

Do not start or intensify training in an attempt to resolve pain, constipation or other medical symptoms. An autistic child may communicate discomfort through gestures, changes in usual activity, refusal to use the toilet or behaviour. These signs are non-specific and cannot establish a diagnosis on their own.

### Make an appointment with a paediatrician

Discuss recurrent constipation, hard or painful stools, stool withholding, regular soiling, persistent urinary difficulties or persistent incontinence. Do not delay seeking advice if difficulties continue. If the child's condition suddenly changes, follow the more urgent guidance below.

### Seek same-day medical assessment

For new painful urination, visible blood in the urine, fever with urinary symptoms, new incontinence accompanied by signs of illness or unexplained blood in the stool, contact a medical service for timely assessment. Unusually intense thirst, frequent urination, weight loss or marked fatigue may be signs of diabetes. Suspected type 1 diabetes in a child requires same-day medical assessment, even if the child appears relatively well.

### When emergency help is needed

Sudden inability to urinate, severe acute abdominal pain, marked abdominal distension with vomiting, altered consciousness or a severe deterioration in general condition require emergency medical attention. Unusual thirst and frequent urination combined with vomiting, deep or rapid breathing or drowsiness also require emergency assessment. In Kazakhstan, call 103 for an ambulance or 112 in an emergency.

These examples do not cover every possible warning sign. If you are unsure, particularly with a young child or a child who finds it difficult to communicate pain, seek medical help. Do not wait until an observation diary or course of sessions is complete.

## How to identify what is making toileting difficult

Observe an everyday situation. Distinguish physical, sensory, communication and organisational barriers. Sometimes they occur together.

| What happens | What to check |
| --- | --- |
| Afraid to enter the toilet | Flushing noise, hand dryers, lighting, smells and previous unpleasant experiences. |
| Does not want to sit down | Pain, seat stability, foot support and fear of falling. |
| Does not communicate the need | Access to a gesture, word, symbol or another way of communicating. |
| Difficulty removing clothing | Fasteners, accessibility of clothing and motor abilities. |
| Urinates in the toilet but passes stool only in a nappy | Possible constipation, previous painful experiences, familiar posture and conditions. |
| Uses the toilet only at home | Accessibility, noise, privacy and a familiar form of support elsewhere. |
| Incontinence has returned | New medical symptoms and changes in routine or environment. |

If you cannot identify the cause, discuss your observations with a paediatrician and, if necessary, a specialist in teaching daily living skills. Refusal should not be taken as proof that the child simply does not want to cooperate.

## Preparing for training

### Checklist for parents

- There are no new medical symptoms requiring assessment. For known conditions, a care plan has been agreed with a doctor.
- The child can reach the toilet safely with the support they need.
- The potty or seat is stable and the child's feet have suitable support.
- The child can manage clothing independently or with assistance.
- The child can communicate the need, pain, refusal or a request for help.
- Adults have agreed on clear words, ways of providing support and responses to incontinence.
- There is time for calm practice and an opportunity to stop if discomfort arises.

There is no need to wait until the child can perform all these actions independently. The checklist describes conditions adults should provide, not eligibility criteria for starting training.

### Seven-day observation diary

If there are no symptoms requiring medical attention, observe the usual routine for a few days without deliberately changing it. Seven days is a convenient reference period for organising records, not a mandatory diagnostic period.

Record the time, event, support and relevant observations. Note urination and bowel movements separately. If a signal or discomfort was not noticed, record that rather than assuming it did not occur.

| Time | Event | Support | Observations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 08:10 | Bowel movement in nappy | No prompt | Straining. |
| 10:25 | Urination outside toilet | No prompt | No signal noticed. |
| 12:40 | Urination in toilet | Reminder | No visible discomfort. |

These are fictional example entries. Do not wake the child, restrict fluids or deliberately delay toilet access in order to complete the diary.

## How to organise training at home

### Choose one goal

For example, a child can sit on the potty but does not know how to ask for help. A goal could be to use an accessible gesture or symbol to communicate 'toilet'. If clothing is the barrier, begin by teaching the child to take off and put on comfortable trousers in an everyday situation.

The goal should not require complete independence within a specified number of days. If medical care or specialist equipment is needed, provide it independently of the training goal.

### Prepare the space

Choose a stable seat or potty with suitable support. If needed, use a non-slip footrest and clothing that is easy to remove. Consider noise, smells, lighting, temperature and privacy. If practical, flush after a child who is sensitive to noise has moved away.

In the presence of significant motor difficulties or a risk of falling, discuss suitable equipment and a safe method of transferring with an appropriate specialist.

### Explain the necessary actions

Use short, familiar instructions. Actions involving clothing, using the toilet, hygiene and handwashing can be taught separately. It is not necessary to teach the whole sequence at once. Support may include words, gestures or a familiar visual symbol already used in the child's communication system.

Offer help when needed and gradually reduce unnecessary prompts. Do not remove an adaptation that allows a task to be performed safely or comfortably.

## Scheduling, prompts and encouragement

If a child still finds it difficult to notice or communicate the need, a calm reminder at an appropriate time may help. Use your observations and the usual routine. Do not copy intervals from research protocols or restrict toilet access between scheduled visits. If the child refuses, identify the barrier and offer a different approach.

Positive reinforcement means that a specific action is followed by an outcome that matters to the child. For example, calm praise if the child enjoys it or a choice of the next activity. Small successes such as communicating the need or asking for help can be acknowledged. Do not make water, food, rest, communication, toilet access or necessary assistance conditional on the outcome.

If urination occurs outside the toilet, calmly help the child change and clean themselves. Do not require cleaning as punishment. Consider whether pain, an inaccessible toilet or a communication difficulty has occurred. If episodes suddenly become more frequent, discuss this with a doctor.

Do not use physical restraint, prolonged sitting, punishment, ridicule, forced drinking, water restriction or abruptly remove a necessary nappy without a suitable alternative. More intensive training does not necessarily mean better results.

Further reading: [how to use reinforcement ethically](/kz/en/parents/reinforcement-parent-guide/).

## Bowel movement difficulties and stool withholding

Some children urinate in the toilet but prefer to pass stool in a nappy. Sometimes this is related to constipation or previous pain. Other possible factors include an uncomfortable posture, fear of flushing, the need for privacy or a familiar way of opening the bowels.

When withholding stool, a child may strain, clench their buttocks, stand on tiptoe or hide. These signs do not establish the cause. Even frequent small episodes of soiling can sometimes be related to constipation rather than a lack of hygiene skills. Constipation and painful bowel movements should be discussed with a paediatrician.

Do not abruptly remove familiar support while the child needs it. First ensure comfort, accessibility and medical assessment when symptoms occur. Once medical barriers have been addressed, work with a specialist to choose an achievable change, such as making the toilet more familiar or allowing the child to choose a safe time to practise. Do not deliberately withhold stool for training purposes or demand that the child open their bowels on command.

Treatment of constipation is determined by a doctor. This article does not provide doses, regimens for laxatives, enemas or other medical procedures.

Sources: [NICE CG99](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg99/chapter/Recommendations), [Furuta and colleagues, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23118260/) and [ERIC](https://eric.org.uk/children-with-additional-needs/toileting-support-for-autistic-children/).

## Communication, consent and privacy

Spoken language is not a prerequisite for using the toilet. If a child uses augmentative and alternative communication (AAC), it must remain accessible, including in the toilet. Choose a method that can be used safely in that space.

Explain assistance with clothing or hygiene before touching the child, and respect refusal and signs of discomfort. Provide necessary hygiene respectfully, with minimal assistance and privacy. If safe care is difficult to arrange without significant distress, discuss this with an appropriate specialist.

Do not photograph or film intimate procedures for reporting purposes. Do not discuss episodes of incontinence in front of other children or unrelated adults. For older children and adolescents, it is particularly important to agree on support with them and respect their right to personal space.

## Common questions from parents

### The child does not notice the need or cannot communicate it in time

Do not require the child to first learn to recognise every bodily signal. Ensure access to the toilet and a clear way to communicate, and observe the usual routine. Seek medical advice for new or persistent urinary symptoms. A specialist can help select support that takes account of motor, sensory and communication needs.

### The child is afraid of the toilet or flushing noise

Check seat stability, noise and possible pain. Allow the child to become familiar with the space without requiring immediate use of the toilet. Do not deliberately activate a frightening sound for habituation. If fear increases, reduce demands and identify the causes.

### The child cannot wipe independently

Teach hygiene separately while providing necessary assistance. Accessible toilet paper, comfortable clothing and a clear explanation may make the task easier. Do not leave the child without necessary care for the sake of independence. Seek medical advice for skin irritation, pain or other symptoms.

### The child stopped using the toilet after successful training

Do not assume intentional refusal. New incontinence may be related to constipation, infection, changes in conditions or other causes. If there is a sudden deterioration, medical assessment comes first. Previous support can then be reconsidered. Urgency depends on the symptoms described above.

### The child uses the toilet during the day but wets the bed at night

Daytime skills and nighttime dryness are distinct goals. Do not punish bedwetting. If the family is concerned, nighttime wetting returns after a dry period or other symptoms occur, discuss the situation with a paediatrician. Do not independently restrict fluids to keep the bed dry. See [NICE CG111](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg111/chapter/Recommendations).

### The child does not use the toilet at school or in public places

Establish in advance whether a toilet is accessible, who can assist when needed, how the child can communicate the need and what adaptations are necessary. Agree with educators on sharing only the information they need. Home and school routines may differ, but the communication method and respectful response to difficulties should remain clear to the child.

## How to assess outcomes and when to reconsider the plan

A small table can help with assessment. Compare similar periods and conditions. If the number of toilet visits changes, the number of successful visits on its own can be misleading.

| Measure | What to track |
| --- | --- |
| Urination | Events in the toilet and elsewhere. |
| Bowel movements | Location, signs of discomfort and necessary assistance. |
| Initiation | Communicated independently, went independently or needed a reminder. |
| Support | Adult prompts, ongoing adaptations and hygiene assistance. |
| Comfort | Visible signs of pain, fear or requests to stop. |
| Generalisation | Availability of the skill in different familiar settings. |

For example, on one day a child urinates in the toilet three times after a reminder. The next day the child uses the toilet three times again, but on one occasion asks to go independently. The number of successful episodes has not changed, but independent initiation has emerged. This is a separate outcome worth recording.

Reconsider the plan if the child continually needs more assistance, refuses more often, the skill does not generalise, the family cannot manage the demands or there is no useful change within the agreed timeframe. Pain, stool or urine withholding, new symptoms or sudden deterioration require the medical issue to be addressed first. Do not automatically increase training intensity.

## What does the research say?

The [systematic review by Simon and colleagues (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rasd.2022.102049) included 26 studies of toilet training for autistic children. Research examined different combinations of schedules, prompts, reinforcement and other components. Small samples and heterogeneous methods limit the ability to compare programmes and identify the most effective component.

The [school study by Cocchiola and colleagues (2012)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3592490/) involved five children with autism or other developmental delays. The [2021 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7840080/) involved four autistic schoolchildren, three of whom began independently initiating toilet visits. These are small-group studies with individual outcome tracking, not evidence of a universal programme for every child.

[Little and colleagues (2023)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10333974/) examined remote support for 34 families of children aged 2-8, of whom 25 completed the intervention. The authors reported preliminary positive findings. Participant attrition and the nature of the assessment limit confidence in the conclusions.

A [review by Donnelly and Karsten (2024)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38405293/) examines ways to address barriers to teaching urination skills in children with developmental disabilities. [Osos and colleagues (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1002/bin.70006) examined a programme without punishment, and a [2026 study](https://doi.org/10.1002/jaba.70092) examined caregiver implementation of an individualised programme with remote support. These data do not establish that every component is necessary or suitable for independent use at home.

Research supports considering structured and individualised teaching, but does not justify promising the same timeframe or outcome for everyone. Urination, bowel movements, independent initiation and nighttime dryness should be assessed separately.
