## When does a child's brain begin to develop?

A child's brain begins to develop long before birth. In early pregnancy, cells and structures appear that will gradually form the nervous system.

At first, the developing brain looks nothing like the organ we recognise. An area of cells in the embryo gives rise to the **neural tube**. Different parts of this tube later develop into the brain and spinal cord.

New nerve cells then appear, different brain regions begin to form, and cells gradually establish contact with one another.

Development does not end at a particular week of pregnancy. It continues after birth, through childhood and into adulthood.

Why do autism researchers pay attention to this early period? Autism is a neurodevelopmental condition. Some genetic differences associated with autism affect processes that are active before birth. This does not mean that every autistic person has the same brain change or that scientists can identify a particular week when autism “began”.

## Where do nerve cells come from?

The human brain contains billions of nerve cells called **neurons**. They receive and transmit signals and contribute to movement, perception, learning and communication.

Early in development, **progenitor cells** appear. They can divide and give rise to new cells. Some continue dividing, while others gradually acquire the characteristics of neurons or other cells of the nervous system.

Think of a workshop in which materials gradually become different components. But the brain is not assembled to a single rigid blueprint. Different cells develop at different times, guided by the activity of many genes and by signals from neighbouring cells.

Neurons are not all alike. Some transmit information over long distances, while others help regulate the activity of nearby cells. They gradually acquire different characteristics that allow them to perform these functions.

Researchers study these processes because some genetic variants associated with autism are involved in regulating them. This is not evidence that every autistic child produces too many or too few neurons. No such universal mechanism has been established.

## How do nerve cells find their place in the brain?

After they form, young neurons do not always remain in the same location. Some move to another part of the developing brain. This process is called **neuronal migration**.

Imagine a building under construction. Making the parts is not enough. Each part must also be positioned correctly. Similarly, brain development involves not only making cells but also determining where they settle and which other cells they can interact with.

Neurons migrate in response to signals from neighbouring cells. For some neurons, long extensions of cells called **radial glia** serve as guides. Not all neurons migrate in the same way, however.

Different layers of cells gradually form in the **cerebral cortex**. These layers differ in their properties, connections and functions. This is one example of how early cellular processes help create the brain's complex organisation.

It would be wrong to infer that nerve cells are “in the wrong place” in every autistic person. Autism cannot be reduced to a single disturbance of neuronal migration.

## How do nerve cells begin communicating?

Even after cells reach different areas of the brain, they need connections to work together.

Neurons gradually grow extensions and establish contacts with other cells. Many of these contacts are called **synapses**, and they enable signals to pass between nerve cells.

Large numbers of interconnected neurons form **neural networks**, which are involved in processing information and supporting many functions of the body.

A newborn's brain is still developing. Connections continue to form, strengthen and reorganise during childhood and beyond. Some connections are reduced over time, while others become more stable.

The nervous system's capacity to change its organisation and function is called **neuroplasticity**. It contributes to learning skills and changing how we interact with our surroundings. Neuroplasticity does not mean that every brain difference can be changed at will, or that there is a way to eliminate autism with a guaranteed result.

## How is prenatal brain development related to autism?

Researchers have identified many genetic variants associated with the likelihood of autism. Some of the affected genes are involved in producing neurons, differentiating them into specialised cell types and establishing neural connections.

Researchers are investigating which cells express these genes and at what stages of development. This can suggest possible biological mechanisms rather than simply producing a list of associated genes.

For example, [Willsey and colleagues' 2013 study in *Cell*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2013.10.020) identified particular neuron types and periods of prenatal development worth investigating in relation to certain autism-associated genes. This identifies a research direction. It does not show that all autistic people have the same changes in those cells.

Other studies examine the effects of particular genetic variants on nerve cell development in the laboratory. Some variants produce partially similar findings, while others yield markedly different results.

**None of these findings can tell us exactly how an individual child's brain developed during pregnancy.** Autism is heterogeneous, and there is no single explanation that applies to everyone.

### Does this mean something went wrong during pregnancy?

No. The fact that the nervous system starts developing early does not show that autism was caused by something a mother did, a particular emotional experience or one event in pregnancy.

Some genetic variants are inherited. Others arise for the first time. Researchers also study certain medical and environmental factors, but a statistical association with autism likelihood does not establish that a particular event caused autism in one child.

Trying to identify a parent's “mistake” by looking back over a pregnancy has no sound scientific basis. Medical questions about risks during pregnancy should be discussed with a clinician.

## How do researchers investigate brain development before birth?

Scientists cannot observe every cell in a child's developing brain throughout pregnancy. They therefore use several research approaches.

One involves examining nervous tissue samples obtained within authorised scientific research. Modern techniques can identify cell types and patterns of gene activity, allowing researchers to construct detailed **cell atlases** of the developing brain.

Another approach studies nerve cells grown in the laboratory. Researchers can sometimes grow small three-dimensional structures that reproduce certain features of developing nervous tissue. These are called **organoids**.

An organoid is not a complete child's brain. It does not reproduce the entire nervous system, its interactions with the body or a person's lived experience. It can nevertheless help researchers investigate specific cellular processes and the effects of genetic changes.

Such results require cautious interpretation and confirmation by other methods. They provide evidence about possible mechanisms, not a way to predict the development of an individual child.

## Can autism be detected before birth?

There is currently no reliable, generally accepted method for diagnosing autism in a fetus using ultrasound or MRI.

These examinations can be important for other clinical questions, such as assessing anatomical development. They cannot confirm or rule out autism.

Even after birth, autism cannot be diagnosed from one brain scan, blood test or genetic result alone. Clinical assessment considers developmental history, communication, social interaction and behaviour.

### Why are autistic characteristics sometimes noticed years later?

Many skills emerge gradually. We do not expect a newborn to use connected speech, engage in complex cooperative play or communicate independently with peers.

As a child grows, parents and professionals may notice differences in communication, behaviour, interests or sensory experience. Some differences are apparent early, while others become more noticeable with age.

The age at which a difference is first recognised does not necessarily indicate when its biological foundations arose.

## Why is support still important after birth?

If some neurodevelopmental processes begin before birth, families may wonder whether support later in childhood can make a difference.

Yes. The brain continues to develop after birth. Children gain new experiences, acquire skills and learn to interact with other people.

Appropriate support may make communication, learning and daily life easier. For example, a child who struggles to express needs through speech can be helped to use accessible forms of communication. An environment can be adapted if particular sounds cause intense discomfort.

The aim is not to make the child “neurotypical”, but to recognise individual characteristics, build useful skills and improve quality of life. Support should be selected according to the person's needs and the quality of the supporting evidence.

Research on early brain development helps scientists understand autism. For families, what a child can do today also matters: how they communicate, what helps them, and what support they need.
