There is no single autism intervention that every child needs. Autistic people's needs differ and may change with age and circumstances. Support may focus on communication, learning, sensory comfort, independence, physical health, sleep, nutrition, mental health, relationships and participation in school and everyday life.

Several different things should be distinguished. Some approaches teach or maintain skills. Others change the environment. Some are assessment and planning tools, not therapies. Separately, medical care may be needed for epilepsy, pain, constipation, ADHD, sleep problems and other co-occurring conditions.

A better starting question than "Which therapy is best?" is:

> **What is currently difficult for the child or family, and what specific outcome would genuinely help?**

## Where to start

The same situation can have very different causes.

For example, a child shouts at school. Perhaps they cannot ask for a break, are in pain, find the noise overwhelming, do not understand the task, feel anxious or are struggling with an unexpected schedule change.

A good support plan usually begins with a specific goal and investigation of possible causes rather than choosing a familiar method by name.

## 1. Communication and AAC

If a child has difficulty expressing wants, understanding speech, conversing, asking for help, declining an activity or explaining pain, support may target functional communication.

This can involve speech and language support, augmentative and alternative communication (AAC), visual aids, teaching adults to support communication and structured approaches such as PECS.

AAC can be low-tech, such as picture cards, symbols or communication boards, or use an electronic speech-generating device.

The aim is not necessarily to make the child speak in a particular way. What matters is having an accessible, reliable way to communicate needs, choices, pain, refusal and interests.

[Go to Communication and AAC](/kz/ru/parents/kommunikatsiya-i-aac/)

## 2. Behaviour and learning

If aggression, self-injury, shouting, avoidance, property damage or other difficult situations occur, do not start by simply trying to "stop the behaviour".

Instead, examine what happens before and after. Can the child express what they want? Could they be in pain? Is the setting too noisy? Is the task understandable? Is the situation sufficiently predictable?

Depending on the cause, approaches may include functional behaviour assessment, functional communication training (FCT), positive behaviour support (PBS), environmental changes, visual supports and other teaching strategies.

## 3. Development, interaction and play

Some programmes focus on joint attention, play, social interaction and communication in natural settings.

These include naturalistic developmental behavioural interventions, JASPER and various developmental or play-based programmes.

When choosing, consider not just the programme's name but its specific goal, the child's age, delivery format, involvement of parents or educators and the outcomes actually studied.

A programme will not necessarily affect communication, play, adaptive skills and quality of life equally.

## 4. Sensory differences and environmental adjustments

When noise, bright light, clothes, touch, smells or other sensations cause substantial distress, adjusting the environment may be the first step.

Examples include a quieter space, reducing noise, changing lighting, comfortable clothing, access to breaks or changes to the school environment.

Distinguish **environmental adjustments**, **Ayres Sensory Integration**, individual **sensory-based strategies**, so-called **sensory diets** and commercial listening programmes.

They are not interchangeable. Their goals, procedures and evidence differ.

## 5. Movement and physical activity

When someone has difficulty with coordination, balance, endurance, mobility or participating in physical education and sport, support may include adapted physical activity, physical therapy, therapeutic exercise, motor skill development, adapted sport or aquatic activities.

The goals here concern health, movement and participation in activities that matter to the person.

This differs from commercial programmes that promise to "treat autism" by stimulating the cerebellum, integrating reflexes or invoking other proposed neurological mechanisms. Such claims require separate evidence.

## 6. Independence and daily living skills

Support can address toileting, dressing, hygiene, brushing teeth, eating, household tasks, transport and safety.

Occupational therapy, teaching tasks step by step, visual prompting, modelling, environmental adjustments and fading assistance may be useful.

Goals should be functional and meaningful to the person.

## 7. Education and school support

Support at school may include structuring the setting, visual schedules, adapted tasks, AAC, sensory adjustments, peer support and an individual education or support plan.

An individual plan is not itself a therapy. It helps set goals, specify necessary adjustments, assign responsibilities and identify how to measure outcomes.

## 8. Psychological support and mental health

Autistic children and teenagers, like anyone else, can experience anxiety, depression, obsessive thoughts, trauma-related stress or other mental health difficulties.

Support should address the specific condition, with delivery adjusted when needed for the person's communication, sensory and cognitive profile.

Do not automatically attribute mental health conditions to autism itself.

## 9. Nutrition and eating difficulties

If a child eats very few foods, refuses new foods, fears eating or has chewing problems, first consider possible medical causes.

Assess growth and weight, possible nutritional deficiencies, pain, constipation, allergy, coeliac disease, vomiting and swallowing difficulties.

After assessment, the child may need feeding assessment, dietetic support or individual feeding interventions.

More: [Assessing feeding difficulties before choosing support](/kz/ru/parents/otsenka-trudnostey-pitaniya/)

If support is needed after assessment: [Feeding therapy and support](/kz/ru/parents/feeding-therapy/).

A diet prescribed for diagnosed coeliac disease or allergy is different from an exclusion diet promoted as "autism treatment".

## 10. Sleep

Difficulty falling asleep, frequent waking or early rising may relate to routines and environment, but also anxiety, pain, gastrointestinal conditions, epilepsy or sleep-disordered breathing.

For significant or sudden sleep problems, it is important to investigate causes rather than rely only on "sleep hygiene".

Behavioural approaches, environmental adjustments and medical methods are distinct parts of support and should be considered separately.

## 11. Co-occurring medical conditions

Epilepsy, ADHD, constipation, pain, dental problems, sleep disorders and other conditions require their own assessment and treatment.

Treating constipation, epilepsy or ADHD may substantially improve wellbeing and daily life.

That does not mean these treatments "cure autism".

## 12. Support for parents and family

Support is not only for the child.

Parents and other family members may benefit from practical-skills training, caregiver support programmes, psychological support, sibling support, respite or short breaks, and coordination among professionals and services.

The family's wellbeing matters in its own right, not merely as a means of making the child's sessions more effective.

## 13. Social participation and life skills

As people grow older, friendship, safety, relationships, puberty, transport, education, employment, independent living and self-advocacy may become increasingly important.

Support should help people participate in areas of life that they themselves value.

The aim should not simply be to make someone appear "less autistic".

## 14. Technology, creative activities and other formats

Apps, digital technologies, virtual reality, music, art, activities with animals and other formats can be used for different goals.

Simply including technology, music, animals or sensory equipment does not prove that a programme is effective.

Check the exact method, its goals and components, costs, risks and studies of the specific approach.

## 15. Assessment and planning are not therapy

Parents often see some terms listed alongside therapies even though they describe assessment or planning tools.

Examples include **ADOS-2**, **ADI-R**, **Vineland**, **Sensory Profile**, **VB-MAPP**, **ABLLS-R**, **AFLS**, **ATEC**, and individual education or support plans.

These may be used for diagnostic assessment, describing skills, understanding needs, planning or monitoring changes.

They are not therapies in themselves.

A score change on one scale also does not establish that any specific intervention caused the change.

## 16. Experimental, insufficiently researched and unsafe approaches

Not all methods marketed to families have comparable evidence.

Three situations need to be distinguished.

**Insufficient evidence** means a reliable conclusion cannot yet be drawn.

**Research has not shown the expected benefit** means the method was studied but its claimed effect was not confirmed.

**Unsafe practice or method** means the concern extends beyond uncertain benefit to risks to health or safety.

These categories should not be collapsed into a vague statement that something is simply "unproven".

## How to choose support

Start with a concrete functional goal, check possible medical causes, consider environmental adjustments, ask for the method's precise name and examine evidence for the outcome you actually want.

Consider the demands on the child and family, costs, time, the child's views of sessions and whether skills transfer into daily life.

Record a baseline before starting and decide in advance when to review outcomes.

If meaningful benefit does not occur, unwanted effects arise, the child experiences persistent severe distress, demands become excessive or the goal is no longer relevant, the plan may need changing or stopping.

## What matters in Kazakhstan

A service being available in Kazakhstan, a professional having a certificate or an organisation holding a licence does not prove that the method is effective.

For medical services, relevant permissions and licences can be checked using the official eGov/eLicense services.

Kazakhstan's Law on Psychological Activity is dated 2 July 2026. Article 22 provides for commencement 60 calendar days after its first official publication, except for Article 17. Articles 21 and 22 allow a one-year transition period for psychologists to register in the state register, while Article 17 on certification enters into force on 1 January 2028. This does not mean that every short-course certificate establishes a psychologist's professional qualifications.

For educational and other regulated roles, requirements must be checked for the particular position and organisation.

AutismHub therefore separates three questions:

**What does the research show?**

**Who is legally and professionally permitted to provide the service?**

**Is the service currently available in Kazakhstan?**

An answer to one does not automatically answer the others.

## When to see a doctor first

Do not explain every new symptom as autism.

Medical assessment is especially important after loss of previously acquired skills, new seizures or unusual episodes of unresponsiveness, significant pain, major changes to eating or weight, swallowing problems, sudden deterioration in sleep or new severe self-injury.

An immediate threat to life or safety requires emergency medical assistance.

## What to do next

Choose the problem you want to address first, rather than choosing a method by its name.

If you already know a particular method, check five things:

**its intended goal, what research reports, how reliable the evidence is, what risks are known, and what is known about its use and regulation in Kazakhstan.**
