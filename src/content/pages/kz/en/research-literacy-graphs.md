## 1. Why use graphs in the first place?

A numerical table can contain dozens of results that are difficult to compare. A graph helps us see the distribution of values, the direction of change or differences between groups. But it can also mislead readers if an axis starts at a misleading value, percentages are given without the number of observations or uncertainty is not shown.

All the figures in this article are illustrative. They do not measure autism or demonstrate the effectiveness of any method.

## 2. Four things to read first

Before interpreting brightly coloured bars, look for:

1. The title: what exactly was studied?
2. The axes: what is plotted horizontally and vertically?
3. The units: percentages, scale points, days, people or risk ratios?
4. The caption and source: who participated, what were the dates, and what do the colours and lines mean?

If a figure says "improvement: 80" without explaining whether that means 80 people, 80% or 80 points, it does not provide enough information to draw a conclusion.

## 3. How one scale can make a difference look enormous

![An educational pair of bar charts: the same two illustrative values appear more or less different depending on the vertical axis scale.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/research/research-literacy-graphs-diagram-en.svg)

*Both pairs of bars represent the same illustrative numbers. The visual impression changes because of the scale.*

Imagine mean scores of 80 and 84. If the axis starts at zero, the bars look similar in height. If it starts at 79, they appear sharply different. The numerical difference is four points in both cases.

A truncated axis is not categorically wrong. It can be useful for showing detail, but the scale must be clear to the reader. Take particular care with bar charts: bar length visually implies a comparison with zero.

## 4. Line graphs: what changes over time?

Time is usually plotted on the horizontal axis. The vertical axis shows the value of the measure. A line joining points does not necessarily indicate continuous measurement: there may have been no observations between two visits.

A trend that increased for five years does not prove that it will always increase. For example, growth in registered diagnoses may reflect changes in record-keeping or access to assessment rather than a single biological explanation.

## 5. Percentage bars: where is the denominator?

"80% of participants support an initiative." How many responded: 8 out of 10, or 800 out of 1000? Were they randomly selected? Who did not respond?

Identical percentages can conceal very different degrees of uncertainty. In studies of small subgroups, always check the number of observations.

## 6. Scatter plots

Each point usually represents an observation with two quantitative characteristics. If the cloud of points slopes from lower left to upper right, it indicates a positive statistical association.

But the picture does not tell us why the variables are associated. A third factor, reverse causation and sampling errors remain possible. It is also worth checking whether the apparent trend is driven by two unusual points.

## 7. Error bars, standard deviations and intervals

An error bar can represent a confidence interval, standard deviation or standard error. These are different quantities, and the name does not imply that a mistake occurred. Do not infer the meaning of the bar from its appearance. Look for the definition and calculation method in the caption.

A wide confidence interval often indicates substantial uncertainty in the estimate. However, its meaning depends on the effect size and the method used to calculate it.

## 8. Forest plots: reading results from several studies

Systematic reviews use a type of graph called a forest plot. Each row usually represents a separate study: a square marks its point estimate, a horizontal line shows the confidence interval, and the square's area often represents the study's weight in a meta-analysis.

A diamond at the bottom may represent the pooled estimate and its interval.

If the horizontal axis shows a risk ratio (RR), the no-difference value is 1. For a difference between means, the no-difference value is usually 0. These values cannot be used interchangeably.

Educational example: three studies estimate RRs of 0.8, 1.1 and 0.9. We cannot interpret the overall result without knowing the intervals, pooling method, weights, study quality and comparability of the groups. RR = 0.8 is a risk ratio, not an 80% improvement.

## 9. What a forest plot shows and what it conceals

Forest plots are useful, but they do not automatically tell us whether participants' ages, programme durations, diagnostic methods and outcome measurements were comparable. A pooled effect requires particular caution when heterogeneity is high.

A graph does not become proof simply because it contains many rows and a diamond.

## 10. Reading tables in a research paper

Look for columns reporting n, the name of the measure, the mean or median, frequency, effect size, interval, missing data and explanatory notes. A table describing participant characteristics is often more informative than the most striking graph.

For example, if groups differed substantially in initial age or severity of co-occurring difficulties, the observed differences in outcomes may need further investigation.

## 11. An educational example involving autism registration

Suppose region A records 200 new diagnoses and region B records 100. Can we say autism is twice as common in A? No. The size of the child population, ages, reporting period and access to assessment may differ.

Comparison requires suitable rates per defined population size and comparable registration procedures. Even a standardised rate does not explain why a regional difference exists.

## 12. Six warning signs of a misleading visualisation

- [ ] Axis labels or units are missing.
- [ ] An axis is truncated without explanation, exaggerating the visual difference.
- [ ] Percentages are displayed without absolute observation counts.
- [ ] The legend does not explain the colours or intervals.
- [ ] Only favourable points are shown, while inconvenient ones are hidden.
- [ ] The graph shows an association, but the caption asserts causation.

The main point: a good graph helps us see the data. A reliable conclusion also requires an understanding of the methods, sample and limitations.
