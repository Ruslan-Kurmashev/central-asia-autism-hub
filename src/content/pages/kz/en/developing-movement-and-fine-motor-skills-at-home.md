Many everyday tasks require coordination: crossing a room, holding a spoon, turning a book page, putting a toy in a box or pulling up a sock. These abilities can be supported through **short games and ordinary family routines**, without expensive “developmental” kits or required daily practice.

This article includes **25 play ideas**: 10 for larger body movements and 15 for hands and fingers. Eight activities have step-by-step project illustrations. Printable resources are linked at the end.

> **Key point:** Home play is not physiotherapy, diagnosis or a way to make a child “normal.” Choose only accessible, safe and enjoyable actions, and accept refusal. Significant difficulties call for professional advice rather than more repetitions.

## 1. Gross and fine motor skills: what's the difference?

**Gross motor skills** involve the trunk, arms, legs and control of body position. Everyday examples include walking, moving between objects and playing with a large ball. Some children benefit from seated movement, support or assistive devices.

**Fine motor skills** involve coordinated actions of hands and fingers: grasping and releasing objects, opening boxes, crumpling paper and using spoons, fasteners or pencils.

**Bilateral coordination** allows one hand to stabilise an object while the other acts, such as opening a lid or holding paper for a sticker.

These skills help with everyday participation but do not measure intelligence, personality or autism. Finger exercises by themselves cannot be promised to “trigger speech.” Speech and movement are distinct functions that may require separate assessment.

## 2. How to choose a suitable game

Consider not just age but **what a child can currently do without pain or overload**.

| What is accessible now | A possible starting point | Not required |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prefers playing while seated | Roll a ball on a table or floor, crumple a large sheet, open a light box. | Standing or jumping for practice. |
| Walks but dislikes complex routes | Wide line on nonslip flooring and two large markers to go around. | Narrow kerbs or obstacle courses. |
| Finds two-handed actions easier | Crumple paper, move a large block, smooth fabric. | Immediate pincer grips or tiny objects. |
| Has a favourite toy | Include the movement in a preferred game, such as driving trains along a path. | Switching to an adult's “correct” activity. |
| Uses gestures, pictures or AAC | Offer two activity choices, a break and a way to say “enough.” | Saying a word aloud to receive a toy. |

For established movement limitations, joint conditions, major balance difficulties or specialised support equipment, discuss suitable activities with a physiotherapist, occupational therapist or treating clinician.

### General safety before play

- Use clear, level flooring without sliding rugs or sharp edges.
- Do not build obstacle courses on chairs, tables or piles of high cushions.
- Avoid small beads, coins, buttons, dry grains, magnets and small construction pieces for children under three **and any child likely to put these objects in their mouth**.
- Stickers, dough, peg components and torn paper also need swallowing-risk assessment.
- Continuously supervise water play and promptly clean up spills.
- Avoid long cords near the neck, body ties and unsafe homemade hanging equipment.
- Stop for pain, dizziness, pronounced fatigue, fear or overload. Never use physical force.

## 3. Four gross motor games: illustrated instructions

Each image is a simplified example, **not a mandatory technique**.

### Game 1. Following a floor line

**Materials:** painter's tape and flat, dry flooring.

![Three steps: lay down a wide line, demonstrate walking along it and let the child choose a route.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/motor/motor-walking-line-steps.webp)

**Easier:** make the line wider. The child may use their usual support or move beside the line.

**Harder:** add a gentle turn or carry a light toy. Do not insist on heel-to-toe walking.

**Stop if:** the child is unstable, tired or wants to stop.

### Game 2. Rolling a large soft ball

**Materials:** a large lightweight ball and free floor space.

![Three steps: a mother and child sit facing each other, the mother rolls the ball and the child may roll it back.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/motor/motor-rolling-ball-steps.webp)

**Easier:** shorten the distance or roll the ball nearby. Supported sitting is fine.

**Harder:** make wide “goalposts” using two large stable objects.

**Safety:** do not throw the ball at the face or demand eye contact or perfect aim.

### Game 3. Going around “islands”

**Materials:** two or three large soft toys or flat nonslip floor markers.

![Three steps: soft toys are spaced far apart, the child moves around them and chooses their own route.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/motor/motor-islands-route-steps.webp)

**How:** leave a wide gap between objects. Invite the child to go around, choose a shorter route or observe. They can design their own route.

**Easier:** use just two markers, or stay seated and move a toy car between them.

**Harder:** add another turn if the child enjoys it.

**Safety:** avoid unstable boxes and high obstacles.

### Game 4. Stepping over a low rolled towel

**Materials:** a small tightly rolled towel on dry flooring.

![Three steps: the mother places a rolled towel, demonstrates stepping over it and the child may copy when ready.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/motor/motor-step-over-towel-steps.webp)

**How:** show that the towel can be stepped over. Do not pull the child's hands or demand jumping. If stepping over is not accessible, use a drawn or taped floor line.

**Easier:** use a flat mark without a raised object.

**Harder:** place two low markers well apart.

**Stop if:** the child trips, feels unsure, experiences pain or refuses.

## 5. Four hand and finger games: illustrated instructions

### Game 5. Pegs on wide cardboard

**Materials:** large intact pegs that open easily and a sheet of stiff cardboard.

![Three steps: a mother fixes a blue peg to cardboard, the child removes it and may attach a green peg.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/motor/motor-clothes-pegs-steps.webp)

**How:** Attach one peg yourself, then offer to remove it. If interested, demonstrate squeezing and replacing it.

**Easier:** start with removal and hold the cardboard steady.

**Harder:** make a “sun” from several pegs or choose colours.

**Safety:** check that springs and components cannot detach and avoid pinching skin.

### Game 6. Tearing and crumpling paper

**Materials:** a large sheet of paper.

![Three steps: the mother shows a sheet, the child tears it and then crumples paper.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/motor/motor-tear-and-crumple-paper-steps.webp)

**How:** Offer to crumple the sheet with both hands. If tearing appeals, start a small tear and let the child continue. Large pieces can make a collage.

**Easier:** thin paper and adult help with the first tear.

**Harder:** create a picture from several large pieces.

**Safety:** replace this game if the child mouths paper scraps.

### Game 7. Modelling a ball, roll and imprint

**Materials:** a large piece of play dough with a safe composition and texture.

![Three steps: the mother shows a large dough ball, the child makes impressions and rolls a long piece.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/motor/motor-play-dough-ball-imprints-roll-steps.webp)

**How:** Offer to flatten dough, trace a line with a finger or roll a thick cylinder. The child can just watch if they prefer not to touch it.

**Easier:** use a large safe tool if touching dough feels unpleasant.

**Harder:** make an impression with a large cutter.

**Safety:** check ingredients for allergens and prevent eating the dough or mouthing small pieces.

### Game 8. Transferring a large object with a spoon

**Materials:** two stable bowls, a large spoon and a few large safe blocks.

![Three steps: a mother shows a block in a bowl and moves it with a large spoon, then the child transfers it.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/motor/motor-spoon-transfer-cubes-steps.webp)

**How:** Offer to carry a large block from one bowl to another using the spoon. If this is awkward, use a hand first.

**Easier:** stir an empty bowl with a large spoon or ladle.

**Harder:** move the second bowl slightly further away while keeping a stable position and allowing breaks.

**Safety:** **Do not substitute rice, beans, coins or beads** if swallowing is a risk.

**Important:** An activity appearing in NHS materials does not mean it suits every child or age. Beads, grains, long cords and tiny buttons can cause choking, swallowing or entanglement hazards. **Larger alternatives** are preferred here, and dangerous small objects are not advised for independent play.

## 7. Including movement in everyday life

Separate “therapy sessions” at a table are not required. Choose ordinary routines the family already follows.

| Situation | Movement opportunity | Adaptation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dressing in the morning | Hold a sleeve, open a strap or pull a large zip. | Adult begins and child finishes a manageable part. |
| Preparing meals | Stir with a spoon or place a large napkin. | Stable dishes, accessible height and nonslip surface. |
| Tidying toys | Carry large light toys into a basket. | Keep the basket close, allow sitting. |
| Reading | Turn pages or point to chosen pictures. | Thick-page cardboard books. |
| Construction play | Connect and separate large pieces. | Adult stabilises pieces if helpful. |
| Walking outside | Roll a ball or go around safe markers. | Choose an accessible route and necessary support. |
| Folding laundry | Smooth a towel or sort large garments. | Break the task into one comfortable step. |

Notice not only movement but **how the child chooses, asks for help or requests a break**.

## 9. Observing progress without scores or pressure

Focus on practical opportunities, not required repetitions:

- What did the child choose?
- Was the activity comfortable?
- What worked independently or with help?
- Which materials, movements or sensations were unpleasant?
- How did the child ask for help, a break or refuse?
- Did environmental changes make daily participation easier?

Example:

> **Situation:** The child wanted to play with large blocks.  
> **Action:** Placed three blocks, then offered a fourth to an adult.  
> **Support:** The adult stabilised the base.  
> **Observation:** Building while seated on a mat was more interesting than standing.  
> **Next step:** Keep the comfortable option available.

This is observation, not a measurement of treatment effectiveness. Use the weekly plan PDF or discuss the notes with an appropriate professional.

## 10. What to avoid

- Do not try to diagnose a child through play or judge them by one age-based task.
- Do not use pain, fear or physical restraint to obtain a movement.
- Do not demand repetitions, completing every task or guaranteed success.
- Do not replace medical assessment of persistent motor difficulties with home exercises.
- Do not claim that finger games or modelling must produce speech.
- Do not prohibit safe preferred movements just to teach “correct” play.
- Do not use tiny items, unsafe cords or slippery structures under the label of sensory play.

## 11. When to contact a professional

Talk with a paediatrician or another suitable health professional if:

- The child has lost previously stable walking, hand movement or self-care skills.
- New pronounced asymmetry or a clear preference for one side raises concerns.
- Movements cause pain or usual activity drops sharply.
- Falls increase or unusual coordination difficulties appear.
- Significant everyday difficulties with grasping, balance or self-care persist.
- Parents or educators remain concerned about motor development.

**Sudden weakness, inability to put weight on a leg, a painful injury, altered consciousness or other acute symptoms require urgent medical assessment.** Do not first try to treat them with exercise at home.

Professional support may include developmental assessment by a paediatrician, medical investigations if indicated or consultation with a physiotherapist or occupational therapist. Home games must not delay seeking help.

## 12. Sources and limitations

NHS paediatric occupational therapy resources describe playing with pegs, paper, dough and everyday objects. NHS physiotherapy services discuss large balls, walking and routes. WHO and UNICEF emphasise responsive play and everyday adult involvement. CDC provides movement milestones but does not treat them as a replacement for standardised assessment. AAP and CPSC recommend selecting toys with choking risks and individual needs in mind.

**Limitation:** An activity being mentioned in a guide does not mean each specific home exercise has been tested in a high-quality clinical trial. This is general education for parents, not evidence for promises to “fix movement” or “trigger speech.”

## Key points in one minute

Choose **one safe game the child genuinely enjoys** and adapt it: large rather than tiny objects, stable floors rather than height, sitting rather than mandatory standing, and adult help instead of demanding independence. Follow the child's interests and right to say “no,” and use the PDF resources only as ideas.

Persistent concerns about motor development deserve professional advice. Play should make everyday life more accessible and enjoyable, not another examination.
