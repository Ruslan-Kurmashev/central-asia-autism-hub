## Why is this important to discuss?

Autism occurs in people of different sexes. Historically, however, boys were described more often in clinical practice and research. As a result, some characteristics seen in girls may be recognised later or overlooked. The NICE clinical guideline explicitly warns that autism may be under-recognised in girls (NICE, 2017).

This **does not mean there are two entirely separate types of autism, "male" and "female"**. There is considerable variation within each group. What matters is the individual child, not a preconceived idea of how an autistic girl is "supposed" to behave.

## What is social camouflaging?

Social camouflaging refers to strategies a person uses to hide difficulties or adapt their behaviour to other people's expectations. For example, a child might watch classmates carefully, repeat their phrases, prepare answers in advance or maintain eye contact even when doing so takes considerable effort.

Some autistic girls may be able to follow social rules at school but still experience fatigue, stress or difficulties in informal interactions. Their behaviour at school and at home may differ noticeably.

However, **camouflaging is not a diagnostic sign on its own**. Imitating other people's behaviour, being shy or wanting to be liked also occurs in non-autistic children. Boys may camouflage their characteristics too (NHS, 2024 review).

## Why might characteristics go unnoticed?

Research and clinical reviews discuss several possible explanations:

- **Stereotyped expectations.** Adults may expect only certain behaviours, such as a lack of interest in peers, and overlook other difficulties.
- **Social imitation.** Some children use learned patterns of conversation, so their difficulties may not be immediately apparent during a short appointment.
- **Interests that appear typical.** A child's interests may be common among peers but differ in their intensity, rigidity or importance in everyday life.
- **Alternative explanations.** Anxiety, attention difficulties or emotional concerns may be more apparent than differences in communication and sensory experiences.
- **Differences between settings.** Familiar rules and support may help a child in one environment, while difficulties emerge in another.

These are possible reasons for under-recognition, **not a checklist for self-diagnosis**. Recent reviews also highlight limitations in research on these differences: findings depend in part on the diagnostic methods used and on who was included in the study.

## Can a girl have friends and communicate well and still be autistic?

Yes. Having friends, feeling close to family, speaking fluently, smiling or making eye contact does not by itself rule out autism. NICE explicitly states that autism must not be ruled out simply because a child makes eye contact, smiles, shows affection or develops spoken language at the expected age.

It is more helpful to explore **how understandable and manageable relationships feel to the child**, and how they cope with unwritten social rules, changes in plans and interactions across settings. These experiences cannot be judged solely by the number of friends someone has.

## Why do questions sometimes arise closer to adolescence?

Social environments become more complex with age: there are more unwritten rules, expectations of independence, group interactions and unpredictable situations. Characteristics that were not previously obvious may become more significant. But later recognition **does not mean autism first developed during adolescence**.

Sometimes families seek advice because a child feels exhausted after socialising, experiences anxiety or struggles with everyday demands. Such difficulties can have different causes and deserve careful assessment whether or not they are related to autism.

## What can parents discuss with a professional?

If concerns about development persist, it can help to prepare concrete observations rather than trying to make a diagnosis independently:

1. How does the child communicate at home, at school, and with familiar and unfamiliar people?
2. Are changes in plans, informal interactions or noisy places difficult?
3. What strategies does the child use to manage these situations, and how much effort do they take?
4. What do different adults notice, and what does the child say about their own experience?
5. What strengths, interests and conditions help the child feel comfortable?

A professional assessment considers developmental history and information from several sources, not just the impression formed during a short conversation. No single behaviour can confirm or exclude autism (NICE, 2017).

## What should parents avoid?

Do not ask a child to "act more autistic" so that a professional will notice their characteristics. Equally, not every instance of anxiety, withdrawal or difficulty at school should automatically be attributed to autism. There may be other or co-occurring causes.

There is no reliable stand-alone online "autism test for girls" that replaces a comprehensive assessment. The key consideration for families is the child's actual needs, well-being and access to appropriate support.
