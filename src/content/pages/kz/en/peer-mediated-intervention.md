During a break, several children are building a town from blocks. Another child wants to join but does not know how to begin. An adult can help arrange the activity so each child has a clear way to participate, for example by proposing a road to build, allowing more time to respond or showing an accessible choice.

This is one situation in which peer-mediated support may be used. It is not about assigning a child the role of therapist. The aim is to make shared activities more accessible and allow children to interact because they want to.

Peer-based support includes different ways to organise communication and play. Some programmes prepare peers to use particular strategies, whereas others change the conditions of a group activity. Similar names do not mean these approaches are identical.

## What this can look like at school or preschool

Suppose a child enjoys trains and classmates are building a railway. A teacher suggests a shared activity and helps the children understand one another without directing every move.

![Three children build a wooden toy railway together. One child receives a track piece from a peer while another contributes to the construction, with a toy train and blocks nearby.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/diagrams/peer-mediated-children-train-play-approved.webp)

*An example of shared play: children offer one another railway pieces and continue building together. This is an educational illustration, not a depiction of a formal intervention protocol.*

Peers might:

- invite the child to play without requiring an immediate answer
- offer a choice of two pieces or play roles
- use understandable gestures and visual supports
- notice the child's request, comment or suggestion
- respect a wish to change the game or take a break.

When needed, the adult can support all participants, not only the autistic child. Support may be reduced as interaction becomes more independent.

This example is illustrative, not a required protocol or proof that the activity will improve any particular child's social skills.

## What peer mediation, PMI and PBII mean

Several related terms appear in research.

- **Peer-Mediated Intervention (PMI):** an intervention in which peers, with specific preparation or support, help facilitate social interaction.
- **Peer mediation:** the term used in NICE recommendations for peer involvement in supporting social communication among school-age children.
- **Peer-Based Instruction and Intervention (PBII):** a broader category that includes both programmes with prepared peers and activities structured by an adult to make participation possible.
- **Peer support:** a general description of support from peers, not the name of a standardised intervention.

The National Clearinghouse on Autism Evidence and Practice (NCAEP) combined its previous categories Peer-Mediated Intervention and Instruction and Structured Play Groups into PBII. Findings from the whole PBII category cannot simply be applied to a particular service described as "peer mediation".

[Hume et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8510990/).

## What research shows

### The NCAEP review

In the review by Hume and colleagues (2021), PBII was one of 28 focused practices meeting the authors' evidence criteria. The evidence base included 44 studies, eight group studies and 36 single-case studies.

Forty-four is a study count, not an effect size. The studies differed in participants' ages, procedures, settings and measured outcomes. It would be inaccurate to say they all independently confirmed the effectiveness of one identical programme.

### A systematic review of school studies

The review by Ragaglia, Trovato and Bulgarelli first appeared online in October 2024 and in a journal issue in 2026. It examined 24 papers on peer-mediated interventions for children aged 6-12 in primary schools.

Most studies used a multiple-baseline design, with repeated measurements for individual participants. Positive changes in social skills were frequently reported, but only five of the 24 papers included follow-up, and these findings were mixed.

The review therefore does not establish durable long-term benefits for every child. Nor can its findings automatically be extended to preschoolers, adolescents or every out-of-school programme.

[Ragaglia and colleagues' systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39467895/).

### Evidence from group studies

Chang and Locke (2016) included five group-design studies in their systematic review, four randomised controlled trials and one pre-post study. Four took place at school and one at a camp.

All five reported improvements in certain social measures after intervention, such as social initiations or responses. Nevertheless, the small number of studies and differences between programmes limit confidence about generalisation and maintenance.

[Chang and Locke's systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27807466/).

### NICE recommendations

UK guideline NICE CG170 recommends considering a social-communication intervention using play-based strategies, delivered by a trained professional. It specifically suggests considering peer mediation for school-age children.

This is not an endorsement of every activity described as "socialisation". NICE also does not set the requirements for services in Kazakhstan.

[NICE CG170 recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations).

## What outcomes actually matter

Minutes spent near other children are not the same as quality interaction. A child may sit with a group without participating in a chosen activity. Equally, playing together without continuous conversation can be meaningful participation.

A practical goal might be to give a child the opportunity to join a game of interest independently and communicate whether they wish to continue.

When evaluating progress, distinguish:

1. Spontaneous initiation from interaction that follows an adult prompt.
2. Responding to an invitation from independently proposing a game.
3. Use of accessible communication, including gestures, pictures or AAC.
4. Participation with different children and in ordinary school life, not only during planned sessions.
5. Maintenance of change once specialised support is reduced.
6. Whether the child wishes to continue or end the interaction.

Improvement in a measured social skill does not prove that a friendship has developed. Friendship cannot be guaranteed or assigned as a treatment outcome.

## Voluntary participation, safety and adults' responsibilities

Peers should not become therapists, permanent aides or people held responsible for another child's behaviour. Adults remain responsible for organising the programme and keeping it safe.

Every child's participation should be voluntary, age-appropriate and allow refusal. An autistic child should not be required to play in a large group or communicate in a prescribed way. One friend, a small group, a brief shared activity or playing alongside other children without constant speech may be preferable.

Adults should also avoid publicly singling out a child as "the one who needs help making friends". A diagnosis or personal information should not be shared without a justified need and appropriate permission. Assigning the same child as a permanent partner does not make their relationship a genuine friendship.

These are principles of respectful, safe support. They should not be misrepresented as specific effects established by the cited studies and systematic reviews.

## AAC and other communication methods

A child may accept an invitation, suggest a game, ask for a break or decline through speech, gesture, a picture, a communication device or another form of augmentative and alternative communication (AAC).

AAC must remain available during peer play. Speech or eye contact should not be required as a condition of participation.

[Learn more about AAC](/kz/en/parents/aac-communication-guide/).

## When to reconsider the programme

Reconsider the plan if children are forced to participate, refusals are ignored, a peer is assigned as a permanent supervisor or the programme contributes to teasing and exclusion.

Persistent distress, an unclear goal, counting contacts as the only outcome, restricting AAC or change visible only in a structured session are also reasons to discuss the approach.

A child's preference to interact with one friend or play alone for a while should not automatically count as programme failure.

## Questions to ask a school or professional

Before starting, clarify:

1. What is the programme called, and which studies actually investigated it?
2. What meaningful goal was chosen for this child, and was the child's perspective considered?
3. Who organises the sessions and is responsible for safety?
4. What are peers asked to do, and may they decline?
5. How will privacy and the child's right to choose be protected?
6. Can the child use AAC, gestures and visual support?
7. How will independent participation, generalisation and maintenance be measured?
8. When will the programme be reviewed or stopped if it is not suitable?

Simply involving other children does not establish a programme's effectiveness. The procedures, adult preparation, aims and implementation quality matter.

## Considerations in Kazakhstan

Similar activities may be offered in schools, preschools, resource classrooms, clubs, camps and inclusive groups. Labels such as "peer support", "peer mediation" and "inclusive play programme" do not guarantee identical practices.

Check who runs the activity, how voluntary participation and privacy are ensured, whether accessible communication is supported, and how outcomes are assessed. International reviews and NICE guidance do not replace applicable local organisational or legal requirements.

## Key takeaways

Peer-mediated support can offer additional opportunities for communication and shared participation. Studies have found positive results for certain social skills, especially in schools, but programmes vary and evidence for long-term maintenance and generalisation is limited.

The aim is not to maximise the number of contacts at any cost. It is to make activities the child wants to join more accessible, with reciprocal, safe and voluntary participation for everyone.
