At break time, several children are building a town out of blocks. Another child wants to join but is unsure how to begin. An adult can organise the activity so that everyone has a clear opportunity to participate. For example, they might suggest making a road, allow more time for a response or demonstrate an accessible way to choose a role.

This is a situation in which peer-based support could be used. The goal is not to assign a child the role of therapist, but to make shared activity more accessible and give children the opportunity to interact if they wish.

Peer-based support encompasses different ways of organising communication and play. Some programmes train peers to use particular strategies, while others focus on changing the conditions of group activities. Similar labels do not make these programmes identical.

## What it can look like at school or preschool

Imagine that a child enjoys trains and classmates are building a railway. A teacher proposes a group game and gently helps the children understand one another.

![Three children assemble a wooden toy railway together: one accepts a piece from a peer, another helps build, and a toy train and blocks are nearby.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/diagrams/peer-mediated-children-train-play-approved.webp)

*An example of shared play: peers offer one another railway pieces and continue building together. This is an educational illustration, not a depiction of a specific intervention protocol.*

Peers may:

- invite the child to play without demanding an immediate response
- offer a choice between two pieces or play roles
- use understandable gestures and visual supports
- notice the child's request, comment or suggestion
- respect a wish to change the game or take a break.

When necessary, the adult supports all participants, not only the autistic child. Support can be reduced gradually if the children begin interacting independently.

This is an illustrative example, not a mandatory protocol or evidence that a particular episode will improve a child's social skills.

## Understanding peer mediation, PMI and PBII

Research uses several related terms.

- **Peer-Mediated Intervention (PMI)** refers to interventions in which peers, given some preparation or support, help create opportunities for social interaction.
- **Peer mediation**, in NICE guidance, refers to peers taking part in supporting the social communication of school-age children.
- **Peer-Based Instruction and Intervention (PBII)** is a broader category that includes both interventions involving trained peers and activities arranged by adults to support shared participation.
- **Peer support** is a broad description and does not itself identify a standardised programme.

The National Clearinghouse on Autism Evidence and Practice (NCAEP) merged the previous categories Peer-Mediated Intervention and Instruction and Structured Play Groups into PBII. Findings for the entire category therefore cannot automatically be attributed to a particular service advertised as “peer mediation”.

[Hume and colleagues, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8510990/).

## What the research shows

### NCAEP review

In the review by Hume and colleagues (2021), PBII was among 28 categories of practices meeting the authors' evidence-based criteria. Its evidence base contained 44 studies: eight group-design studies and 36 single-case design studies.

Those figures count studies. They are not effect sizes. The studies varied in participant age, procedures, setting and outcomes. It would therefore be incorrect to say that 44 studies independently confirmed the effectiveness of one identical programme.

### Systematic review of school-based studies

The review by Ragaglia, Trovato and Bulgarelli first appeared online in October 2024 and was included in a 2026 journal issue. It examined 24 articles on peer-mediated interventions for children aged 6-12 in primary school.

Most studies used multiple-baseline designs with repeated measures for individual participants. Positive changes in social skills were frequently reported, but only five of the 24 articles included follow-up assessments, with mixed results.

Consequently, the review does not establish sustained long-term benefits for every child. Nor can its conclusions be applied directly to preschoolers, adolescents or any out-of-school programme.

[Ragaglia and colleagues' systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39467895/).

### Evidence from group studies

Chang and Locke (2016) included five group-design studies in their systematic review: four randomised controlled trials and one before-and-after study. Four took place at schools and one at a camp.

All five reported improvements in some social outcomes after the intervention, such as initiating interactions or responding to others. However, the small set of studies and differences between programmes limit confidence about whether skills generalise and persist.

[Chang and Locke's systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27807466/).

### What NICE recommends

The UK's NICE CG170 guideline recommends considering specific social-communication interventions with play-based strategies, delivered by a trained professional. For school-age children it specifically recommends considering peer mediation.

This does not mean that every programme described as “socialisation” has the same evidence base. NICE guidance also does not determine service requirements in Kazakhstan.

[NICE CG170 recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations).

## Which outcomes really matter

Minutes spent near other children do not necessarily reflect the quality of an interaction. A child may be present in a group without taking part in an activity they have chosen. Conversely, shared play without continuous talking may be a meaningful form of participation.

An example of a functional goal is that a child can independently join a game they find interesting and communicate whether they wish to continue.

When assessing progress, distinguish:

1. Spontaneous initiatives from interactions prompted by an adult.
2. Responding to an invitation from proposing a game oneself.
3. Using accessible communication, including gestures, pictures or AAC.
4. Participating with different children in everyday school activities, not only in arranged sessions.
5. Maintaining changes after specialised support is reduced.
6. The child's own wish to continue or end an interaction.

Improvement in one social skill is not evidence that a friendship has formed. Friendships cannot be guaranteed or assigned as outcomes of an intervention.

## Voluntary participation, safety and adults' responsibilities

Peers should not become therapists, permanent assistants or people responsible for another child's behaviour. Adults retain responsibility for organising the programme and ensuring safety.

Every child's participation should be voluntary, age-appropriate and allow refusal. An autistic child should not be required to play in a large group or communicate in one prescribed way. One friend, a small group, a short shared activity or playing alongside others without constant conversation may be a better fit.

Adults should also avoid publicly identifying a child as someone who “needs help making friends”. A diagnosis or personal information must not be disclosed to other children without an appropriate basis and authorisation. Repeatedly assigning one child as another's partner is not equivalent to a genuine friendship.

These are principles of respectful and safe support. They should not be portrayed as separate effects established by the studies and systematic reviews cited above.

## AAC and other forms of communication

A child may accept an invitation, suggest a game, request a break or decline participation through speech, gestures, pictures, a communication device or other augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) methods.

Access to AAC should continue during peer play. Spoken responses and eye contact should not be prerequisites for taking part.

[More about AAC](/kz/en/parents/aac-communication-guide/).

## When to reconsider the programme

Review the plan if a child is forced to participate, their refusal is ignored, a peer is made into a permanent supervisor, or the programme results in teasing or exclusion.

Other reasons for concern include persistent distress, a lack of clear goals, assessing success only by the number of contacts, restricting AAC or observing change only during specially organised sessions.

A preference to interact with one friend or to play alone for a while should not automatically be treated as programme failure.

## Questions for a school or specialist

Before starting, seek answers to the following:

1. What is the programme called and which studies examine this particular programme?
2. What goal matters to the child and were their preferences considered?
3. Who organises the activities and is responsible for safety?
4. What are peers asked to do, and can they refuse?
5. How are the child's privacy and right to choose protected?
6. Can the child use AAC, gestures and visual supports?
7. How are independent participation, generalisation and maintenance measured?
8. When will the programme be changed or stopped if it is not suitable?

Involvement of other children alone does not establish a programme's effectiveness. What matters is the specific procedure, adult training, goals and quality of implementation.

## Considerations in Kazakhstan

Similar approaches may be offered in schools, preschools, resource classrooms, clubs, camps and inclusive groups. Terms such as “peer support”, “peer mediation” and “inclusive play programme” do not guarantee equivalent content.

Check who is responsible for organisation, how voluntary participation and privacy are protected, whether appropriate communication support is available and how outcomes are assessed. International research reviews and NICE guidance do not replace checking local organisational and legal requirements.

## Key points to remember

Peer-based support can create additional opportunities for communication and participation in shared activities. Research reports positive effects on some social skills, particularly in school settings, but programmes differ and evidence of long-term maintenance and generalisation is limited.

The aim is not to maximise the number of social contacts at any cost. It is to make shared activities chosen by the child more accessible. Participation should be mutual, safe and voluntary for everyone.
