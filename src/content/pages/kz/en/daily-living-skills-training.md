## What counts as a daily living skill?

A child may know how to put on a T-shirt but wait for an adult to help every morning. A teenager may know how to prepare a snack but forget the sequence of steps. In both situations, first identify what is preventing the person from completing the task and what support could make it more accessible.

Activities of Daily Living (ADL) are basic everyday self-care activities. They include dressing, washing the face, brushing teeth, washing hands, using the toilet, eating and bathing.

Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) are more complex activities involved in managing everyday life: preparing food, cleaning, doing laundry, shopping, managing money, using a phone and travelling. As the person gets older, safe participation in community life and asking for help outside the home may become relevant.

ADL and IADL describe categories of activities, not a single universal autism therapy. Goals depend on the person's age, interests, abilities and needs. Learning should not be reduced to complying with adult demands.

## How to choose a skill and understand the cause of difficulties

Start with one activity that matters to the child or teenager in everyday life. Discuss the choice with them whenever possible. Instead of setting a goal to "improve self-care", choose something more specific: putting on a comfortable T-shirt with help only at the difficult step, or preparing a familiar cold snack using a checklist.

Observe what the person can already do and where difficulties arise. Do not immediately increase the number of sessions if the problem involves pain, unsuitable equipment or unclear instructions.

| Barrier | What to check |
| --- | --- |
| Unclear sequence | Does a short instruction or arranging objects in order help? |
| Motor difficulty | Is it easy to hold a toothbrush, open packaging or fasten clothing? |
| Sensory discomfort | Are noise, smell, texture or temperature causing difficulty? |
| Difficulty starting or finishing an activity | Does a checklist, reminder or timer help? |
| Communication | Can the person ask for help, communicate pain or refuse? |
| Learning conditions | Is there enough time, and is the adult taking over steps the person has already learnt? |

If a medical cause is suspected, appropriate assessment is needed. For example, reluctance to brush teeth may be related to pain, while difficulties using the toilet may be associated with constipation. Do not automatically interpret these situations as disobedience.

## How to break a skill into steps and teach the sequence

Task analysis helps identify the smaller actions that make up a skill. Tailor the number of steps to the person. For example, one person may only need the instruction "put on your sock", while another may benefit from learning separately how to hold the sock, insert the toes into the opening and pull it over the heel.

### Example: handwashing

The following is an illustrative baseline assessment. It is not an age-based standard or a diagnostic scale. The hygiene steps are based on [CDC recommendations](https://www.cdc.gov/clean-hands/about/).

| Step | Example observation |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Approach the sink | Independently |
| 2. Turn on the water at a safe temperature | Independently |
| 3. Wet hands | Independently |
| 4. Apply soap | Independently |
| 5. Rub palms, backs of hands, between fingers and under nails for at least 20 seconds | Visual support and adult help needed |
| 6. Rinse the soap off with clean water | Independently |
| 7. Turn off the water | Independently |
| 8. Dry hands | Gestural prompt needed |

In this example, six out of eight steps are completed without adult help. Find out why the remaining two are difficult. The height of the sink could be adjusted, an easier-to-use dispenser selected or a clear timer added. Adapt the sequence to the equipment without leaving out essential hygiene steps.

### Chaining

Once the steps have been identified, choose how to teach them.

| Approach | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Forward chaining | The person completes the initial steps they have learnt and receives necessary help with the rest. The next step is then added. |
| Backward chaining | The adult helps with the earlier steps and the person completes the task. As the skill develops, the independently completed portion expands. |
| Total-task presentation | The person carries out the entire task and receives help only where needed. |

The choice depends on task complexity and what the person can already do. If they can complete several steps, there is no need to start teaching from scratch. When there is pain or fear, investigate the cause rather than insisting that the task be completed at any cost.

## How to choose support and adapt the environment

First give the person a chance to start or complete the activity independently if it is safe to do so. Then offer appropriate support.

### Prompts and visual supports

A prompt may involve pointing to the relevant object, giving a short verbal instruction, demonstrating the action or providing a visual sequence. Some people benefit from photographs, others from a written checklist or arranging objects in the order they are used.

Provide enough help without giving more than necessary. If a gesture enables the person to complete the action, continuous verbal guidance may be unnecessary. Physical assistance requires particular attention to consent, bodily autonomy and safety. Do not hold a person's hands to force them to complete a task.

[Read more: prompting and prompt fading](/kz/en/parents/prompting-and-prompt-fading/).

### Teaching through demonstration and video modelling

An adult can demonstrate a specific action or offer a short video showing it. After watching, the person tries the action with suitable support. If the full sequence is difficult to remember, it can be shown in sections.

Video modelling has been studied as a way to teach functional living skills. This does not guarantee that any activity will be learnt after watching a video. [Read more about video modelling](/kz/en/parents/video-modeling/).

### Self-management and executive function support

When starting an activity, moving to the next step, remembering the sequence or finishing a task is difficult, external organisation aids may help. For example, a teenager may start a timer, check a shopping list or mark completed steps on a checklist independently.

These supports do not necessarily need to be removed. If the person uses them independently, they can remain part of everyday life. [Read more about self-management](/kz/en/parents/self-management/).

### Environmental adaptations and natural reinforcement

Sometimes replacing clothes with difficult fastenings, clearing unnecessary items from the table, labelling drawers or choosing a non-slip surface is sufficient. An adaptation that makes the required activity accessible is a valid outcome in its own right.

Reinforcement may be used when teaching skills. For example, finishing the preparation of a snack allows the person to eat it. Additional reinforcement can be considered if it genuinely supports learning and does not become pressure. Basic needs, food, water, rest and access to communication must not depend on task completion.

## Practical examples

### Dressing

If an adult dresses the child completely in the morning because time is limited, choose a quieter time to practise with comfortable clothes. Observe whether the child can distinguish the front and back of a T-shirt and put their head and arms through the openings. Help only where the action is not yet accessible. Later, try a similar T-shirt without changing all the conditions at once.

### Preparing a cold snack

Choose a familiar food for a teenager that does not require using a cooker or sharp objects. Consider food textures that are safe for the person and any dietary restrictions.

1. Gather the necessary ingredients and utensils.
2. Arrange them on a suitable work surface.
3. Open packages with the necessary help.
4. Assemble the snack following familiar instructions.
5. Put the ingredients away and tidy the work surface.

If a checklist allows the person to complete the task with less adult help, keep it available. When using hot water, a cooker, a knife or other hazardous objects, appropriate risk assessment and necessary supervision are required.

### Brushing teeth

Before changing the teaching approach, check whether toothache, inflammation, sensitivity to the toothbrush, the taste of toothpaste or difficulty holding the toothbrush is causing problems. Consult a dentist if pain is suspected.

A visual sequence may help the person prepare for brushing their teeth. However, it does not address medical causes of discomfort. Research on these strategies has limitations, described below.

### Toileting

When teaching toileting skills, consider constipation, pain, urinary problems, toilet accessibility and the ability to communicate the need to use the toilet. Do not punish accidents, restrict drinking or hold a child on the toilet. [Separate article on toileting skills](/kz/en/parents/obuchenie-tualetnym-navykam-pri-autizme/).

## How to assess outcomes and skill generalisation

A skill performed during a teaching session may not yet be accessible in everyday life. Generalisation means using the skill in other relevant situations. Maintenance means the skill remains accessible over time.

When observing progress, consider not only the number of completed steps but also the type of assistance, safety, discomfort, opportunities for choice and supports the person uses independently. Ongoing use of visual instructions or augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) does not in itself indicate failure.

An illustrative goal based on the handwashing example above: the child completes seven out of eight steps using accessible visual instructions without adult help, and receives the necessary support for one step. Reassess the outcome in the familiar home setting and at school if relevant. Agree on the review interval and level of support according to the child's abilities. This is an example of a goal, not an expected outcome for everyone.

If the steps cannot yet be completed at school, check the sink height, tap type, noise, and accessibility of soap and instructions. Do not assume the skill has been lost without considering differences in the environment.

## Safety, consent and privacy

### The right to complete an activity in a comfortable way

The person should be able to communicate "help", "stop", "it hurts", "I don't want to" or "break" using speech, gestures, cards or AAC. Do not remove communication aids to encourage speech. Respect refusal of physical assistance. Allow alternative ways of completing an action if they are safe and comfortable for the person.

Limit the presence of others during dressing, bathing, intimate hygiene and toileting. Consider age, personal preferences and the level of support required. Do not use humiliation, punishment, forced restraint or deprivation of water, food or other basic needs. Independent use of hazardous equipment should not be a goal merely to increase formal measures of independence.

### When to change the teaching approach or postpone practice

Stop the relevant training and contact a healthcare professional if pain, noticeable loss of previously acquired skills, repeated choking, difficulty swallowing, weight loss, marked constipation, blood in stool or urine, painful urination or new unusual motor symptoms occur. Do not delay seeking help to complete a teaching programme. Urgency depends on the person's symptoms and condition.

If choking prevents normal breathing, a first seizure occurs, consciousness is lost with impaired breathing, or sudden weakness of the face or limbs occurs, call emergency services immediately. In Kazakhstan, the ambulance number is 103 and the general emergency services number is 112. [Official information](https://www.gov.kz/situations/55/199?lang=ru).

## What is known about the effectiveness of skills training?

[NICE CG170](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/Recommendations) includes support for adaptive skills and communication in autistic children and adolescents. The 2024 AOTA guidelines also address environmental adaptations and caregiver training in occupational therapy.

The review by [Hume and colleagues (2021)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8510990/) identifies task analysis, video modelling and visual supports as practices with a research basis in autism support. This classification covers a broad range of goals and does not demonstrate that these approaches are equally effective for every daily living skill.

A [2022 review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35488176/) included 25 publications on the acquisition of daily living skills by autistic adolescents and young adults. The authors identified gaps in research on generalisation, maintenance and the inclusion of participants' and families' perspectives.

The [meta-analysis by Aydin and colleagues (2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12167301/) included 15 single-case studies. Nine met What Works Clearinghouse methodological standards fully or with reservations. None of the included studies examined self-management for community living skills. This is a limitation of the particular review, not a statement that these skills cannot be taught.

The [systematic review by Liu and colleagues (2025)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39540347/) included 38 publications on caregiver involvement in daily living skills training. Common methods included task analysis, prompting and reinforcement. Participants had a range of developmental disabilities, so the findings cannot be interpreted as an assessment of autistic participants alone or of individual methods in isolation.

In a pilot randomised trial by [Duncan and colleagues (2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12327085/), 58 adolescents without intellectual disability were allocated to the STRW programme or an active comparison group. Forty-eight completed the study. Caregivers reported greater improvements in overall daily living skills and domestic skills in the STRW group. These findings relate to a specific programme and population, not a universal prediction or evidence of long-term independent living.

For toothbrushing, the [review by Floríndez and colleagues (2025)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39056277/) found preliminary support for visual preparation. Of 36 oral care studies, 23 were rated as methodologically weak and eight as adequate. Therefore, equal benefit cannot be promised for everyone, and visual instructions cannot replace dental care.

Taken together, these findings support considering different teaching approaches but do not identify a single best method for everyone. Assess the usefulness of a particular strategy by its outcomes in the person's everyday life.

## Who can help and what to consider in Kazakhstan

Daily living skills training can be part of the work of caregivers, occupational therapists, special educators, other teachers and behaviour specialists. The choice depends on the nature of the task and the professional's competence. If organising the motor aspects of an action or choosing suitable equipment is difficult, consider discussing occupational therapy support. For difficulties learning sequences or organising the learning environment, a special educator may be helpful within their professional scope of practice.

The title "ADL therapist" does not in itself establish qualifications or authorisation to provide a medical service. In Kazakhstan, the role of an occupational therapist is described in the [occupational standard](https://career.enbek.kz/ru/professionalstandart/402/2945), and occupational therapy specialists are included in the [list of regulated professions](https://www.gov.kz/org/enbek/documents/details/146227?lang=ru). Occupational therapy is also listed among the specialties and specialisations subject to certification of healthcare professionals, approved by [Order No. ҚР ДСМ-218/2020](https://old.adilet.zan.kz/rus/docs/V2000021699), as amended by Order No. 68 of 24 June 2026. This does not mean that all daily living skills training constitutes medical occupational therapy. Check the requirements for the specific practitioner and service separately.

The term "neuropsychological correction" does not identify a specific programme. If such a service is offered, ask which methods and goals are proposed, what evidence supports them and how outcomes will be assessed.

Before starting work with a professional, consider asking:

- Which skill has been chosen and why does the person need it?
- What can the person already do and what help do they need?
- Should the environment be adapted or should a healthcare professional be consulted first?
- What methods and supports will be used, and how will refusal be respected?
- How will the reduction of unnecessary assistance and retention of helpful adaptations be assessed?
- Where and when will generalisation and maintenance be assessed?
