## 1. Proven yesterday, disproved today?

News headlines might say that a programme helped participants, only for a new study a month later to report no effect. This can make science seem unreliable. But studies differ, and every individual study has statistical uncertainty and limitations.

It is important to distinguish a genuine contradiction from a situation in which studies answered different questions or produced compatible estimates with different degrees of precision.

## 2. Two studies and one possible conclusion

![An educational diagram of two studies: study A has a narrower interval, study B has a wider interval, and the uncertainty ranges overlap.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/research/research-literacy-contradictions-diagram-en.svg)

*The data in the diagram are synthetic. It illustrates differences in estimates and uncertainty, not the effectiveness of an actual programme.*

In fictional study A, the estimated change was 3 points, while in B it was 1 point. Do the results contradict each other? Not necessarily. We need to examine confidence intervals, the age and characteristics of participants, measurement methods and data quality.

A result being statistically significant in one study and non-significant in another does not, by itself, demonstrate a statistically significant difference between the studies.

## 3. Small samples increase random variation

Imagine two classrooms with 12 pupils each responding differently to a change in schedule. A few participants with unusual support needs can substantially influence the mean result.

In large studies, random variation is often smaller. But a large sample does not correct poor sampling, inaccurate measurement or systematic errors.

## 4. Different participants mean different questions

One study may involve preschool children and another adolescents. In one setting, people have access to AAC, while in another they do not. Language, teacher training, intensity of support, medical conditions and family circumstances may differ.

We should not necessarily expect the same effect size in these situations. Such variation is called heterogeneity. Sometimes it helps us understand who might find a form of support acceptable and in which circumstances.

## 5. Researchers may have measured entirely different outcomes

Headlines may describe improvements in communication, but one study counted requests for objects, a second spontaneous messages, and a third how satisfied people were with communication.

These outcomes are not interchangeable. An increase in repeated requests does not necessarily indicate greater freedom to communicate, and no change on one scale does not rule out meaningful improvement in everyday life.

## 6. Measurements depend on who makes them

A parent who knows their child is taking part in a programme may expect an improvement. That does not necessarily mean they deliberately report inaccurately. Expectations can influence assessments.

Where possible, researchers use independent assessors, prespecified outcomes and repeated measurements in natural settings. However, eliminating every form of bias is difficult.

## 7. Participant dropout: whose experiences are missing?

Suppose 100 families started a programme but only 60 provided final data. If families who found the programme inconvenient were more likely to leave, the final results may appear better than they really were.

Information about those who dropped out, their reasons for leaving and how missing data were handled is therefore particularly important.

## 8. Publication bias: we do not see every study

Studies reporting striking positive findings may be easier to publish than studies with uncertain results. If a review includes only published studies with favourable results, the overall impression may be distorted.

Protocol registration and open reporting reduce some of these problems, but do not eliminate them completely.

## 9. Why twenty measurements create a risk of false discoveries

If researchers test many measures and report only those with low p-values, they increase the risk of finding an apparent effect by chance. This is one reason to specify primary outcomes in advance and to handle multiple testing appropriately.

We should not presume researchers are dishonest. What matters is knowing which decisions were planned, which were made later and how these changes were explained.

## 10. What are reproducibility and replication?

Reproducibility of an analysis means that someone can repeat the calculations with the same data and procedures. Replication means investigating a similar research question using new data or a new study.

These approaches complement each other. Replication may produce a different estimate, and researchers should investigate the reasons rather than automatically choose the more appealing result.

## 11. How a systematic review helps

A systematic review with prespecified methods aims to examine all eligible studies, not just those supporting a preferred conclusion. It assesses differences in settings and risk of bias, and sometimes includes a meta-analysis.

But mechanically averaging unreliable or incompatible studies does not solve the problem. Sometimes the most honest conclusion is that there is not yet enough evidence.

## 12. Four levels of certainty in GRADE

GRADE distinguishes high, moderate, low and very low certainty in the body of evidence for a particular outcome. Reasons for rating down include risk of bias, inconsistency across studies, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias.

This system does not assign a scientific rating to a person or child. Nor can a single study automatically be described as having high GRADE certainty without a proper assessment process.

## 13. How readers can distinguish a contradiction from a refinement

| If you see | Ask |
| --- | --- |
| The results differ | Did the studies examine the same people, conditions, outcomes and time periods? |
| One study is significant and the other is not | Was the difference between study results assessed directly? Overlap of intervals alone is not a formal test of differences between effects. |
| A new meta-analysis proves something | Was there a systematic search, an assessment of study quality and an evaluation of heterogeneity? |
| Scientists changed their minds | Was new evidence obtained or a systematic error corrected? |

## 14. What families should remember

There is no need to change support after every new headline. Important considerations include the child's goals, safety, expressed preferences, individual outcomes and the consistency of the wider body of evidence.

The main point: disagreement does not make science weaker. Good research investigates why results differ and revises conclusions when better evidence becomes available.
