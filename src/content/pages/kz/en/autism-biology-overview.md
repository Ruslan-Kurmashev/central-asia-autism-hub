## What is known about the biological origins of autism?

Why does a child develop autism? Can the cause be identified? Why can children with the same diagnosis have very different developmental characteristics?

Many parents ask these questions. Scientists know that autism is associated with differences in nervous system development and that genetics plays a substantial part. However, they have not found one cause that explains autism in every person.

The brain begins developing before birth. Nerve cells form, establish connections and gradually organise the systems involved in perception, movement, learning and communication. Many genes participate in this process, and some genetic variants can influence how nerve cells develop and work.

This does not mean that every autistic child carries one particular gene responsible for autism or that all autistic brains have the same structure.

One child, for example, might use very little spoken language yet understand familiar instructions. Another may speak fluently but find social communication challenging or experience painful discomfort in response to loud sounds. Both may have an autism diagnosis.

Such differences are not explained by one brain characteristic. They reflect complex combinations of biological factors, individual development and living circumstances.

## How are genes linked to autism?

Genes contain inherited information involved in the body's development and functioning. A child receives genetic material from both parents. Some DNA changes also arise for the first time in a child and may not be present in either parent.

Research indicates that inherited factors make a substantial contribution to autism.

### How large is the genetic contribution?

A large [2019 study](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2019.1411), including more than two million people in five countries, estimated the heritability of autism at approximately 80%.

This number is easily misunderstood. It does not mean that a specific child's autism was 80% caused by genes and 20% by environment or parenting.

Researchers compared large populations to estimate how much genetic differences help explain variation in the likelihood of autism between people. Heritability is a population statistic. It cannot identify why a particular child is autistic.

### Is there one autism gene?

No. There is no single gene that explains every case of autism.

Many genetic variants have been associated with autism. Some are common and have a small individual effect. Others are rare but can have a substantial effect on nervous system development.

Sometimes a child has a specific genetic condition associated with developmental differences and other medical needs. Nevertheless, having a genetic variant does not always mean a person will be autistic.

### Can a child be autistic when neither parent is diagnosed?

Yes. A child can be diagnosed with autism even when neither parent has that diagnosis.

Inherited genetic variants can occur in different combinations, while some changes arise for the first time in a child.

If a family already has an autistic child, that child's siblings have a higher average likelihood of autism than children in families without an autistic child. This does not mean other siblings will necessarily be autistic.

Questions about individual inheritance, especially after a genetic condition is identified, can be discussed with a medical geneticist.

## How does a child's brain develop?

The brain contains an enormous number of cells, including neurons, also called nerve cells. Neurons receive, process and transmit signals.

For example, when a child hears a familiar voice and turns their head, different groups of neurons contribute to processing the sound and controlling movement.

Neurons communicate through specialised connections known as synapses. As the brain develops, some connections form and strengthen while others weaken. This is part of ordinary development and learning.

### How is this related to autism?

Some genes associated with autism are involved in neuron development and connections between nerve cells. Researchers therefore investigate how differences in these processes may be associated with autism.

It is not accurate to claim that every autistic person has the same nerve-cell activity or one specific neurological impairment.

### Does the brain continue developing after birth?

Yes. The brain changes throughout life, particularly rapidly during childhood.

The nervous system's ability to alter its activity and connections is called neuroplasticity. Development and learning allow a child to acquire skills, discover ways to communicate and become more independent.

Autism does not mean that brain development has stopped. However, neuroplasticity does not prove that any characteristic of autism can be removed through training or exercises. Development and support needs differ between individuals.

## Why does autism present differently in different people?

Consider two autistic children. One barely speaks but understands familiar instructions and expresses wishes with gestures or pictures. The other speaks fluently but struggles to interact with peers and is highly sensitive to loud sounds.

Both children are autistic, but their abilities and support needs are different. Why?

Many genes and biological processes are involved in brain development, and the combination varies between people. Age, co-occurring conditions, the environment, lived experience and access to support also influence everyday life.

For example, a child who experiences substantial discomfort from noise may struggle in a crowded classroom but find it much easier to learn and communicate in a quiet setting. Autism has not disappeared in the quiet room. The conditions that enable the child to use their abilities have changed.

### What is meant by the autism spectrum?

The spectrum is sometimes drawn as a line from "mild" to "severe" autism. This overlooks the diversity of individual characteristics.

One child may have well-developed spoken language but considerable sensory difficulties. Another may need substantial communication support yet cope well with changes to a familiar routine.

It is more useful to consider several areas separately, including communication, social interaction, sensory perception, behaviour and everyday skills. Support needs can differ by situation and change with age.

[Research](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-022-01072-5) suggests that some autistic characteristics have partly distinct genetic associations. DNA analysis cannot yet reliably determine a person's abilities or future support needs.

## What may influence brain development before birth?

The brain starts developing during pregnancy. Researchers therefore study circumstances surrounding prenatal development as well as genes.

Studies examine factors such as parental age, certain illnesses during pregnancy, premature birth and particular environmental exposures. Statistical associations with autism likelihood have been found for some factors.

An association is not proof that the factor caused autism. For example, when one group has more autism diagnoses, scientists must investigate whether family genetics, parental health or other circumstances could explain the difference.

Even a confirmed statistical association cannot identify the cause of autism in a particular child.

### Can parenting cause autism?

No. The ideas that autism results from a lack of parental love, emotional coldness or incorrect parenting are not supported by modern science.

Living conditions and parenting may affect a child's wellbeing, learning and acquisition of skills, but that does not make parenting style a cause of autism.

### Are vaccines linked to autism?

Scientific research does not support a causal link between vaccination and autism.

The World Health Organization has repeatedly reviewed vaccine safety. Its [updated 2026 evidence review](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240123533) also did not find a causal link.

Sometimes the first noticeable developmental differences occur around the age of routine childhood vaccination. Two events happening around the same time does not establish that one caused the other.

Similarly, pregnancy complications do not, by themselves, establish that they caused a child's autism.

## How do researchers study autism?

Researchers use several methods to understand autism biology.

### Studying genes

Researchers compare the DNA of many people and look for genetic variants associated with autism. This can help identify biological processes involved in nervous system development.

### Studying the brain

MRI allows scientists to investigate brain structure and connections between regions. Studies sometimes report average differences between groups of autistic and non-autistic people.

Such findings do not mean that autism can be diagnosed from one brain scan.

### Monitoring children's development

Scientists investigate how language, communication, attention and other skills change as children grow. Some studies begin in infancy and continue for several years.

This helps identify when developmental characteristics appear and how they change. Researchers also study nerve cells in laboratories to investigate possible mechanisms.

Each research method answers particular questions. Stronger conclusions emerge when results are replicated across several well-designed independent studies.

## Is there a test that detects autism?

Parents sometimes ask whether a blood test, genetic analysis or MRI could determine with certainty whether a child is autistic.

There is currently no universal biological test that can do so. Diagnosis is based on a comprehensive assessment of developmental history, communication and behaviour, together with information from caregivers.

### What is genetic testing used for?

Genetic testing can sometimes identify a particular genetic condition associated with developmental differences. This may be useful for medical monitoring and assessment of co-occurring conditions.

However, a genetic test cannot replace an autism assessment. A negative genetic result does not rule out autism.

### What about MRI, EEG and blood tests?

MRI examines brain structure, EEG records the brain's electrical activity, and blood tests help assess particular health conditions.

A doctor may recommend these investigations for specific medical indications. EEG may be useful, for example, if epileptic seizures are suspected.

None of these investigations independently confirms or rules out autism. Not every autistic child needs the same tests.

The decision should be made with a qualified clinician considering developmental history, symptoms and health. See the [NICE recommendations on autism recognition, referral and diagnosis in children](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg128/chapter/Recommendations).

## What can science not yet explain?

Despite substantial progress, several questions remain unanswered.

### Why is this particular child autistic?

In most cases, scientists cannot identify one exact cause.

Even when a genetic change is found, it may be unclear why one child has certain developmental characteristics while another child with a similar change has different ones.

### Can a child's future development be predicted?

Genetic research and brain imaging do not reliably predict when an individual child will start speaking, how independent they will become or which kinds of support they will need.

Development depends on many circumstances. A person's characteristics and abilities can also change with age. A definitive prognosis cannot be made from a DNA test or brain scan alone.

### Can the biological mechanisms of autism be changed?

Researchers investigate nerve cells, genes, immune-system activity and other processes. This work may improve understanding and contribute to future medical developments.

But identifying a mechanism does not show that changing it would be safe or helpful. A substance that alters nerve-cell activity in a laboratory has not thereby been shown to improve communication or daily living skills in autistic people.

Such claims require high-quality clinical research. There is currently no basis for promising a universal treatment or the elimination of autism by targeting synapses, immune processes or the gut microbiome.

Co-occurring medical conditions may nonetheless need their own assessment and treatment.

## What does this knowledge mean for parents?

After an autism diagnosis, families often want to find its exact cause. Sometimes additional medical investigations identify a genetic condition or another health condition.

An unknown biological cause does not prevent parents and professionals from understanding a child's needs and supporting their development.

In everyday life, it is more useful to understand how the child communicates, which situations are difficult, what makes learning easier and what support is needed.

If a child finds it difficult to express wishes verbally, families might discuss gestures, pictures or alternative forms of communication with a specialist.

If noisy environments are challenging, changes to the environment may help. Concerns about sleep, food or health should be assessed for their possible causes.

Support should reflect the child's individual characteristics, not only their diagnosis.

### Which questions are useful for parents?

Parents can discuss the following with professionals:

1. How does the child express wishes, and how could communication become more accessible?
2. Are there health problems that require further assessment?
3. Which daily situations are especially difficult?
4. Which skills are particularly important for the child at present?
5. Would any additional investigations change the next steps?

Autism is associated with neurodevelopmental differences, not with an inability to develop or learn.

Research into genes and the brain can help families distinguish established evidence from unproven claims. The child's health, ways of communicating, abilities, interests and support needs remain the main considerations.
