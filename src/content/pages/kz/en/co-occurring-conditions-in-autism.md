## What does “co-occurring condition” mean?

Autism involves differences in brain development, communication, behaviour and perception. A **co-occurring condition** means that the same person has another health condition or diagnosis as well. It may be statistically associated with autism without automatically being one of its “symptoms” or consequences.

For example, an autistic child might also have ADHD, an anxiety disorder or epilepsy. Another child may have none of these conditions. An autism diagnosis alone cannot predict which additional difficulties an individual will experience.

Why is this important? Explaining every concern as “just autism” can mean missing pain, sleep problems, anxiety or a medical illness that needs treatment in its own right. NICE emphasises recognising and assessing co-occurring conditions (NICE CG170).

## 1. ADHD: attention, activity and self-regulation

![Wooden blocks spelling ADHD, referring to attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/cooccurring-adhd-blocks.webp)

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) can occur alongside autism. In daily life, this may involve difficulties sustaining attention, organising activities, waiting or controlling impulsive responses. However, these behaviours can have other explanations, including insufficient sleep, anxiety or demands that do not suit the child.

**An important distinction:** autism and ADHD are different diagnostic concepts. The fact that they may co-occur does not mean that every active or inattentive autistic child has ADHD.

When difficulties are persistent and affect learning or daily life, a professional may assess them separately, taking account of observations in different settings, the child's age and other possible explanations.

## 2. Anxiety, depression and emotional wellbeing

![A child sitting against a wall hugging their knees, illustrating emotional distress without implying a diagnosis.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/cooccurring-anxiety-child.webp)

Autistic people can experience anxiety and depression, just like anyone else. Sustained overload, unpredictable environments, communication barriers or negative social experiences may also affect wellbeing. Anxiety should not automatically be considered part of autism, but neither does every instance of avoidance or school reluctance establish an anxiety disorder.

Parents can look for **changes from the child's usual pattern**, such as a marked loss of interest in familiar activities, prolonged sleep disruption, avoidance of previously comfortable situations or a noticeable decline in mood. These observations are not a diagnosis, but they are reasons to discuss wellbeing with a professional.

Psychological and psychiatric support can be adapted to an autistic person's communication and sensory needs. NICE considers treatment of co-occurring conditions separately from support for core characteristics of autism.

## 3. Epilepsy and other neurological conditions

![Schematic illustration of a person during a seizure and another person nearby. This illustration is not a first-aid guide.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/cooccurring-epilepsy-illustration.webp)

Some autistic people have epilepsy, but an autism diagnosis does not imply epilepsy in most people. Seizures can present in different ways, not only as convulsions. **Unusual episodes involving loss of contact, changes in consciousness or a new seizure-like event** therefore require medical assessment.

Seek urgent medical help for a first seizure, breathing problems, injury or continuing impairment of consciousness. This article cannot determine from a description whether an episode was epileptic.

**Loss of previously acquired speech, social or motor skills** also warrants medical attention rather than automatically being attributed to autism. Read more in the Russian-language guide on [loss of acquired skills](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/ru/parents/rebenok-perestal-delat-to-chto-umel-poterya-navykov/).

## 4. Sleep and persistent tiredness

![A sleeping baby resting their head on their arms, illustrating sleep.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/cooccurring-sleeping-child.webp)

Difficulty falling asleep, waking during the night or pronounced daytime sleepiness may have different causes. Noise, light, anxiety, irregular routines, pain, illness and some medications can all affect sleep.

NICE advises considering **physical discomfort, medical conditions and the environment**, not only the bedtime routine. If night waking is explained exclusively as an “autism characteristic”, a treatable cause might be overlooked.

Do not independently give a child medication or supplements “for sleep” based on advice from the internet. A healthcare professional should assess whether treatment is appropriate.

## 5. Eating, digestion and pain

![Illustration of a child eating a sandwich beside fruit and milk.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/cooccurring-eating-sandwich.webp)

Some autistic children eat a limited range of foods, are sensitive to food textures or experience constipation, abdominal pain or other symptoms. Sensory differences may influence eating, but not every refusal of food has a sensory cause.

Swallowing difficulties, food allergies, constipation, dental pain and other explanations require consideration in their own right. A persistently restricted diet should prompt assessment of possible risks to nutrition and growth. NICE notes that restrictive food preferences can lead to nutrient deficiencies.

When a child cannot describe pain, changes in sleep, appetite, behaviour or responses to touch may provide important clues to discomfort. No single change, however, identifies the cause.

## 6. Intellectual development and learning difficulties

![Illustration of children looking together at a large educational book.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/cooccurring-learning-children.webp)

Autism **does not in itself mean intellectual disability**. Intellectual abilities vary among autistic people. Some have a separate intellectual disability or specific learning difficulties, while others do not.

Speaking ability is not the same as intelligence. A person who speaks little or uses augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) may understand more than they can express through speech. Equally, fluent speech does not mean that support is unnecessary.

Assessment of intellectual or learning skills should provide accessible communication methods and take account of vision, hearing, motor and sensory differences.

## How can parents avoid missing a separate health problem?

Imagine that a child starts covering their ears more often, refusing activities and sleeping poorly. Possible causes include sensory overload, ear pain, anxiety, a change in routine or several factors together. Assuming a single cause from the outset would be a mistake.

It helps to describe **what you actually observed**, rather than offering a diagnosis:

- What exactly changed, and when did it begin?
- Does it happen in different places or only in certain situations?
- Are there signs of pain, illness, tiredness or sleep problems?
- What does the child communicate using speech, gestures, pictures or another method?
- Does this affect eating, safety, learning or everyday life?

These observations can help during a consultation. Tests, referrals and treatment are chosen according to individual clinical indications. **There is no mandatory list of tests or medicines suitable for every autistic child.**

## What conclusions should not be drawn?

- An autism diagnosis does not prove that someone also has ADHD, epilepsy or an anxiety disorder.
- A co-occurring condition alone cannot predict a child's independence or future.
- Changes in behaviour are not always “behaviour problems”. They may reflect ill health or a change in circumstances.
- One questionnaire, video or article cannot replace a qualified assessment.
