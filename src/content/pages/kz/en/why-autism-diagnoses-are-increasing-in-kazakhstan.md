## Why is autism discussed more often today?

Until fairly recently, some parents in Kazakhstan spent years looking for an explanation of their child's developmental characteristics. Today, information about autism is more accessible, doctors and educators discuss early development more often, and the number of registered diagnoses has increased.

This raises a worrying question: **why are there now so many children with autism?** But the question contains an assumption: that more diagnostic records necessarily mean that more people have developed autism. Those are not the same thing.

Autism is a neurodevelopmental condition. A person does not need to receive a diagnosis in order to be autistic. A diagnosis means that particular developmental characteristics have been recognised and confirmed through a professional assessment.

To interpret headlines about an 'increase in autism' correctly, we must first ask **what was actually counted**: children newly diagnosed, children already on a register, or an estimated proportion of autistic people in the population.

## Three different measures that are often confused

| Measure | What it means | What it does not mean |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **New diagnoses registered during a year** | How many children received a relevant record for the first time during a defined period | How many children 'became autistic' in that year |
| **Children with a diagnosis on the register** | How many children appear in a registration system at a particular time | The total number of autistic children in the country |
| **True population prevalence** | The proportion of autistic people in a defined population, estimated by an appropriately designed study | It is not automatically determined by the number of patients who attended hospitals |

For example, a register may already contain children known to the system at the beginning of the year. New records are added over the year, while some children move into a different age group or leave the register. Therefore the number of children on the register and the number newly diagnosed are not interchangeable.

**Registration statistics reflect how identification and record-keeping systems work, not only the developmental characteristics of the population.**

## What do studies from Kazakhstan show?

Several scientific studies published in recent years allow us to examine trends more reliably than isolated news reports.

| Study | Data | Main result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nukeshtayeva and colleagues, 2025 | Children aged 0-17, 2016-2022 | New registrations of childhood autism increased almost fivefold and atypical autism approximately fourfold |
| Mussina and colleagues, 2026 | Registry cohort of children under 18, 2014-2021 | Rate of newly registered ASD diagnoses increased from 12.8 to 26.6 per 100,000 children |
| Baikatov and colleagues, 2026 | National registry of children under 16, 2014-2024 | Number on the ASD register per 100,000 increased from 16.8 to 190.5 |
| Mansharipova and colleagues, 2026 | Psychiatric service reports, children aged 0-17, 2015-2025 | Rate of first-time registered ASD cases increased from 3.9 to 63.5 per 100,000 |

Sources: [Nukeshtayeva and colleagues, 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1520460), [Mussina and colleagues, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-46715-3), [Baikatov and colleagues, 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2026.1880419), [Mansharipova and colleagues, 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare14223910).

All four studies found increases in registered cases, but they measured different outcomes. **Their figures cannot be combined into a single time-series chart**, because the diagnostic codes, age ranges, counting methods and outcomes differ. Registration records also exclude children who never entered the relevant systems.

### What does 'an average increase of 27% per year' mean?

Baikatov and colleagues used a statistical model to estimate the average annual change in the ASD registration indicator for 2014-2024. They calculated approximately **+27.0% per year** (95% confidence interval 25.4-28.7%).

This does not mean the indicator rose by exactly 27% in every individual year. Nor is it **the probability that a particular child will become autistic**. It is a modelled trend in administrative registration over the study period.

That study analysed 214,904 records across all the neurodevelopmental diagnostic groups it covered. The records represent **child-years**: the same child could appear in more than one year's data. It would be wrong to say that researchers identified 214,904 different children with autism.

### What did the 2015-2025 study add?

In November 2026, [Mansharipova and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare14223910) published an analysis of annual reports from Kazakhstan's psychiatric services (Form 10-f). The outcome was **cases first registered during the year** under ICD-10 codes F84.0, F84.1 and F84.5 among children aged **0-17 years**.

Nationally, the rate increased from **3.9 per 100,000 children in 2015 to 63.5 per 100,000 in 2025**. Over the same period, the availability of child psychiatrists fell from **2.06 to 1.43 per 100,000 children**.

The researchers reported substantial regional differences. In 2025, Astana had **221.4 first registrations per 100,000 children**, compared with **28.7 in Karaganda Region combined with Ulytau**. The authors analysed 16 historical territorial units. These are not the same measures as the total number of children on a register and do not estimate the true prevalence of autism.

This was an **ecological study** using aggregate data. It cannot establish that the increase in registrations was caused by a change in the number of child psychiatrists. The authors also calculated registrations per specialist, but that figure is not the actual caseload of any individual doctor.

Changes to administrative boundaries required additional estimates for some regional population denominators. Differences between regional and national child-population totals also remained for 2015-2022. These limitations affect the precision of some territorial rates.

## How does a child enter official statistics?

For a case to appear in a medical register, several steps must take place.

![Educational diagram showing a possible path from observed developmental differences to a registered diagnosis.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/research/kazakhstan-autism-registration-pathway-en.svg)

*Diagram 1. Original AutismHub schematic. It illustrates a registration process, not a quantitative chart, a prevalence measurement or a single compulsory pathway for every family.*

Importantly, **developmental characteristics do not automatically create an entry in a register**. A child may be autistic but not yet have been assessed, have a confirmed diagnosis or appear in the government's statistics under the relevant category.

Over time, the system may become better at identifying and assessing children who previously would have remained outside official records. Registered diagnoses can then increase **without a corresponding change in true population prevalence**.

### A simplified hypothetical example

Imagine a particular group of children that includes **40 autistic children**. This is a fictional number used solely for illustration, **not a statistic about Kazakhstan**.

- With limited access to assessment, only 10 of those 40 children may receive a diagnosis.
- If information and professional assessment become more accessible, 30 of the same 40 children may receive a diagnosis.
- The number of registered children triples even though the number of autistic children in the hypothetical group has not changed.

This **does not demonstrate** that Kazakhstan experienced precisely this process. It shows why a registration chart alone cannot reveal the cause of an increase.

## What changes may affect registration?

### 1. Some children may previously have remained undiagnosed

Knowledge about autism among professionals, parents and educators changes. Characteristics that might once have been attributed only to personality, speech delay or 'difficult behaviour' may now prompt a comprehensive developmental assessment.

Autism is diverse: two autistic children may differ greatly in speech, communication, sensory experiences, support needs and co-occurring conditions.

**What the evidence supports:** the registry trends are compatible with better recognition and diagnosis. **What remains unknown:** exactly what share of the increase in Kazakhstan is attributable to greater awareness.

### 2. Earlier identification may bring families to services sooner

A [2026 announcement from Kazakhstan's Ministry of Health](https://www.gov.kz/org/dsm/press/news/details/1192936?lang=ru) describes efforts to improve early identification, including use of M-CHAT-R at ages 16-30 months.

Two processes must be distinguished:

- **Screening** helps identify an elevated likelihood of developmental concerns and determine whether further assessment is warranted.
- **Diagnosis** requires a professional clinical evaluation of developmental history, behaviour and other relevant information.

An M-CHAT-R result **is not an autism diagnosis** and must not automatically become a record of confirmed ASD.

As assessment pathways become clearer, more families may reach an appropriate professional. This may increase the number of confirmed diagnoses.

### 3. Access to specialists varies across regions

Baikatov and colleagues reported substantial differences in registered rates between regions. These were associated, among other things, with the specialty of the physician involved in diagnosis. Obtaining a referral and assessment may be easier in large cities than in remote areas.

Therefore, **a high number of registered children does not prove that autism is biologically more common in a particular city**. It may partly reflect where the diagnostic system operates more intensively.

The authors also note that some private and educational assessments do not appear in the register if they are not accompanied by a government-recognised diagnostic code.

For more detail, see [Which regions of Kazakhstan register autism more often?](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/en/research/which-regions-of-kazakhstan-register-autism-more-often/).

### 4. Rules governing social benefits, services and registration change

The researchers noted faster growth in registered indicators after social and medical policy changes in **2021**, which concerned recognition of certain autism categories for disability-related benefits and subsidies.

In the 2026 study, a statistical model estimated the annual rate of change in registered ASD indicators at approximately **+23.5% for 2014-2019**, **+28.0% for 2019-2021** and **+33.7% for 2021-2024**.

Timing must not be mistaken for causation. The study shows that changes in the trend **coincided** with changes in policy. It **was not an experiment** capable of showing exactly what portion of the increase was caused by the new rules.

New rules may affect families' willingness to request assessment, how diagnoses are documented and whether records enter the register. This matters when comparing the years before and after administrative changes.

### 5. Similar developmental characteristics may receive different diagnostic codes

In the past, some children may have been given a different or broader developmental diagnosis. After further assessment, some may subsequently receive an ASD diagnosis.

The 2026 study found that a rising proportion of ASD records coincided with changes in the shares of other diagnostic categories. However, the researchers cautioned that **their data could not track a diagnosis being replaced for a specific child**. The register does not reliably reconstruct individual movement between categories.

It is therefore appropriate to say the findings are **consistent with changes in diagnostic practice**, not evidence of large-scale 'diagnosis replacement'.

## Does rising diagnosis mean autism was rare in the past?

No. The absence of a diagnosis in historical medical records **does not mean autistic people did not exist**.

Different diagnostic categories may have been used in the past, some families had little access to specialists, and some autistic people never came into contact with specialist medical services.

This is particularly important for adults: a register of children cannot establish how many autistic adults live in Kazakhstan.

## Could the true prevalence of autism also change?

The scientifically appropriate answer is that **administrative statistics alone cannot rule out this possibility, but these studies do not demonstrate it**.

The WHO notes that estimates of autism prevalence vary between studies and that high-quality data are lacking for many countries. Differences may reflect eligibility criteria, identification methods, age and access to services.

To determine whether the true prevalence of autism in Kazakhstan has changed, comparing hospital patient numbers is insufficient. Population studies would need to:

1. Recruit representative samples of children across regions.
2. Apply consistent age ranges and definitions of autism.
3. Use standardised procedures for identification and diagnostic confirmation.
4. Include children who had not previously accessed specialist services.
5. Repeat the study in later years using comparable methods.

Such a design can help reduce the influence of changes in registration practices.

### What about air pollution, diet or vaccines?

The observed increase in registration rates **does not show that any particular environmental exposure, diet or vaccination caused it**.

Genetic and environmental risk factors are investigated separately in studies designed for those questions. Population-level associations do not identify the cause of autism in an individual child.

High-quality research and assessments by international organisations **do not support a causal link between vaccination and autism**. More on biological mechanisms and causes is available in [Why does autism develop? What science tells us](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/en/parents/why-autism-develops/).

## Why should increasing identification not cause panic?

When professionals recognise autism more accurately, more children and families have an opportunity to understand their characteristics and needs. A diagnosis itself **does not worsen** a child's development or cause autism.

At the same time, rising service use means that government, schools and support providers must plan resources, including access to assessment, ongoing support, educational accommodations and clear information for families.

The most useful question is therefore not only 'How many new diagnoses were recorded?' but also:

- How much time passes between a family's first request and a professional assessment?
- Can families in every region obtain such an assessment?
- Are recommendations after assessment understandable?
- Can children access the support they need?
- Does families' quality of life improve after they obtain support?

These measures can tell us more about the performance of services than a single total of registered diagnoses.

## What does this mean for an individual family?

Parents do not need to judge their child's development by news headlines, percentages showing registration growth or the position of their region in a ranking.

If there are developmental concerns, it can help to record specific observations and discuss them with a qualified professional. Online questionnaires should not be used to make an independent diagnosis. Nor should a family delay seeking advice because its region has relatively few registered cases.

For further reading, see AutismHub's [statistics on registered children in Kazakhstan (Russian version)](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/ru/research/statistika-autizma-kazahstan-2020-2026/) and the [Support in Kazakhstan](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/en/help-kazakhstan/) section.

## What is established, and what remains uncertain?

**Known:** the number of registered autism diagnoses in Kazakhstan has risen substantially. The observed trend is affected by how identification, professional diagnosis and administrative registration operate.

**Plausible:** improved recognition, greater access to diagnostic services, changing coding practices and social policies all contributed to the rise. Their individual causal contributions to the national trend have not been measured.

**Unknown:** whether the true population prevalence of autism among children in Kazakhstan changed over the same period, and what proportion of the change in registration was attributable to each specific factor.

**Main conclusion:** **more registered diagnoses do not establish an autism epidemic**. They show that more children have become visible to the system. Further research is needed to explain the full increase.
