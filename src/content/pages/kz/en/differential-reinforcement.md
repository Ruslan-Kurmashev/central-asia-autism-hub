When a task becomes too difficult, a child may get upset, push the paper away or raise their voice. An adult may try to stop this response, but the first question should be what the child needs: a clearer explanation, rest, an accessible way to ask for help or a change in the environment.

Differential reinforcement can support the learning of an action that works better for the child. For example, they learn to say "Help" or communicate it through a gesture or AAC, and the adult responds. The key question is not whether the child has become more "obedient" but whether they now have an accessible way to solve the difficulty.

## What is differential reinforcement?

Differential reinforcement refers to procedures in which different actions, or different measures of an action, receive different reinforcing consequences. For example, an adult responds more promptly and clearly to an accessible request for help than they previously responded to a less safe way of signalling difficulty. If the child begins making that request more often in similar situations, the adult's response may be functioning as reinforcement.

This is a learning principle, not a separate autism therapy or a ready-made programme for everyone. Choosing a procedure depends on the goal, the reason for the difficulty and the child's abilities. Not every action that others find unusual needs to change. Safe repetitive movements may help someone regulate themselves and are not, on their own, a reason for intervention.

## First understand why the difficulty occurs

Before changing the consequences of an action, consider the circumstances. A child may move away from a task because of pain, tiredness, anxiety, sensory overload, unclear instructions or excessive difficulty. In such cases, adapting the environment or providing help may matter more than any reward system.

[NICE CG170](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations), which addresses autistic children and young people, recommends considering physical and mental health, communication, the environment and changes to routine. If difficulties persist, [functional behaviour assessment](/kz/en/parents/functional-behavior-assessment-guide/) may help investigate when an action occurs, the needs it may serve and what happens afterwards. Its function cannot reliably be inferred from appearance alone.

For example, the same shout may signal difficulty with a task, an excessively noisy setting or pain. The accessible alternative messages will differ. Signs of illness or a sudden change in condition call for appropriate medical assessment first.

## Four commonly used procedures

DRA, DRI, DRO and DRL are not four names for the same approach. They involve different rules for reinforcement, different goals and different evidence and limitations.

### DRA: reinforcing an alternative behaviour

With differential reinforcement of alternative behaviour (DRA), a specific alternative action that helps the person reach a meaningful outcome is reinforced. For example, when a child cannot understand an assignment, they show a "Help" card and the adult helps them begin. The request needs to work in practice. If the adult answers only occasionally or insists on a complicated phrase instead of an accessible gesture, the alternative may become too difficult to use.

DRA is often linked to [functional communication training (FCT)](/kz/en/parents/fct-functional-communication-training/). A request is not automatically reinforced just because it received a response: we need to observe whether the child uses it more often in relevant situations. Existing clear forms of communication, including refusal and nonverbal signals, must also be respected.

### DRI: reinforcing an incompatible action

With differential reinforcement of incompatible behaviour (DRI), the reinforced action cannot physically occur at the same moment as another targeted action. For example, during a game a child holds a ball with both hands and so cannot use those same hands to tap the table at that instant. This illustrates the principle, but it does not mean that the tapping necessarily needs to stop. First establish whether it causes harm and whether support is needed at all.

DRI should not be used to suppress harmless forms of self-regulation simply because adults find them unusual.

### DRO: reinforcing an interval without a particular behaviour

With differential reinforcement of other behaviour (DRO), reinforcement is delivered after a defined interval during which the specified target behaviour has not occurred. For example, a professional may observe whether a dangerous action occurs within a selected interval. The absence of an action does not, however, show that the person has learned to express a need.

If a child shouts less often but remains highly distressed and still cannot ask for help, the underlying difficulty remains. DRO should therefore not be presented as teaching an alternative skill. Families should not experiment independently with dangerous behaviour or withholding necessary support.

### DRL: reinforcing a lower rate

With differential reinforcement of low rates (DRL), the aim is not to eliminate a behaviour that is itself acceptable. It is to reduce its frequency when that frequency genuinely interferes with meaningful activity. Before choosing this approach, consider whether reducing the frequency benefits the person rather than only those around them.

DRL must not become a ban on asking questions, requesting a break, reporting pain or using communication. In such situations, the first step is to make information and help more accessible.

## Practical example: a child learns to ask for help

Imagine a written assignment. A child does not understand how to begin, pushes the page away and becomes upset. The adult's aim is not to stop these reactions at any cost but to make it easier for the child to get help in an understandable way.

1. The adult checks whether the task is achievable, the instructions make sense and the child is tired or overwhelmed. If needed, they change the conditions straight away.
2. The child is offered an accessible way to request help: speech, a gesture, a card or AAC. Already understandable requests and refusals must continue to be accepted.
3. When the child asks for help, the adult responds meaningfully by explaining the first step, demonstrating it or shortening the task. Needed help must not be withheld just to practise making a request.
4. In similar situations, the adults observe whether the child increasingly communicates difficulties independently, whether distress decreases and whether the child can use this communication with other adults.

Even if the child learns to ask for help, the assignment may remain too difficult. The environment and task must still be adapted when needed. Differential reinforcement does not replace changes to the learning environment.

## Must adults stop responding to the earlier behaviour?

Not necessarily. DRA is sometimes incorrectly described as "reinforce the new skill and completely ignore the old behaviour". Extinction has a narrower meaning: a specific consequence that previously maintained a behaviour is no longer delivered after that behaviour. It is not the same as simply withholding adult attention.

[Vollmer et al. (2020)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32196670/) proposed a definition of DRA that does not require extinction. A systematic review by [MacNaul and Neely (2018)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29117712/) included ten studies of DRA without full extinction. Nine reported positive findings and one reported mixed findings. The evidence is small and heterogeneous. It does not mean that every form of DRA will work without professional assessment.

If a child communicates pain, fear or sensory overload, or asks for help, they must not be ignored in order to obtain the "correct" response. Self-injury, dangerous aggression or running away require an individual safety plan led by competent professionals. Parents should not deliberately provoke these situations or test what happens when they stop responding.

## What does the research show, and what are its limits?

The volume and quality of evidence are not the same for all procedures. A review by [Weston et al. (2018)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29169240/) included 45 studies of DRO in autistic children. That is the number of studies meeting the review criteria, not 45 proofs that DRO suits every child.

[Cividini-Motta, Livingston and Efaw (2024)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38966267/) reviewed ten articles on differential reinforcement during skill acquisition. A common arrangement was to provide more favourable reinforcement for an independent response than for one made after a prompt. For most participants, this was associated with more acquired responses or faster learning. Participants, settings and goals differed, so the results cannot automatically be applied to every home programme.

When comparing procedures, ask not only whether DRA, DRI, DRO or DRL is used, but also which skill is taught, how progress is measured and whether there are adverse effects. [NICE NG11](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng11/chapter/recommendations) discusses functional assessment, adapting the environment, reinforcement schedules and quality of life. However, it applies to people with learning disabilities whose behaviour challenges and cannot be treated as universal guidance for all autistic children. Both NICE documents are UK guidance, not legislation governing services in Kazakhstan.

## Safety, communication and the right to refuse

Basic needs must never become rewards that a child has to earn. Drinking water, food, toilet access, pain treatment, safety, sleep and necessary rest must not depend on completing an instruction. Where a device is the child's main AAC system, restricting access to it as a reinforcement technique is unacceptable. The child must remain free to refuse, request an explanation or stop an activity.

## How to assess results and choose a professional in Kazakhstan

Support should be evaluated in two ways at once: whether the targeted behaviour changed and whether it became easier for the child to address a real difficulty. For example, they may ask for help independently more often, need fewer prompts, use the request at home and at school, experience less distress and retain the ability to decline an unsuitable activity. A reduction in behaviour accompanied by greater anxiety or poorer communication access is not an adequate success.

Every programme needs a monitoring plan agreed with the family: how independent requests, distress, dangerous reactions and quality of life will be tracked, who will review them and when a procedure should stop or change. Check for possible adverse effects, including increased anxiety, avoidance, self-injury or reduced access to support. If a child's condition worsens, the programme needs to stop safely and be reassessed. Pain, illness, refusal and signs of sensory overload must not be ignored to follow a reinforcement rule.

In Kazakhstan, the labels "DRA" or "ABA" do not establish anyone's qualifications. Ask how the professional explains the goal and the proposed function of the behaviour, what alternatives they have considered, which needs the child can meet unconditionally, how communication and safety are protected, what will be measured and when the plan will be reviewed.

Differential reinforcement is worthwhile only when it helps a person develop a more accessible and useful way of acting, rather than simply making their behaviour more convenient for others.
