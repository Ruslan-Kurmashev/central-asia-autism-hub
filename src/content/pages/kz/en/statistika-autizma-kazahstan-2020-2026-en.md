News reports in Kazakhstan regularly describe a threefold, fourfold or fivefold increase in the number of children diagnosed with autism. It can be difficult for families to tell which figure is correct. The problem is not just when a number was published, but **what researchers or public authorities actually counted**.

This article examines published figures for 2020-2026, presents one consistent series in a chart, explains why government reports differ, and considers what Kazakhstani research tells us about autism statistics.

## The main figure: how many children were registered in 2026?

According to an [official statement by Kazakhstan's Ministry of Health](https://www.gov.kz/org/dsm/press/news/details/1192936?lang=ru), **17,196 children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) were under ongoing medical monitoring in 2026**.

Ongoing monitoring means that a child is already known to the healthcare system and is recorded in its information systems as part of the relevant medical follow-up. This figure **does not mean** that exactly 17,196 autistic children live in Kazakhstan. Some people may not have received a diagnosis, may not be included in this particular register, or may be subject to recording practices not described in the announcement.

A separate [Factcheck.kz analysis](https://factcheck.kz/articles/faktchek-deputaty-o-roste-chisla-detey-s-autizmom-v-kazahstane) cites information from the National Scientific Centre for Health Development: **3,632** children were under ongoing monitoring at the beginning of 2021, compared with **16,690** at the beginning of 2026. These are two specific reporting dates. The later figure of **17,196** refers to 2026, but it should not automatically be treated as the next point in the same time series without confirming that the recording rules match.

## How the number of registered children changed in 2020-2024

In April 2025, [Inbusiness.kz](https://www.inbusiness.kz/ru/news/chislo-detej-s-autizmom-bet-rekordy-trehkratnyj-rost-za-pyat-let) published statistics attributed to the *Electronic Register of Dispensary Patients*. The figures cover children and adolescents **aged 0-17 years**.

| Year | Registered children and adolescents with ASD |
| --- | ---: |
| 2020 | 3,568 |
| 2021 | 4,886 |
| 2022 | 6,558 |
| 2023 | 9,611 |
| 2024 | 12,456 |

Within **this particular series**, the figure rose from 3,568 to 12,456, approximately **3.49 times** over four year-to-year intervals, an increase of around **249%** relative to the 2020 figure. This is a change in **registered cases**, not a measure of how much the true prevalence of autism increased.

The same report gives a separate dated figure of **12,807** children and adolescents under ongoing monitoring on **1 March 2025**. We have not automatically extended the chart with that figure because the dates of earlier observations would first need to be made comparable.

## Why figures in other government documents differ

Another published source provides a different series. A [letter from Kazakhstan's Deputy Prime Minister dated 15 June 2023](https://prg.kz/document/?doc_id=34276831) reported the following numbers of children registered with an ASD diagnosis:

| Year | Electronic-register series reported in 2025 | Series in the 2023 government letter |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 2020 | 3,568 | 6,771 |
| 2021 | 4,886 | 8,796 |
| 2022 | 6,558 | 12,087 |

The differences are substantial. It would be wrong to choose the higher or lower series simply because it looks more plausible.

**The publicly available extracts do not allow us to establish a single reliable explanation for these discrepancies.** Different reporting dates, age ranges, diagnostic codes, counting methods or register updates might explain them, but these are hypotheses that need checking against methodological descriptions and official responses.

We therefore cannot combine the 2020 figure from one report, the 2021 figure from another and the 2026 figure from a third in a single chart as though they measured one consistent indicator.

## Registered counts are not the same as autism prevalence

Four terms are worth distinguishing when reading this kind of statistic.

**Number of registered cases.** The number of people with a particular diagnosis recorded in a specified administrative system at a given date or during a given period. It does not necessarily include every autistic person in the country.

**Register-based prevalence.** The number of registered cases in a given age group divided by the population size of that same age group, often expressed per 100,000 people. It depends on how completely people are identified and entered into the register.

**Population prevalence.** An estimate of the proportion of autistic people in a defined population, obtained from a study with an appropriate sample and methods. The quality and representativeness of the study determine how widely its findings can be applied.

**Incidence or the number of new diagnoses.** These terms are sometimes used imprecisely in autism reports. Receiving a new diagnosis must be distinguished from the emergence of the neurodevelopmental condition itself. This matters especially when diagnosis occurs years after birth.

**Example.** Suppose a region has 100,000 children and its medical register lists 200 children diagnosed with ASD. The register-based rate is 200 per 100,000, or 0.2%. That does not establish that exactly 0.2% of the region's children are autistic: some children who might benefit from diagnostic assessment may not have been identified.

## What Kazakhstani researchers found in the national register

In July 2026, researchers based in Kazakhstan published [*Diverging trends in neurodevelopmental disorder diagnoses among children aged below 16 years in Kazakhstan (2014-2024)*](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2026.1880419) in *Frontiers in Psychiatry*.

They examined national electronic healthcare records covering **2014-2024** for children **younger than 16 years**. This was a repeated cross-sectional analysis of recorded medical data, not a population-wide clinical assessment of every child in Kazakhstan.

The overall dataset contained **214,904 records** across several groups of neurodevelopmental diagnoses. An important distinction is that **214,904 refers to child-year records, not unique children**. The authors reported 68,384 distinct identifiers across the full dataset, but that number covers all diagnostic groups studied, not autism alone.

For ASD, the register-based rate increased as follows:

| Study year | Registered ASD cases per 100,000 children under 16 |
| --- | ---: |
| 2014 | 16.8 |
| 2024 | 190.5 |

Using a statistical model, the authors estimated an average annual percentage change of about **+27%** over the period. Registration increased unevenly between regions, with higher recorded rates in large cities and some northern and eastern regions.

### What the study did and did not establish

The analysis documents a large increase in *registered* ASD diagnoses in the national system over ten years, as well as variation across regions. It also discusses differences in diagnostic practices and the organisation of specialist services.

However, register data **cannot measure the entire true prevalence of autism** and cannot prove that any particular reform caused the observed changes. The authors considered the possible roles of specialist availability, referral pathways, diagnostic practices and reimbursement rules.

A different Kazakhstani research group published a [study of trends in 2016-2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1520460) in 2025. It also reported rising registrations and regional differences. The studies complement one another but differ in scope, definitions and methodology. Their numerical results should not be combined or appended to the administrative 2020-2026 series without checking comparability.

## Does an increase in registrations mean autism has become more common?

**That conclusion cannot be made automatically.** Several steps lie between a child's developmental characteristics and their appearance in a medical register:

1. A parent, teacher or healthcare professional notices developmental differences.
2. The family is able to access professional assessment.
3. A qualified professional conducts a comprehensive assessment and, where appropriate, establishes a diagnosis.
4. The diagnosis is recorded correctly in the state information system and remains available for counting.

If access to services, referrals, awareness, diagnostic criteria or registration practices changes at any of these stages, the number of visible cases may increase **even without a comparable change in the true prevalence of autism**.

This does not mean that identification is complete or that administrative factors explain every difference. Answering questions about population prevalence requires well-designed population studies with clear sampling methods and consistent assessment criteria.

## Can Kazakhstan's figures be compared with global statistics?

In its [updated autism fact sheet](https://www.who.int/ru/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/autism-spectrum-disorders), the WHO states that, according to an estimate for **2021**, about **one person in 127** worldwide was autistic. This is approximately **0.79% of people across all age groups**.

That figure cannot be directly compared with **17,196 registered children** in Kazakhstan: the measures have different units, age groups and methods. Even the Kazakhstani register rate per 100,000 children under 16 is not equivalent to a global all-ages estimate.

It would also be misleading to multiply the global average by Kazakhstan's population and present the result as an officially established number of autistic people in the country. Such a calculation would only be an approximate illustration based on an unverified assumption that the populations are comparable.

## What the figures mean for families and support services

The figures primarily point to **a growing recorded demand on systems that identify and support children with ASD**. Even if part of the increase reflects improved identification, a larger number of registered families may need accessible consultations, educational support and reliable information about available services.

However, **one diagnosis count cannot tell us** how many places are needed in a particular service, how much support individual children require or how many families have not received an assessment. Planning requires additional information on age, individual needs, specialist availability, waiting times, education and regional differences.

National statistics are not a tool for assessing an individual child. When a family has questions about development or support, it is more useful to seek professional assessment of needs and information about available support pathways than to rely on a regional autism rate.

## What data still need to be clarified?

Reliable, publicly accessible statistics for Kazakhstan would require regularly published tables specifying, for every year:

- the exact **reporting date** and registration system
- the **age group**, for example 0-15 or 0-17 years
- included **diagnostic codes** and rules for avoiding duplicate counts
- the number of registered people and **first-time diagnoses**
- the population denominator for the same age group, to calculate rates per 100,000
- missing data, methodological changes and revisions of earlier figures.

Until a single consistent series for **every year from 2020 to 2026** can be confirmed from accessible primary sources, it is more accurate to publish clearly distinguished snapshots than to draw a seemingly smooth line through incompatible figures.

## In brief: how to read news about autism statistics

Whenever you see a new figure, ask four questions: **when** was it counted, **who** was counted, **which system** supplied the data and **what measure** is being reported? Registered counts are useful for understanding the demands placed on services, but they do not equal the full population prevalence of autism.
