When parents say that their child "is not talking much yet", they may be describing very different situations. One child understands everyday requests and uses gestures to express wishes but uses few spoken words. Another says many separate words yet finds a long question difficult to understand. A third communicates with family members in two languages and selects words from both.

To understand what needs attention, it helps to look beyond spoken words and consider **understanding, gestures, initiative, ways of expressing needs and participation in communication**.

This article explains what you can notice at home, how to describe your observations to a professional and when not to delay seeking advice. It does not replace medical or speech and language assessment.

## 1. Speech, language and communication: what is the difference?

In everyday conversation, we often use "speech" to refer to almost everything related to communication. Professionals distinguish several aspects.

**Communication** means conveying a message to someone else. A child might ask for water, refuse food, call for help or share enjoyment using words, gestures, looking towards an object, a picture or an action. Not all of these require spoken language.

**Language comprehension** (receptive language) is the ability to understand messages: words, combinations of words, questions and explanations in context.

**Language expression** (expressive language) is the ability to convey meaning independently using words, phrases, sentences or other language forms.

**Speech production** refers to the physical production of sounds and words, including articulation, clarity, fluency and voice. Sometimes a child knows the word they want but finds it difficult to say. At other times a child pronounces words clearly but struggles with their meaning or use.

These aspects are related, but none is an exact measure of another.

### Example: two children say the same word

One child says "ball" to request a ball, directs the word towards an adult and later uses it in a different situation. Another says "ball" while repeating a line from a familiar song but does not yet use it to request a ball.

Both experiences matter for development. However, the number of spoken words does not explain **how the child uses language to communicate**. Repetition alone does not establish what a child understands or indicate a diagnosis.

## 2. How communication begins before the first words

Even before a child begins speaking, they may develop ways of expressing needs, responding to people and sharing interests.

You might notice how your child:

- responds to familiar voices and sounds
- makes sounds and changes their intonation
- responds when addressed with movement, a smile, gesture or sound
- reaches towards or indicates a desired object
- draws an adult's attention to an event or interesting object
- uses familiar actions to request, refuse or greet
- gradually connects other people's spoken words with objects and actions.

These are **areas to observe**, not a checklist of required actions for every age. Motor, sensory and other differences can affect which communication methods are accessible.

For example, a child may not point with a finger but may draw an adult's attention in another way that works for them. Describe what actually happened and discuss it in the context of the child's abilities, rather than equating the absence of one gesture with an absence of communication.

## 3. Understanding language: what can you notice at home?

Sometimes a child appears to understand everything because they participate smoothly in familiar routines. In fact, they may be using words, gestures, visible objects and the familiar sequence of events together. This is normal: people use multiple cues when communicating.

If parents want to describe comprehension of spoken words specifically, it helps to distinguish between situations.

| Everyday situation | What you can observe | What one event cannot tell you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Let's get our shoes, we're going out" | The child goes to the door after hearing the words and seeing a coat. | You cannot tell whether they responded to the sentence, the coat, the routine or all of these. |
| "Bring the cup" | The child brings a cup after hearing the words without a pointing gesture. | One successful response does not prove understanding of every similar request. |
| "Put the toy on the table" | The child performs a familiar action in a calm setting. | Refusal or a lack of response later does not automatically mean they "do not understand" or "are disobedient". |
| "Would you like an apple or a banana?" | The child chooses by gesture, word, picture or taking an object. | Choosing an object alone does not show how much of the question's grammatical structure they understood. |

There is no need to turn observations into a home examination or repeatedly test your child. It is much more helpful to record **the words used, the cues that were visible, how your child responded and the conditions**.

A child may understand a familiar person better than a stranger or follow a request in a quiet room but not respond in a noisy group. Notice those differences rather than assuming someone has done something wrong.

## 4. Using words: the number alone is not enough

It is useful to notice:

1. **What messages your child communicates:** requests, refusals, greetings, questions, comments, invitations to play or reports of pain.
2. **Which means they use:** sounds, gestures, words, combinations of words, pictures or an AAC device.
3. **Whether they communicate independently or after a prompt**.
4. **Whether a message is used in different settings:** home, outings, with family members or with an educator.
5. **How communication changes over time:** whether new, understandable ways of expressing needs and interests appear.

For example, a child may initially say "more" only during a favourite song and later use it to ask someone to continue a game. That is useful information about using a familiar meaning in another context.

Communicating with a picture, gesture or communication device is also functional communication. Spoken words should not be treated as the only acceptable way to ask for something or express discomfort.

### What if a child repeats words or phrases they have heard?

Repeating words and phrases (echolalia) can occur during development and can serve different functions. Sometimes it is an attempt to join an interaction, make a request, recall a familiar situation or process information. You cannot infer its meaning from one repetition alone.

It is more useful to consider **when the phrase was said, who it seemed directed towards and what happened next** than to assume it was meaningless or insist that repetition stop immediately. Ongoing questions about understanding and using language call for an individual assessment by a professional.

## 5. Developmental milestones: how to use them without overinterpreting

Communication develops and changes during the first five years. Official CDC and ASHA age-based resources describe abilities that most children typically develop by particular ages. These are guides for discussion, **not diagnostic thresholds**.

For example, CDC milestones at **18 months** include trying to say **three or more words** besides "mama" and "dada", as well as following a one-step direction without a gestural cue. At **2 years**, milestones include combining at least two words. One milestone cannot diagnose a language delay or autism, and concerns that arise earlier should not be left until the next age category. Use the age-appropriate checklist for your child.

See the interactive AutismHub guide [Child development from 2 months to 5 years: milestones for parents](../child-development-milestones/). It covers several developmental domains, not language alone.

Also record what your child can already do, which messages they convey without words and what helps them participate in communication. Comparing word counts alone gives an incomplete picture.

## 6. A bilingual family: Russian, Kazakh and other languages

In Kazakhstan, a child might hear Russian at home, Kazakh at preschool and a third language from relatives or their surroundings. This does not automatically mean their development is "delayed".

ASHA professional guidance emphasises that **bilingualism itself does not cause a language disorder**, and stopping the use of a family language is not a standard way to treat delayed language development. An assessment should consider which languages a child hears and uses, from what age, in which situations and with which accessible communication methods.

For example, using the word "су" with a grandparent and "вода" with a parent involves two languages, but both demonstrate the use of words to refer to the same concept, water. Depending on the question, a professional may examine the vocabulary in each language, combined vocabulary and concepts understood. Assessment cannot be reduced to adding words together into an arbitrary "score".

For parents, the practical message is to communicate naturally in the languages you know best, notice communication in each language and tell the professional about your child's whole language environment.

## 7. Supporting communication in everyday life

Conversation, play, reading and shared activities offer natural opportunities to develop language. They do not require expensive equipment and should not become constant lessons or pronunciation tests.

### At mealtimes

If your child points to a cup, you could respond, "Water? Here's water," show the cup and give them time to respond in their preferred way. There is no need to demand that they repeat "water" before giving them a drink.

### On a walk

Follow your child's interest: "Look, a bus", "It's moving", "A big bus". Pair words with gestures, pointing to the object or a picture if helpful. Do not insist on eye contact before talking.

### While reading

You do not have to finish a book from beginning to end. Pause at a picture that interests your child, describe it with simple words and allow them to turn the page, gesture or point to the image.

### During play

You can copy your child's action, add one new word or small shared element and see whether it interests them. Respect signs that they are tired, do not wish to interact or need quiet time.

These everyday examples are consistent with responsive interaction described in WHO Caregiver Skills Training. They **do not guarantee any particular rate of speech development** and cannot replace support recommended by a professional.

## 8. When a hearing assessment may be needed

Hearing is important for perceiving speech sounds, but everyday responses cannot reliably establish that a child hears all relevant sounds and frequencies equally well.

For example, a child may respond to loud music yet have difficulties perceiving some speech sounds. Saying that "they can hear cartoons" does not rule out a hearing difficulty.

If you have questions about your child's speech or understanding, ask your doctor whether **a hearing assessment by an appropriate professional** is needed, even if the child passed newborn hearing screening or responds to some sounds. Ear infections, medical history and other factors may also be relevant.

Hearing assessment, speech and language assessment and general developmental assessment answer different questions. One does not automatically replace another.

## 9. When to seek professional assessment

Speak to a doctor or appropriate professional if:

- you are concerned about how your child understands speech or communicates needs
- age-related milestones are not being reached, or development seems unusual for your child
- their use of words, gestures or interaction methods has changed noticeably
- communicating is so difficult that it affects everyday life
- an educator or another familiar adult also notices persistent difficulties
- you have doubts about hearing, recurrent ear problems or a need to turn the volume up frequently
- previously established words or communication skills have been lost.

**Loss of previously established speech, social or motor skills calls for timely medical assessment.** You do not need to wait a month, try home exercises first or complete every observation table. Sudden marked deterioration, altered consciousness, a new significant loss of motor function or other serious acute symptoms require urgent medical care.

Do not try to determine for yourself whether the signs mean "autism or just a speech delay". Difficulties can have different causes and any conclusion requires consideration of developmental history, hearing, communication and the child's general health.

## 10. A short observation sheet before an appointment

You can write just five specific examples. There is no need to award points or calculate scores.

| Question | Example of a short note |
| --- | --- |
| How does your child let you know they need something? | "Takes my hand to the cupboard with cups and points to one." |
| How do they express refusal or discomfort? | "Pushes the plate away and says 'no', then points to water when asked." |
| Which words or messages do they understand? | "Brings the coat when asked in a quiet room without a gesture, often does not respond in noise." |
| Which words, gestures or pictures do they use? | "Says 'give', waves goodbye, sometimes shows a card for 'more'." |
| When is communicating easier or harder? | "Communicates more readily when playing with a familiar adult than immediately after preschool." |

Before the appointment, you can also note **when your questions first arose, which languages are used in the family, any hearing concerns and what other adults have noticed**.

A more detailed printable form is available through [How to observe your child's development at home](../how-to-observe-child-development-at-home/).

## What not to do

- Do not force a child to repeat words many times as a test.
- Do not withhold something they need, including water or food, until they say a word.
- Do not regard gestures, pictures or AAC as "incorrect" communication.
- Do not stop using a family language because of the belief that two languages necessarily "confuse" a child.
- Do not treat one incidental observation or an online test as a diagnosis.
- Do not delay hearing or developmental assessment because of the hope that difficulties will necessarily disappear on their own.

## What to remember

**Speech is only one part of communication.** To understand your child's development, notice what they understand, what they try to communicate, how they express their thoughts and needs and in which settings interaction is easier.

If you have concerns, specific observations are more useful to a professional than trying to make a diagnosis yourself or demanding that a child meet one isolated speech milestone.
