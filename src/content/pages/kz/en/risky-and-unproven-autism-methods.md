Families of autistic children may be offered sessions, medicines, "body cleanses", devices, diets or procedures claimed to "repair brain function". Some are ordinary services promoted with exaggerated advertising. Others are interventions capable of causing serious harm. Still others are genuinely being investigated but have not become accepted standards of care.

It is impossible to reliably list every commercial name ever used. This overview covers prominent categories, including historical proposals documented in studies, clinical reviews or official safety warnings. Names are included for recognition only, not as a list of interventions to try.

## Distinguishing harm, lack of benefit and experimental research

These situations must not be confused.

| What is known | How to interpret it |
|---|---|
| **Evidence of harm exists** | Toxicity, injury, infection or other serious complications are documented. Without established benefit, such risks are unjustified. |
| **Studies did not confirm the proposed effect** | The intervention was tested, but convincing benefit for the claimed purpose was not found. This is different from "not yet studied". |
| **Too little evidence** | Small studies, no control group or inconsistent findings prevent confident claims of effectiveness. |
| **Experimental approach** | Research is continuing. Participation in properly supervised research is different from buying a commercial procedure. |

Conclusions depend on what was measured. Better sleep, less pain, new ways to communicate and "eliminating autism" are different claims. A treatment effective for a separate disease cannot automatically be marketed as an autism treatment.

## Toxic "cleanses" and approaches based on false assumptions about autism's causes

### Chlorine dioxide, MMS and other "antimicrobial cleanses"

Chlorine compounds have been sold under names such as MMS, "Miracle Mineral Solution" and "cleansing protocols". Some sellers recommended not only swallowing them but also using them for bowel cleansing. The claim was that unknown parasites or "toxins" cause autism.

This causal theory is not supported by evidence. Chlorine dioxide does not treat autism. FDA reports have described severe vomiting, diarrhoea, dangerous drops in blood pressure and acute liver injury after consuming such substances. Local use can also damage mucous membranes. These are not harmless supplements or a way to "remove autism" from the body. [FDA warning](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/congressional-testimony/protecting-americans-covid-19-scams-written-testimony-only-07212020).

### Chelation, "mercury removal" and unreliable heavy-metal testing

Chelating agents bind certain metals and are used by physicians for confirmed poisoning. Alternative protocols have offered them to autistic children on the assumption that mercury or other metals cause autism, sometimes after questionable "provoked" tests.

A Cochrane review found no established benefit of chelation for autism. Severe electrolyte disturbances, kidney injury and fatal complications have been reported. Genuine metal poisoning may require treatment, but this is determined by a separate medical diagnosis, not by an autism diagnosis. [Cochrane](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD010766_chelation-autism-spectrum-disorder-asd), [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/medication-health-fraud/questions-and-answers-unapproved-chelation-products).

### "Vaccine detox", enemas, sorbents and homeopathic "toxin removal"

Over the years, advocates have promoted "vaccine detoxification", cleansing enemas, multiple sorbents, special baths, homeopathic products and other schemes supposedly removing substances that "cause autism". Merely finding a substance in a body does not show that it caused developmental differences or that a proposed procedure will remove it.

A WHO review published in 2026, covering research from 2010 to 2025, confirmed that the strongest studies do not support a causal link between vaccination, including thiomersal-containing vaccines, and autism. Thus, a treatment scheme premised on "clearing vaccines from a child" lacks a demonstrated causal basis. Enemas and other cleansing procedures also have their own risks of injury, dehydration and electrolyte imbalance. [WHO](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240123533).

### "Antiparasitic" protocols and deliberate administration of parasites

Attributing autism to parasites without a confirmed infection is incorrect. Research literature even includes attempts to administer eggs from certain intestinal helminths for a proposed immune effect. A small pilot study in adults did not find a statistically convincing effect between the groups compared. This is not an established autism intervention and does not justify self-administering parasitic materials, antiparasitic drugs or cleansing procedures. [Pilot study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30230399/).

### Ozone, "bioresonance", frequency and energy cleansing

Such services may promise to "remove pathogens", "reset the immune system" or "correct brain frequencies". Their claimed treatment of core autistic characteristics has no convincing objective evidence. One advertised label may cover both non-invasive sessions and medical interventions with very different levels of risk. Safety therefore cannot be judged by a marketing name alone. Promises to eliminate autism without clinical studies or a plausible mechanism are particularly suspect.

## Medically risky procedures offered as autism treatments

### Hyperbaric oxygen therapy

Hyperbaric oxygen has legitimate medical uses for certain other conditions. For autism, it has been advertised to improve speech, behaviour and "oxygenation of the brain".

A Cochrane review found no confirmed improvement in core autistic characteristics and reported cases of ear barotrauma. NICE recommends against hyperbaric oxygen for autism treatment. Genuine indications for hyperbaric treatment due to another condition must be assessed separately. [Cochrane](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD010922_high-pressure-oxygen-therapy-children-and-adults-autism-spectrum-disorder-asd), [NICE](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/Recommendations).

### Stem cells, cord blood, exosomes and "cell cocktails"

Services may advertise "neuron repair", "brain regeneration" or an "immune reset". Cell-based products and exosomes have not been established as standard autism treatments. Individual scientific studies do not justify selling them as already effective therapies.

The FDA warns of infections, immune reactions and other serious complications from unapproved regenerative products. Approval of certain cellular medicines for blood disorders does not authorise their use for autism. [FDA consumer alert](https://www.fda.gov/vaccines-blood-biologics/consumers-biologics/consumer-alert-regenerative-medicine-products-including-stem-cells-and-exosomes).

### Faecal microbiota transplantation and "dysbiosis treatment"

Differences in gut microbiota between autistic and non-autistic people are being studied. However, an association does not prove that changes in bacteria cause autism or that transplanting microbiota can reverse it.

Faecal microbiota transplantation has distinct medical uses, but remains experimental for autism. The FDA has warned about severe infections, including transmission of harmful bacteria from donor material. This is especially concerning when a paid procedure is offered without a properly supervised study and microbiological safeguards. [FDA safety alert](https://www.fda.gov/vaccines-blood-biologics/safety-availability-biologics/safety-alert-regarding-use-fecal-microbiota-transplantation-and-risk-serious-adverse-events-likely).

### Drugs that suppress sex hormones

Historical proposals have included suppressing sex hormone production in autistic children with drugs used in endocrinology for other conditions. In one notable case, Maryland's medical board regarded such practices as risky and inconsistent with standards of care. This is not an accepted autism treatment. A genuine endocrine condition must be treated separately on medical grounds. [Geier court case](https://law.justia.com/cases/maryland/court-of-special-appeals/2019/0338-18.html).

## Medicines and immune-based regimens without an established indication

### Antifungals, antibiotics, antiparasitic and antiviral medicines

Autism is sometimes incorrectly attributed to Candida, a "hidden infection", parasites or a chronic virus. Long courses of medicines may then be offered without diagnosis of the relevant illness.

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) found no support for antifungal drugs as treatments for autistic characteristics. Antimicrobial medicines have adverse effects and interactions, and uncontrolled antibiotic use contributes to antimicrobial resistance. Confirmed infections, by contrast, should be treated for their own medical indications. [AAP](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2019-3447), [NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/providers/digest/autism-spectrum-disorder-and-complementary-health-approaches-science).

### Immunoglobulins, immunomodulators and anti-inflammatory regimens

Immune-related hypotheses about autism have long been, and remain, research topics. They do not justify immunoglobulins, prolonged immunomodulator courses or other major interventions for every autistic child. Convincing clinical evidence for treating core autistic characteristics in this way is lacking, and risks depend on the particular drug. Separate immunodeficiency, autoimmune or inflammatory disease may create different medical indications. [AAP](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2019-3447).

### Secretin

In the late 1990s, the hormone secretin became widely known following reports of improvement in a handful of children. It was later tested in controlled trials. A Cochrane review of 16 randomised studies involving more than 900 children did not establish an effect on core autistic characteristics. NICE does not recommend secretin to treat autism. This is an example of early enthusiasm being overturned by stronger testing. [Cochrane](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD003495_intravenous-secretin-autism-spectrum-disorders-asd).

### Bumetanide and other medicines proposed to "reset the brain"

Researchers studied the diuretic bumetanide hoping to influence neuronal signalling. Two international phase III randomised trials, each enrolling 211 children and adolescents, were stopped early because no significant advantage over placebo was found. Thirst, frequent urination and reduced potassium were more common in those taking the medicine. This illustrates why a promising preliminary theory cannot replace large controlled trials. [Bumetanide studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37794745/).

Other proposed agents discussed in the scientific literature include suramin, naltrexone and various amino-acid and "metabolic" mixtures. Some have been evaluated in small studies, but this does not establish a safe and effective standard autism regimen. For example, a randomised pilot trial of suramin involving 52 boys did not show a convincing positive result on its primary outcome, despite signals on some exploratory measures. That is a reason for further high-quality studies, not for marketing the drug as a proven treatment to families. [Suramin study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37932739/).

### Medicines for sleep, anxiety, attention and behaviour do not treat autism itself

Antipsychotics, antidepressants, antiseizure medicines and other drugs may be prescribed for specific medical indications, including co-occurring conditions or certain severe behaviour-related difficulties under professional supervision. They must not be marketed as medicines that "remove autism". NICE recommends against using these classes specifically to treat core autistic characteristics, which is different from prohibiting them for every co-occurring condition. [NICE](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/Recommendations).

## Restrictive diets, supplements and "biomedical protocols"

### Gluten-free, casein-free, ketogenic, sugar-free and other exclusion diets

Programmes have promoted excluding gluten, casein, sugar, yeast or whole food groups as autism treatments. Convincing evidence that such restrictions treat core autistic characteristics is lacking. NICE does not recommend exclusion diets for this purpose.

This does not override genuine medical indications, such as coeliac disease or allergy. A ketogenic diet may be used under medical supervision for some forms of epilepsy, but not as a universal autism intervention. Without nutritional assessment, severe restrictions can worsen selective eating and cause nutritional deficiencies. [NICE](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/Recommendations), [NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/tips/things-to-know-about-complementary-health-approaches-for-asd).

### High-dose vitamins, minerals and supplements

Historically proposed products include vitamin B6 with magnesium, B12, vitamin D, zinc, omega-3, coenzyme Q10, carnitine, methylfolate, probiotics, enzymes, sulforaphane, dimethylglycine (DMG), trimethylglycine (TMG), carnosine, glutathione and various "mitochondrial cocktails". Some appeared in early AAP reviews. [AAP historical review](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article-abstract/120/5/1162/71080/Management-of-Children-With-Autism-Spectrum).

Evidence for treating core autistic characteristics with these products is either insufficient or inconsistent. Some substances are being studied in specific subgroups, but findings cannot be generalised to all children. High doses can cause adverse effects, while mixtures make it harder to know which substance affected wellbeing. Confirmed vitamin deficiencies should be treated as distinct medical problems. [NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/providers/digest/autism-spectrum-disorder-and-complementary-health-approaches-science), [AAP](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2019-3447).

### Homeopathy, isopathy and CEASE

Homeopathic dilutions and schemes to "remove traces of toxins" have been presented as ways to treat or "cure" autism. There is no evidence for such an effect. The UK advertising regulator has specifically challenged unsupported claims made for CEASE. Risk lies not only in what a product contains but also in delaying or abandoning necessary care. [NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/homeopathy), [UK ASA](https://www.asa.org.uk/resource/enforcement-notice-cease-therapy.html).

### Leucovorin and newer "metabolic" proposals

Folinic acid (leucovorin) has been studied in certain subgroups of children. In March 2026, the FDA approved it for a rare genetically confirmed cerebral folate transport deficiency associated with *FOLR1*. This does not constitute approval for autism generally. The AAP does not recommend routinely prescribing leucovorin to all autistic children because the available evidence remains limited. [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-treatment-patients-cerebral-folate-transport-deficiency), [AAP](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/autism/use-of-leucovorin-in-autistic-pediatric-patients/).

## Devices, sensory programmes and "brain training"

### Auditory integration programmes

Approaches known by names such as Berard or Tomatis involve listening to specially processed sounds in the hope of improving speech or sensory processing. NICE does not recommend auditory integration training for speech and language difficulties in autism. That does not mean ordinary music or comfortable headphones are harmful. The unsupported claim concerns the therapeutic effect of a standardised paid protocol. [NICE](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/Recommendations).

### Neurofeedback, EEG training, magnetic and electrical brain stimulation

Neurofeedback claims to teach the brain to work "correctly" using EEG signals. NICE does not recommend it for speech and language difficulties in autistic children. Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS/rTMS) and electrical stimulation such as tDCS are being researched for particular goals. Reviews include heterogeneous studies with often low confidence in outcomes, so these procedures cannot yet be recommended as routine treatments for core autistic characteristics. Risks depend on the device and protocol, and the safety of home devices should not be assumed. [NICE](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/Recommendations), [2026 rTMS review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42727523/).

### Sensory "diets", brushing, weighted vests, special chairs and massage

Not all sensory activities are equivalent. Changing lighting, reducing noise or allowing a sensory break chosen by the child may be helpful environmental adjustments. Claims that a fixed sequence of brushing, weights, swinging or massage will "rewire the brain" and eliminate autism require separate rigorous testing. The AAP has noted limited evidence for some sensory techniques. Weights, pressure and holding without the child's assent also raise concerns about safety and comfort. [AAP](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2019-3447).

### Craniosacral therapy, manual and "corrective" techniques

Craniosacral treatment, chiropractic, osteopathic manipulation and techniques promising to "normalise the nervous system" have also been offered for autism. There is no reliable evidence that they treat core autistic characteristics. They should not be confused with ordinary medical assessment for pain or movement problems, or safe physical activity to improve wellbeing. [AAP](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2019-3447).

## Coercive and historical practices that may traumatise a child

### "Holding therapy", "rebirthing" and forced "attachment formation"

Historically, some approaches involved physically restraining or wrapping children against their will, preventing them from escaping physical contact or deliberately provoking strong distress to produce an emotional response. These were sometimes linked to outdated, unsupported ideas that autism resulted from parents treating a child incorrectly.

Such explanations of autism are scientifically unsound. Coercive practices have been associated with psychological trauma, physical injury and tragic outcomes. NICE states that restrictive interventions must not be used to punish, inflict pain, humiliate or control a person. Emergency protective action in the face of imminent harm must not be confused with coercive "therapy". [ASAT](https://asatonline.org/for-parents/learn-more-about-specific-treatments/holding-therapy/), [NICE NG10](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG10/chapter/recommendations).

### Aversive practices, punishment and forced eye contact

Electric punishments, causing pain, humiliation, deprivation of basic needs, forced suppression of harmless repetitive movements and coercion to make eye contact are not acceptable goals for support. When assessing a behavioural programme, look beyond its name at what professionals actually do, whether the child can express discomfort or refusal, and whether there are safe ways to stop a session.

This does not mean that all skills teaching or all behavioural support is harmful. The questions concern specific goals, methods, safety and respect for the child. [NICE](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG10/chapter/recommendations), [NHS inform](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/brain-nerves-and-spinal-cord/autism/myths-about-autism/).

## Communication methods in which an adult may speak for the child

**Facilitated Communication (FC)** involves an adult assisting with pointing to letters or typing. Controlled research has demonstrated a high risk that the helper rather than the disabled person determines the message. ASHA considers FC discredited and recommends against its use.

**Rapid Prompting Method (RPM)** and **Spelling to Communicate (S2C)** involve letterboards and intensive prompting. Unlike FC, there has not been sufficient authorship testing to establish independent communication. ASHA also recommends against RPM/S2C due to dependence on prompts and lack of scientific validity. This matters especially when messages are used to make decisions on behalf of a child.

Do not confuse these methods with augmentative and alternative communication (AAC), including gestures, accessible picture cards, tablets and independent typing. AAC aims to enable people to express their own thoughts and preferences. [ASHA: FC](https://www.asha.org/policy/PS2018-00352/), [ASHA: RPM](https://www.asha.org/policy/ps2018-00351/).

## Experimental and complementary approaches: uncertainty is not prohibition

These approaches have sometimes been studied in small trials or used as additional activities, but their ability to change core autistic characteristics cannot be promised.

- **Oxytocin and other hormone hypotheses.** Results are mixed. A hormonal theory alone does not establish a medical indication for a drug.
- **Cannabis-derived medicines and products, including CBD.** Some studies suggest particular possible effects, but sample sizes are small, products differ, and long-term safety and reproducibility remain uncertain. They are not standard autism treatment or a reason to give such products to children without medical supervision.
- **Acupuncture, reflexology and manual approaches.** Individual studies exist, but evidence for changing core autistic characteristics is unconvincing. Risks vary by procedure.
- **Dolphin-assisted programmes, horse-assisted activities and other animal-based sessions.** Contact with animals or swimming may be enjoyable recreation. Claims to "treat autism" require very different evidence, and these activities have their own safety considerations.
- **Music, dance, art, yoga and massage.** Some studies suggest possible benefits for specific skills or wellbeing, but this does not mean "correcting autism". Such activities can be valuable parts of a child's life without therapeutic claims.
- **"Brain reset", "neurocorrection" and proprietary intensive programmes.** Marketing labels may combine familiar educational techniques, device-based sessions and unsupported theories. Assess concrete activities and measurable outcomes rather than a name.

For this category it is particularly important not to mistake limited evidence for proven ineffectiveness of every activity. Equally, one promising small trial cannot justify guaranteeing a result to every family. [AAP](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2019-3447), [NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/providers/digest/autism-spectrum-disorder-and-complementary-health-approaches-science).

## What should not be labelled "unproven autism treatment"?

An autistic child, like any other, may need treatment for epilepsy, constipation, allergy, pain, anxiety, sleep problems, iron deficiency or another confirmed condition. Autism does not remove these indications. In certain circumstances a physician may consider melatonin for persistent sleep difficulties, or a therapeutic diet for an unrelated condition. This treats a specific problem, not "cures autism".

Likewise, speech and language support, accessible communication, environmental adjustments, functional skills training and family support help with everyday life. They need not promise to eliminate autism in order to be useful. [NICE](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/Recommendations).

## How to assess a new offer in a few minutes

Before accepting a course, ask six questions:

1. **What exactly is the proposed change?** Pain, sleep, communication, independence or a vague "autism detox"?
2. **How will the change be measured?** Is the baseline known, are benefits and adverse effects defined, and when will the plan be reviewed?
3. **What studies exist?** Controlled studies of this particular method for the stated purpose matter more than testimonials and "before and after" photos.
4. **What is known about harm?** Are contraindications, infection, injury, nutrition, side effects and drug interactions considered?
5. **What happens if we decline?** Demands to stop medical supervision, hide product ingredients or prepay the entire course are warning signs.
6. **How is the child's perspective included?** Are pain and refusal respected, and are breaks and safe ways to stop the procedure provided?

If someone offers a "proprietary approach" but refuses to provide independent research, professional credentials or known complication risks, do not consent without independent advice.
