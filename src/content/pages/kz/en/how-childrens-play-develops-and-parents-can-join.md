A child stacks blocks, rolls a car, turns the pages of a book, taps a spoon against a saucepan or watches a wheel spin again and again. All of these can be forms of play.

Parents sometimes worry that a child is playing “the wrong way,” perhaps not making up imaginary stories, inviting other children, varying favourite objects or imitating an adult's suggested game. But **there is no single correct way to play**. Play needs to be understood in relation to a child's age, interests, abilities and overall development.

This article explains different forms of play, how to join without pressure and when to discuss observations with a professional. It is not an at-home autism test and does not replace developmental assessment.

## 1. Why play matters

Play can help a child explore how objects work, try movements, notice patterns, solve problems, communicate and simply enjoy themselves.

The American Academy of Pediatrics highlights play's importance for social, emotional, cognitive and language development. But **play has value beyond teaching**. Children also need enjoyment, rest, choice and time with things they find interesting.

Some play happens alone, some alongside or together with others. Every play moment does not need a “learning objective.”

## 2. Different forms of play

Forms of play often overlap in everyday life. These are useful descriptions, not mandatory stages that every child must follow in a strict order.

| Form of play | What it may look like at home | What an adult might notice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Movement play | Rolling a large ball, dancing or following a safe floor path. | Preferred movements, balance support and discomfort. |
| Sensory and exploratory play | Touching fabric, pouring water with adult supervision or listening to an object. | Pleasant and overwhelming sensations, preferences and refusal. |
| Object play | Opening a box, pressing a large button or placing a cup on a saucer. | How the child explores objects and combines actions. |
| Constructive play | Stacking large blocks, building a tower or road. | Attempts to solve problems and revisit interesting tasks. |
| Imaginative and pretend play | “Feeding” a teddy bear or imagining a train adventure. | Preferred stories and how ideas are expressed. |
| Parallel play | Two children each build their own tower beside one another. | Comfort with proximity and awareness of each other's actions. |
| Shared play | Taking turns rolling a ball, building a bridge or agreeing on rules. | Initiatives, suggestions and signs of wanting to continue or stop. |

**Important:** Preferring sorting, examining details or repeating an enjoyable action does not automatically make play “incorrect.” Consider enjoyment, safety and whether the child is open to interaction if they choose.

## 3. How play changes with age

Developmental milestones exist, but no single skill describes a child's whole development.

- **In infancy**, children may become interested in faces, voices, movements and safe objects, explore sensations and start repeating actions.
- **In toddlerhood**, actions with objects often diversify, as do simple toy combinations and ways to use familiar things.
- **In the preschool years**, pretend stories, playing near peers and shared rules become more common for many children. However, participation varies considerably.

For example, the CDC includes using several toys together among the milestones around two years and pretending to be another character among those around four years.

These examples are **not an exam**. Not using a particular pretend story does not explain why and cannot establish autism or developmental delay. Pretend play also does not rule out autism.

For more age-related information, see [“Development from 2 months to 5 years”](../orientiry-razvitiya-rebenka/).

## 4. How an adult can join in: a simple sequence

### Step 1. Observe the child's interests

What does the child voluntarily do? Which object do they choose? Do they enjoy movement, repetition, music, pictures, books or construction toys?

Instead of asking “What should we teach today?”, start with **“What interests the child and feels comfortable right now?”**

### Step 2. Offer participation but leave the choice

Sit nearby and use a similar object. If the child builds a tower, an adult might stack their own block nearby. If the child watches cars, the adult can roll another car.

There is no need to touch the child's toys, interrupt their pattern or demand shared play. Respect a preference for playing alone.

### Step 3. Repeat or comment on an action

An adult can say, “You built a tall bridge,” “The car is moving,” or “The tower fell.” They may gently imitate an amusing sound or movement if the child enjoys it.

Comments do not require a reply. Eye contact is not a condition for participation.

### Step 4. Suggest a small change

When the child welcomes shared play, suggest something small: add a bridge beside a familiar toy road or let a toy passenger “arrive home.”

If the idea is not appealing, return to the familiar activity. The aim is not to “expand play” at any cost.

### Step 5. Accept the child's message

A child may ask to continue, gesture “more,” move an object, smile, leave or say “enough.” Notice all of these messages.

Not wanting an adult to participate is not failure. Independent play matters too.

## 5. Eight play ideas without expensive materials

These are options rather than assignments with required outcomes. Match materials to the child's age, motor abilities, interests, hearing, vision and sensory needs.

| Game | Materials | How to take part | What not to demand |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Tower or road** | Large, safe blocks. | Build nearby, offer a new block or observe. | A “correct” tower or copying an adult. |
| **Roll the ball** | A large soft ball. | Offer to roll it and accept gestures or requests to continue. | Mandatory turn-taking or saying “my turn.” |
| **Toy train journey** | A large toy car or train. | Follow the chosen route, add a pretend “tunnel.” | A set story or sound effects. |
| **Find a suitable object** | Large toys and containers. | Explore what fits into a box. | Naming every colour or shape. |
| **Playing shop** | Pretend groceries or large safe objects. | Suggest buyer and seller roles if the child likes stories. | Pretend play if the child does not enjoy it. |
| **Choosing from a book** | A picture book. | Let the child pick a page and comment on details. | Finishing the book or answering every question. |
| **Musical pause** | An adult's voice or music at a comfortable volume. | Dance or change movement together if enjoyable. | Imitating movements or tolerating unpleasant noise. |
| **Your own path** | Cushions and safe floor space. | Suggest easy movements and allow changes. | Jumping, climbing or movements that are unsafe or uncomfortable. |

### Example 1. A child is fascinated by wheels

A child spins a toy car wheel and watches it for a long time. The adult can sit nearby and say, “The wheel is turning,” then allow the child to continue.

If the child shows interest in the adult, offer another car or watch wheels spin together. **Do not take away the toy or ban a safe repetitive action merely to change the game.**

### Example 2. A child enjoys sorting

A child sorts large pieces by colour. The adult might place a suitable box nearby or sort a separate set.

If the child does not want their arrangement changed, leave it as it is. Playing together does not require the adult to direct everything.

### Example 3. A child does not enjoy pretend play

The adult suggests “feeding” a stuffed rabbit, but the child prefers moving toy dishes. Remove the pretend-play demand and join the movement of objects.

The rabbit might appear later if the child becomes interested. If not, there is no reason to turn shared time into a struggle.

## 6. Must everyone play together?

No. **Independent, parallel and shared play** all have a place. A child may move between these forms even within one day.

Some children like playing nearby without touching other people's toys. Others enjoy movement, music or books together rather than role-play. Social participation varies with the setting, the relationship, communication access and personal preferences.

Adults can remain available without interfering constantly. If a child is content playing alone, there is no need to interrupt every time to “teach communication.”

For more on special play programmes, see [“Play-based interventions”](../play-based-interventions/).

## 7. If a child communicates without speech

Spoken words are not essential for shared play. Offers can be made through gestures, large pictures, accessible AAC buttons or showing an object.

For example, when playing with a ball, an adult offers “Roll it again” or “Finished.” The child can gesture, select a picture, pick up the ball or walk away.

Accept refusal. Do not withhold a favourite toy until the child speaks. Communication support is described in [“Supporting your child's everyday communication”](../kak-pomogat-rebenku-obshchatsya-v-povsednevnoi-zhizni/).

## 8. What if other children do not include the child?

Not every child feels ready for noisy group play, especially with unfamiliar children. A quieter setting can help: one familiar peer, clear materials, an opportunity to play alongside and a brief shared activity.

Adults can help children understand one another: “He wants to keep building the road,” “She is playing on her own for now.” Explain rules verbally, with pictures or through demonstration as needed.

Do not force immediate sharing of favourite toys, hugs or group play despite distress, or demand social gestures solely to appear typical.

When conflict, exclusion or substantial stress repeatedly limit participation, discuss available adaptations with educators and relevant professionals.

## 9. Safety matters more than developmental outcomes

Choose age-appropriate materials and follow manufacturers' advice.

- Small toys and parts that could be swallowed or inhaled are particularly dangerous for children under three.
- Check toy condition and keep batteries, particularly button batteries, magnets and sharp or breakable pieces secure.
- Water play requires continuous adult supervision. Even a small amount of water can be hazardous.
- Do not use objects the child attempts to swallow or materials likely to cause injury.
- Adapt movement games to the child's safety, condition and available support.
- Stop using a toy that causes fear, pain or sensory overload.

Parents do not need expensive “developmental kits.” Often a sturdy safe toy, book, large ball and suitable everyday objects are enough.

## 10. How to tell whether a game suits the child

In everyday play, the quality of participation is more meaningful than completing a checklist:

- Does the child show interest or enjoyment?
- Can they choose, continue and stop playing?
- Can they request help or refuse in a comfortable way?
- Can they play alone or with others if they wish?
- Is the play free from marked distress, pain or overload?
- What did the adult learn about the child's interests?

A short record might look like this:

> **Situation:** In the evening, the child built a garage from large blocks.  
> **What happened:** Their mother built a second garage nearby. The child offered her a toy car and then took it back.  
> **Conditions:** A quiet room and familiar toys, without requests to repeat words.  
> **What was noticed:** The child offered an object and chose when the interaction ended.

This is an example of observation, not a score of the child's “success” or proof that a strategy worked.

See [“Developmental observation diary”](../kak-nablyudat-za-razvitiem-rebenka-doma/) for a structured format.

## 11. When to discuss play and development with a professional

Play can provide useful information about development, but it is not an autism test.

Talk with a paediatrician or another appropriate professional if:

- Questions about development, play, communication or movement persist.
- Pain, substantial discomfort or other limitations make it hard to explore objects or participate in everyday activities.
- Changes in communication, movement or use of objects concern the family.
- The child **loses previously established** play, speech, communication or movement skills.

Discuss loss of established skills with a medical professional without prolonged delay. If the change is sudden and accompanied by acute medical symptoms, seek urgent medical attention.

Families do not have to establish a diagnosis themselves before seeking advice. In Kazakhstan, see [“Where to seek help about a child's development”](../../help-kazakhstan/development-concerns-kazakhstan/).

## 12. How everyday play differs from professional play-based intervention

**Everyday play** is for enjoyment, exploration, rest and relationships. It can be freely chosen and does not require families to count “correct” actions.

**Play-based intervention** is a specific programme with defined goals, trained professionals, consideration of individual needs and monitoring of outcomes. NICE discusses some play strategies within specialist social-communication interventions delivered by trained staff.

Not all play at home is therapy, and not every service called “play therapy” has demonstrated efficacy for autism. Claims depend on the particular programme, the children studied and measured outcomes.

For evidence and limitations, see [“Development, interaction and play”](../razvitie-vzaimodeystvie-i-igra/) and [“Play-based interventions”](../play-based-interventions/).

## Quick checklist: something to try today

1. Find an activity the child chooses.
2. Calmly offer to join without taking control.
3. Comment on something interesting or demonstrate your own action.
4. Let the child choose whether to continue interacting.
5. Accept any clear response, including refusal or a wish to play alone.

**The main goal is not to make the child follow adult rules, but to make play safe, accessible and enjoyable for the child and family.**
