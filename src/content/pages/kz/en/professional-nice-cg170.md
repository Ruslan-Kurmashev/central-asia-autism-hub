## 1. How CG170 differs from the diagnostic guideline

CG170 starts by asking **what support the child and family need**, rather than how to establish a diagnosis for the first time. NICE describes the diagnostic pathway for this age group separately in CG128. Substituting one document for the other confuses assessment with support.

CG170 gives particular attention to specialist team arrangements, professional training, information for families, co-occurring conditions and transitions between services. Choosing a well-known method is not enough when planning support. The aim, acceptability to the child, responsible practitioner and way of assessing outcomes must be established.

## 2. Individual planning and teamwork

When organising support, establish:

- what causes difficulties in everyday life: communication, environmental overload, health, participation in activities, sleep or other circumstances
- the conditions in which the situation improves or worsens
- which communication methods are accessible to the child and how their preferences are considered
- which practitioners are needed and the limits of their professional roles
- which goals are useful to the person and family, and how changes in quality of life can be measured.

NICE recommends ensuring that practitioners understand autism and co-occurring conditions, coordinating access to a specialist team and considering transitions between educational and health care stages ([section 1.1](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations)).

**Practical distinction:** a support plan is better framed as reducing discomfort from noise and enabling participation in an activity, rather than as correcting autism without a measurable outcome.

## 3. Adapting environments and care processes

Recommendation [1.1.9](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations) draws attention to accessible visual supports, individual needs for personal space and sensory aspects of lighting, noise and other environmental features. Recommendation [1.1.10](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations) describes the need to adapt service arrangements themselves where possible.

Example of professional discussion:

| Observed difficulty | Possible focus of assessment and adaptation |
| --- | --- |
| Waiting in a noisy corridor causes severe distress | Predictable appointment time, a less overwhelming waiting area |
| The sequence of actions is unclear to the child | An individually understandable schedule, explanation of the procedure in advance |
| Spoken instructions are inaccessible | An alternative format for information, a visual support or AAC tool |
| Wellbeing noticeably worsens after an activity | Duration, demands, breaks, possible physical causes |

These are examples of **possible environmental adjustments**, not universal treatment prescriptions. Discuss adjustments with the child in an accessible way and review them in light of outcomes.

## 4. Communication and social interaction

In [recommendation 1.3.1](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations), NICE considers specific social communication programmes involving trained practitioners, parents, teachers or peers, according to age and goals. These include play-based strategies, joint attention, engagement and reciprocal communication. Programmes should match developmental level and be delivered by trained practitioners. This does not establish the effectiveness of every commercial programme.

When assessing proposed support, clarify:

1. Which communication goal has been selected and whether it matters to the child.
2. Whether communication methods are adapted to the child's understanding and abilities.
3. Whether adults know how to recognise requests, refusal and signs of discomfort.
4. How benefits are assessed in natural settings.
5. How the programme is changed if progress is absent or adverse effects occur.

**A separate consideration:** having speech does not mean communication support is unnecessary. AAC should not be interpreted as abandoning the development of communication methods accessible to the person.

## 5. When behaviour causes difficulties

An important part of CG170 is **identifying circumstances that may trigger or maintain behaviour**. [Section 1.4](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations) considers physical discomfort, pain, gastrointestinal problems, anxiety and other mental health conditions, communication barriers, environmental overload, changes in routine and social circumstances.

Professional steps include:

- describe **what happened**, in which circumstances and how often
- establish whether medical or other specialist assessment is needed first
- clarify whether the person can ask for help, a break or an end to the situation
- assess possible changes to the environment and communication
- if difficulties persist, consult a competent multidisciplinary team
- select justified support with a defined outcome measure and review process.

Under [1.4.7-1.4.9](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations), a psychosocial intervention informed by functional assessment is considered first-line when no co-occurring medical, mental health or environmental cause triggering or maintaining the difficulties has been identified. Assessment considers context, the person's needs and the consequences of behaviour. A meaningful outcome, review timeframe and methods of assessment before and after support are specified in advance. Functional assessment does not replace medical diagnosis.

### Example

A child starts shouting before a group activity. Possible causes include pain, difficulty understanding the task, high noise levels, anxiety or an inability to ask for a break. Automatically assigning the same behaviour modification programme to every child may miss an important cause.

Instead, the team gathers observations, considers the child's and family's views, rules out medical causes requiring attention and tests appropriate adjustments. Outcomes are assessed not only by the frequency of shouting, but also by **comfort, access to communication and participation**.

## 6. Medication and methods that must not be presented as treatments for autism

CG170 separates support for **core autistic characteristics** from treatment of **co-occurring medical or mental health conditions**. In [section 1.3.2](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations), NICE does not recommend antipsychotics, antidepressants, anticonvulsants or exclusion diets for changing core autistic characteristics. This restriction does not prevent indicated treatment of separate conditions.

Specific situations involving severe dangerous behaviour and co-occurring conditions are covered elsewhere in the document. Medication, indications, adverse effects and monitoring are the responsibility of **a physician**, not a teacher or non-specialist centre. An isolated CG170 recommendation must not be taken out of clinical context and presented as a general authorisation to prescribe a drug.

In [point 1.6.4](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations), NICE does not recommend secretin, chelation or hyperbaric oxygen therapy for supporting autistic children and young people. Points 1.6.1-1.6.3 separately address neurofeedback and auditory integration training for speech and language difficulties, and omega-3 for sleep problems. These restrictions concern the stated purposes, not treatment of other conditions.

## 7. Co-occurring conditions and family support

CG170 stresses that sleep problems, anxiety, other mental health conditions and physical health should be addressed **separately and within the relevant professional competencies**. For instance, changes in sleep may relate to pain, the environment, routine or a medical cause. Describing them simply as an autistic characteristic is insufficient.

Families may also need information, training and practical support. Decisions should take account of family capacity rather than transferring all responsibilities to parents. For young people, preparation for transition to adult services and continuity of support are important.

For snoring with episodes of breathing difficulty or pauses in breathing during sleep, NICE recommends specialist assessment for obstructive sleep apnoea (1.7.5). Eating difficulties, including a highly restricted diet, require assessment of nutrition and possible deficiencies, with specialist referral when indicated. These issues are not simply matters of behaviour modification.

## 8. Using CG170 in professional practice

| Check | Question for the practitioner |
| --- | --- |
| Goal | How will the child's participation, autonomy, communication or wellbeing improve? |
| Basis | Which section of the guideline supports the proposed action? |
| Context | Have health, environment, family, preferences and co-occurring conditions been considered? |
| Responsibility | Who is qualified to assess and carry out the action? |
| Safety | How will deterioration or adverse effects be recognised? |
| Outcome | What is measured before and after, and who reviews the plan? |
| Kazakhstan | Have local clinical and educational rules been checked? |

## Limitations of use in Kazakhstan

NICE CG170 **does not establish a list of mandatory services in Kazakhstan** and does not replace national regulatory requirements. Professional roles, legal grounds for interventions, consent rules, prescribing and care pathways must be checked separately against legislation and current clinical protocols.

When citing the document, record the version date and exact recommendation number, because selective excerpts without context may distort the meaning.

## Main conclusion

CG170 is useful as a systematic guideline for **individualised, coordinated and safe support for children and young people**, encompassing communication, environment, physical and mental health, family and measurable outcomes. It is not a ready-made universal programme or a substitute for professional assessment of an individual child.
