## Why can the same sound bother one person but not another?

During dinner, conversation, a television and clattering dishes may all be audible. One person hears familiar background noise. Another may find the combination so overwhelming that listening, eating or remaining in the room becomes difficult.

Similar experiences occur with light, odours, clothing, touch, movement and sensations from the body itself. This is not necessarily about unwillingness to cooperate or the way someone has been raised.

Sensory perception is how the nervous system receives, processes and interprets information from the senses and body. It involves the physical signal, attention, a person's condition, previous experiences and context.

Sensory differences are common in autism, but there is no single sensory profile shared by all autistic people. One person may struggle with particular sounds, while another actively seeks certain movements or textures. Responses to the same stimulus may change across days.

This article moves from general neuroscience to the everyday experience of sensation, and distinguishes established findings from possible mechanisms still being studied.

## How does the brain turn light, sound and pressure into experiences?

Perception begins with specialised structures that respond to physical or chemical stimuli.

In hearing, receptor cells of the inner ear convert mechanical vibrations into nervous-system signals. Retinal cells respond to light. Receptors in the skin and other tissues respond to different forms of pressure, temperature and potentially damaging stimuli.

Signals travel along neural pathways and are processed in interacting areas of the brain. This is not a simple copy of the external world. The nervous system selects features, combines information and takes the context into account.

Finally a person experiences a sound as pleasant, ordinary, irritating or even painfully unpleasant. The response also depends on whether they can express discomfort or leave the situation.

### Why does the intensity of an experience differ from sound volume?

Two people may be exposed to the same objective noise level and experience it differently. Neither person's discomfort is imaginary.

A response can depend on suddenness, sound frequencies, whether the person controls the source, competing stimuli, fatigue and what is happening.

Scientists distinguish sensory detection, which is noticing a signal, from discrimination, which is telling signals apart, emotional appraisal, which is how unpleasant something feels, and behavioural response, which is what someone does.

These processes are related but not identical. A person might detect sound very accurately yet find it distressing. Another might notice a noise late but respond quickly once they pay attention.

## Hyper-reactivity, hypo-reactivity and sensory seeking

Three concepts help describe sensory responses.

**Hyper-reactivity** is a heightened or disproportionately intense response to a particular sensory input, such as distress from vacuum cleaner noise, bright light or skin contact.

**Hypo-reactivity** is a weaker or slower response to a particular stimulus, such as not immediately noticing a quiet voice or light touch.

**Sensory seeking** describes actively seeking certain experiences, such as rocking, touching preferred textures or listening to a repeated sound.

### Why can these responses occur together?

Parents may ask why a child dislikes loud music but happily bangs objects to make a loud sound. This is not a contradiction.

Responses depend on predictability, meaning, control and the quality of a sensation, not just how strong it is. A self-produced rhythmic sound can be enjoyable and expected, while an unexpected sound from someone else is frightening.

Similarly, a child may avoid an unexpected light touch but enjoy some predictable pressure or close-fitting clothing.

A fixed label such as "sensitive to everything" is less useful than identifying which stimuli matter, in which situations, and with what consequences.

## Are sensory differences diagnostic signs of autism?

The DSM-5 diagnostic description includes possible hyper-reactivity or hypo-reactivity to sensory input and unusual interest in sensory aspects of the environment ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/hcp/diagnosis/index.html)).

That does not mean that disliking noise is sufficient to establish autism.

Sensory differences are only one possible component of a broader diagnostic picture. Assessment considers developmental history, communication, social interaction, repetitive features and other information. One questionnaire or observation cannot replace a comprehensive assessment.

Sensory problems also occur in non-autistic people and may relate to hearing or vision, migraine, anxiety or other conditions. Not all autistic people have obvious sound or light intolerance, and some experiences are hard for observers to notice.

## Why are particular sounds especially unpleasant?

For one person, abrupt sounds such as sirens, a door slamming or a blender are most difficult. For another, constant low-level noise in a classroom or from ventilation is worse. Someone may tolerate loud music comfortably when they select it themselves.

These distinctions are useful:

- **Heightened sensitivity to loudness** means some sounds feel excessively loud or painful.
- **Misophonia** describes a strong unpleasant response to particular sound types, not necessarily due to volume.
- **Phonophobia** means fear associated with sound.
- **Difficulty processing speech in noise** means struggling to separate relevant speech from competing sounds.

These categories are not interchangeable and cannot be diagnosed from a single behaviour.

Auditory research reviews ([*Journal of Neural Transmission*, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36914900/) and [*Autism Research*, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39552096/)) describe a complex interaction between the ear, central auditory processing, attention, emotion and sound tolerance. No single mechanism explains every type of sound discomfort.

### What is habituation to a repeated stimulus?

**Habituation** is the decrease in a particular response after repeated presentation of a stimulus under certain conditions.

A familiar background sound may gradually stop attracting attention. Some researchers have proposed that altered habituation contributes to particular sensory differences in autism.

However, findings are not uniform. A [2023 study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37688470/) observed differences in some electrophysiological responses to repeating sounds, but habituation measurements did not correlate with parent-reported sound intolerance in everyday life.

A laboratory difference in brain activity does not necessarily explain what a person experiences in an ordinary setting.

## Why can bright light and busy visual surroundings be tiring?

Vision processes brightness, contrast, movement, form and other properties.

Some autistic people describe discomfort from bright or flickering lights, visual clutter, detailed patterns or movement across the visual field.

This does not mean that everyone has uniformly "stronger" or "weaker" vision. Visual acuity, contrast detection, movement perception, attention to detail and subjective comfort are different properties.

Experiments may find differences in particular visual tasks, but results depend greatly on the design of the study. Reviews by [Robertson and Baron-Cohen (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn.2017.112) and [researchers in 2022](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-vision-093020-035217) explain possible models and their limitations.

In everyday settings, finding comfortable lighting may be more helpful than assuming a biological cause. Choices can include seating away from glare, softer directed light or reduced visual clutter.

## Why can touch and clothing textures be uncomfortable?

Skin contains receptors that respond to pressure, movement along its surface, temperature and other stimuli. Signals travel through peripheral nerves and are processed in central neural systems.

Touch is more than physical pressure. Control and predictability of contact matter.

A sudden light touch can be distressing, while an agreed touch with similar pressure feels very different. An uncomfortable fabric may interfere with concentration.

Studies examine tactile thresholds, movement across the skin, pressure and other characteristics. A [2018 review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28089657/) stresses that behavioural reports and experimental measurements often answer different questions.

Pain and touch are not the same sensation. Responding weakly to certain stimuli does not prove that someone feels no pain and does not make potentially harmful actions safe.

Do not perform informal "pain threshold tests" or deliberately intensify unwanted touch to force someone to get used to it.

## Beyond the five senses: movement, body position and internal signals

People commonly name vision, hearing, touch, smell and taste. The nervous system also monitors movement, body position and signals from internal organs.

### Vestibular system

The **vestibular system** processes information about changes in head position and movement and contributes to balance and orientation.

Some people enjoy rocking or spinning, while certain movements may distress others. One behaviour does not reveal vestibular receptor function or establish a diagnosis.

### Proprioception

**Proprioception** helps us sense the position and movement of body parts without watching them continuously. Receptors in muscles, tendons and joints contribute.

Some sensorimotor difficulties appear as differences in coordination or learning movements, but that does not necessarily mean one specific "proprioceptive deficit".

### Interoception

**Interoception** refers to processing signals about internal bodily conditions, such as aspects of heartbeats, breathing, hunger and discomfort.

The claim that every autistic person has poor body awareness is not supported by research.

A [2025 systematic review and meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40909407/) found mixed results across interoception tasks and age groups. Some methods showed differences and others did not. An autism diagnosis alone cannot establish an individual's interoceptive characteristics.

## What is sensory overload?

**Sensory overload** describes a situation in which the number, intensity or combination of sensations is more than a person can comfortably handle under current conditions. It is a descriptive term, not an independently defined laboratory disease.

A person may cover their ears, seek quiet, cry, become irritable, move more, speak less or need a break.

Visible responses do not reveal everything someone feels. The same behaviour can also relate to pain, fatigue, anxiety or difficulties communicating a need.

A [2024 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39515075/) of **63 studies including 11,659 people** found statistical relationships between more pronounced sensory differences and emotional or behavioural difficulties. Those relationships do not prove that sensory differences always cause such difficulties.

Overload is often better understood as a sign that circumstances may need adjustment, rather than as grounds for punishment.

## Why does sensory tolerance change with circumstances?

Tolerance can differ between morning and evening. Sleep, temperature, stress, hunger, health, workload and expectations all matter.

A school corridor may be manageable when it is only noisy but much harder when noise combines with bright lighting, crowds and unexpected touch.

Choice can make a difference. A person may cope better when they know what will happen, how long it will last and whether they can stop or leave.

It is helpful to note the stimulus, time of day, previous events, communication opportunities and what made the situation more comfortable.

## What does research show about the biology of sensory differences in autism?

Several broad mechanisms are being investigated.

**Receptors and early processing.** Some differences may involve detection and early signal transformation, but not every sensory difficulty originates in the sense organs.

**Adaptation and habituation.** The brain may change its response to repeated stimuli. Findings depend on age, stimulus and measurement method.

**Excitation and inhibition.** Both contribute to neural networks. A single universal GABA or glutamate abnormality has not been demonstrated in autism.

**Attention and prediction.** Expectations and the ability to direct attention shape perception. Predictive-processing theories remain research proposals, not a confirmed explanation for every autistic person.

**Emotional and autonomic responses.** Physiological arousal, anxiety and experience may intensify unpleasant sensations. This does not make the experience "just psychological" or something a person can simply stop through willpower.

The [2017 review in *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn.2017.112) and later work emphasise that sensory characteristics are multidimensional. One universal mechanism is unlikely to explain every experience.

## Why can family reports and laboratory tests disagree?

Researchers use different tools, each measuring something different.

| Method | What it helps explain | What to remember |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interview with the person and family | Which sensations help or hinder daily life | Reporting depends on communication, memory and context |
| Sensory questionnaire | How frequently specific responses are seen | It does not directly measure neurotransmitters or sensory thresholds |
| Psychophysical task | Detection or discrimination of a stimulus | A narrow laboratory task may not capture everyday overload |
| EEG or MEG | Certain neuronal population responses to sensory events | There is no universal sensory-autism EEG signature |
| Functional MRI | Indirect patterns of activity across brain regions | A group difference does not establish an individual diagnosis |
| Hearing and vision assessment | Potential disorders of sense organs | Autism and hearing or vision impairments can coexist |

Someone may find ordinary noise unbearable yet have an ordinary sound detection threshold in a laboratory. This does not discredit the person's account. Detecting a sound and tolerating it emotionally are different properties.

A high score on a sensory questionnaire likewise does not prove any particular receptor or hormone has changed.

[Schaaf and colleagues (2023)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36167886/) also discuss how sensory functions are related but not identical, so clinical ratings and individual tests can diverge.

## What helps people feel comfortable and participate?

Practical support begins with respecting individual experiences. If a person describes a sound as unpleasant, that should not be dismissed merely because others find it ordinary.

[NICE CG170](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations) recommends considering lighting, noise, colours, personal space and the organisation of medical care.

Possible adjustments include:

- Warn someone before loud or unexpected sounds when possible.
- Offer a quiet place, a break or the option to leave.
- Change lighting and reduce visual clutter.
- Ask permission before touching and allow choices of clothing and texture.
- Support communication about discomfort through words, gestures, pictures or augmentative and alternative communication.
- Allow safe ways of self-regulation, such as comfortable repetitive movements.

No one aid suits everyone. Noise-cancelling headphones or hearing protection may help in particular settings, but should be selected individually with safety in mind, including the need to hear important warning sounds and any relevant clinical advice.

Such tools adapt the environment. They are not "synapse treatment". The goal is to reduce unnecessary distress and improve access, not to force someone to appear typical.

The [National Autistic Society](https://www.autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/about-autism/sensory-processing) provides further information.

## Which approaches require critical evaluation?

Some programmes promise to "normalise the sensory system", "restore brain balance" or "eliminate autism using sound". These claims go beyond the evidence.

Sensory interventions differ, and their studies measure different outcomes.

A [2026 systematic review by Piller and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41324442/) included **12 studies**, among them four randomised trials. Its authors rated evidence for individual functional goals and participation as strong and evidence for everyday living and some other skills as moderate. That assessment concerns a particular therapeutic approach and specific outcomes, not curing autism.

Another [2026 systematic review by Kishida and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10803-026-07283-7) examined **10 studies** meeting Ayres method fidelity criteria and reached a more cautious conclusion. Only four studies had low risk of bias, their findings varied, and some apparently positive studies had shortcomings in measurement of personal goals. The authors regarded current effectiveness evidence as limited.

The reviews applied different inclusion and quality criteria. It would therefore be inaccurate to generalise a claim of "strong evidence" to all sensory therapy or every child.

A "sensory diet", special sound frequencies, a stimulation device or another approach should not be presumed effective just because it involves hearing, movement or touch.

Assess the specific protocol, its safety, research quality and personally meaningful goals. Promises of a cure or compulsory "brain correction" are serious warning signs.

## When should a doctor be consulted?

Not every sensitivity requires medical examination. But a new, severe or painful symptom should not be attributed to autism automatically.

Ear pain, sudden hearing loss, severe light intolerance with headache, changes in vision, dizziness, unusual episodes of unresponsiveness or other new neurological symptoms may justify assessment.

Sudden changes in hearing or other severe acute symptoms may require urgent medical attention.

Sleep problems, migraine, anxiety, pain and dental issues may also cause discomfort. Autism does not remove the need for ordinary medical assessment.

## What is reasonably established, and what remains uncertain?

| Reasonably well supported | Not demonstrated as a universal rule |
| --- | --- |
| Autistic people can have diverse sensory characteristics. | Every autistic person has one specific sensory profile. |
| Hyper-reactivity, hypo-reactivity and sensory seeking can coexist. | Behaviour measures GABA levels or synapse numbers precisely. |
| Perception depends on receptors, brain networks and context. | One molecular mechanism causes all autism sensory differences. |
| Some studies show group differences in hearing, vision or touch processing. | Every such difference causes a particular person's experiences. |
| Adapting environments and respecting needs can improve accessibility. | Everyone needs exactly the same sensory procedures. |
| Certain therapies have been studied for specific functional goals. | One universal intervention can "repair" the sensory system or cure autism. |

## Questions about sensory experiences

### Why does a child cover their ears at an ordinary sound but enjoy banging objects loudly?

Suddenness, control, predictability and the type of sound can influence tolerance. One observation cannot establish a sensory diagnosis or a neurotransmitter mechanism.

### Can a child seek movement and avoid touch at the same time?

Yes. Different sensory systems, specific stimuli and circumstances can evoke different responses in the same person.

### Does failing to answer to their name mean a child is hypo-reactive to sound?

Not necessarily. Attention, communication, hearing and context may all be involved. A hearing concern calls for appropriate medical assessment rather than assumptions about autism biology.

### Should a child be made to endure unpleasant sounds or textures to become accustomed to them?

There is no basis for treating coercion as safe or universally effective. First understand the situation, adapt the environment and find agreed forms of support. Any specialist intervention requires an understandable goal and safety assessment.

### Can blood tests or EEG explain sensory sensitivity in autism?

No single established clinical test explains all the sensory characteristics of an autistic person. Research measurements of brain activity or biochemical markers do not replace developmental history, assessment of needs and clinical evaluation.

### Does high sensitivity mean the child's brain is damaged?

No. Sensitivity does not by itself establish brain damage. It may reflect the interaction of nervous system processing, context and experience. New or concerning symptoms still merit medical assessment.

## What helps us understand a person's sensory profile?

Sensory differences form part of the diversity of neurodevelopment in autism, not one identical symptom pattern in every person.

Responses to sound, light, touch and movement depend on sense organs, neural pathways, brain networks, experience and current wellbeing.

Research identifies various group-level patterns but has not established one universal explanation suitable for diagnosis or selecting a treatment for everyone.

In daily life, listen to the person, respect their experiences, reduce unnecessary sensory demands and offer choice. The aim is not to force discomfort away but to make the surroundings more accessible.
