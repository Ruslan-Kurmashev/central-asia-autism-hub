## From individual publications to sustainable research

In the AutismHub series on science in Kazakhstan and Central Asia, we have reviewed registration statistics, screening results, parents' experiences, educational research and technology development. All matter, but several steps separate a published study from improvements in practice.

Imagine that researchers found many parents in one group reporting delays on the way to developmental assessment. The next question need not be how often the same problem can be described. It may be more useful to ask **which step in the assessment pathway causes delays**, how to measure that consistently at different facilities and whether a change in services can be evaluated.

The future of research does not necessarily require expensive equipment or enormous databases. A carefully validated tool in a local language, a thoughtful interview or a comparable way to measure service access can be highly valuable.

This final article proposes **one possible sequence of future research work**. It draws on published studies and international research-reporting principles, but **is not an agreed plan of ministries, universities or foundations**. Partners, dates, budgets and funding for these projects have not been approved.

## What scientific foundation could support collaboration?

Regional research need not start from nothing. Kazakhstan has published studies of ASD medical registrations covering 2016-2022 and 2014-2024, work on screening questionnaires in local languages, family experiences and education.

Researchers in Uzbekistan and international colleagues have documented seven mothers' experiences seeking diagnoses. Tajikistan published a study of ASD registrations among children for 2020-2025. UNICEF has examined systemic requirements for inclusive education and assistive technology in Kazakhstan.

However, evidence remains heterogeneous. Some papers analyse health records, others interview parents or ask educators to rate their own skills. **These findings cannot be added together as one measure of prevalence or effectiveness.**

The historical review by **Rano Zakirova-Engstrand and Gulnoza Yakubova**, online in 2023 and in *Autism* in 2024, included **eleven empirical studies from 2013-2021**, ten from Kazakhstan and one from Uzbekistan. Its search ended in January 2022, so these eleven are **not the total number of Central Asian papers in 2026**.

[Original Central Asian scoping review](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613231170553).

The main opportunity is not only more studies, but making findings from **different years, institutions and countries genuinely interpretable alongside each other**.

## An illustrative research roadmap for 2027-2030

![AutismHub's illustrative timeline for identifying questions, piloting methods and replicating suitable studies across institutions and countries.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/research/central-asia-autism-future-research-roadmap-en.svg)

*Figure 1. An AutismHub editorial illustration. The years and stages are hypothetical, not current commitments by states or research organisations.*

| Possible stage | Main task | Desired output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **2027: preparation** | Define questions with autistic people, families and specialists, agree terminology, ethics and methods | Testable research questions, protocols, privacy safeguards and language accessibility requirements |
| **2028: pilot studies** | Test approaches with limited samples, questionnaire comprehension and feasibility of data collection | Reports on feasibility, measurement properties and limitations, including negative findings |
| **2029-2030: replication and comparison** | Where partners and resources permit, repeat suitable methods in several settings and countries | Comparable results with acknowledged uncertainty and accessible community reports |

**Not every study must last four years.** Activities may overlap. A qualitative study might be small and quick, while a large population-based investigation may take much longer.

The roadmap is useful chiefly to distinguish **an idea**, **an initial feasibility test** and **evidence that a result can be replicated**.

## First step: choose questions with the people concerned

A research group may prioritise biomarkers or automated analysis of medical records. Autistic adults and parents may instead focus on access to assessment, education or transitions between school and work.

Neither set of questions is inherently less scientific. Different questions demand different methods.

### What an international example of community involvement showed

In a 2024 study by **Cage and colleagues**, **225 autistic adults living in Scotland** rated research priorities. High-priority topics included mental wellbeing, diagnosis, service access, public attitudes and issues affecting autistic women.

The team involved autistic and non-autistic researchers, and community representatives contributed to the survey's organisation. It illustrates how research priorities can be studied among people directly affected.

**However, 225 Scottish participants cannot establish the priorities of people living in Kazakhstan or neighbouring countries.** Local dialogue with diverse participants would be necessary.

[Research priorities among autistic adults, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11401337/).

### How an initial community discussion could work

Before designing a study, participants could be asked which questions they value, what outcomes represent meaningful improvement and how they would prefer to contribute.

Inclusion must not be limited to people able to complete long Russian- or English-language online forms. People using different communication methods and needing different levels of support should have accessible ways to participate.

Clear consent, confidentiality, withdrawal and fair compensation where appropriate should also be considered.

**This is a suggested model for future work, not an announcement of an existing regional research council.**

## Which projects might be suitable first collaborations?

These are examples of concrete research questions, possible methods and outcomes. **None is being represented as an active or funded programme.**

| Potential project | Question to investigate | Suitable initial method | What must not be promised |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Pathway to assessment** | Where do delays occur between first concern, referral and comprehensive assessment? | Prospective tracking of pathway steps across facilities, with informed consent | That all delays are caused by one professional or organisation |
| **Instruments in local languages** | Do parents from different language groups understand questions consistently? | Cognitive interviews, usability assessment and later psychometric testing | That translation alone gives clinical diagnostic accuracy |
| **Classroom communication** | Does a defined accessibility adjustment support participation in lessons? | Pilot with predefined outcomes and examination of actual everyday use | That one adjustment will benefit every autistic student |
| **Parent support** | How do service access and burden change after a specific organisational reform? | Before-and-after process evaluation with suitable comparisons where possible | That all observed improvement is due to this one reform |
| **Transition to adult life** | What barriers and opportunities do autistic young adults report after school? | Accessible interviews and longitudinal work shaped by young people | That a small group represents all adults |
| **Registration quality** | Which groups of children are missed by existing sources? | Audit of definitions and coverage with lawful and ethical data handling | That a register automatically yields true ASD prevalence |

A project's priority should depend **not on how impressive its title sounds**, but on relevance to people, ability to provide reliable answers, risks, resources and usefulness of results.

## Project example: examine the early developmental assessment pathway

Consider the first example in more detail.

Suppose two or three service providers decide to measure time from a family's first contact through referral to completion of professional developmental assessment. Researchers would define the start and end of each step, relevant age groups and how missing information is handled before collecting data.

The resulting report could specify:

1. How many people participated and which centres recruited them.
2. Observed intervals between steps, with distributions rather than just an average.
3. Which groups experienced longer journeys and how uncertain those estimates are.
4. Sampling limitations that may affect generalisation.
5. Organisational questions identified for further study.

The project **does not need to establish an autism diagnosis for any particular child**. Its scientific question is about process accessibility, not clinical diagnosis.

To claim that a later service change shortened waiting times, an appropriate comparison accounting for other changes would be needed. Simply comparing two calendar periods could mislead if workloads, reporting rules and staffing changed at the same time.

## Second example: study Kazakh and Russian versions of an instrument

A Central Kazakhstan study published in 2022 examined Russian and Kazakh versions of **M-CHAT-R/F** among **171 children**, reporting high internal consistency.

Internal consistency describes relationships between questionnaire items. It **is not equivalent to sensitivity and specificity against independently confirmed clinical diagnoses**.

Follow-up research could include:

- examining how parents with different language backgrounds understand specific wording
- testing cultural differences in interpretation
- assessing acceptability and usability
- estimating accuracy independently using a suitable design
- investigating how families are referred after an initial screening result.

Age restrictions and instrument copyright mean that a questionnaire cannot simply be altered or applied to every age group.

[Original Kazakhstan M-CHAT-R/F research](https://doi.org/10.23950/jcmk/11574).

This shows how a relatively small but methodologically careful study may be important for the quality of later projects.

## Third example: assess daily-life changes rather than device deliveries

In Kazakhstan, UNICEF has examined the assistive-technology system for inclusive education. Reports discussed equipment gaps, staff preparation, service coordination and linguistic accessibility.

Such reports describe **conditions needed for implementation**, not clinical trials of a device for autistic children.

If a school introduced augmentative and alternative communication (AAC), evaluation could ask whether a pupil can express requests or refusals more freely, use a tool beyond a dedicated session, be understood by others and find it personally acceptable.

The meaningful outcome might be **verified improvement in communication access**, not the number of tablets bought.

Researchers should also respect the child's preferences and choices. Rejecting a particular device should not automatically be labelled failure by the participant.

[UNICEF Kazakhstan: assistive-technology ecosystem](https://www.unicef.org/kazakhstan/en/reports/assistive-technologies-childs-needs-assessment-and-ecosystem-kazakhstan).

## Comparing countries: where to begin

Across Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan, Tajikistan and Turkmenistan, the hardest task may be agreeing on **the indicator being measured**, not calculating the largest possible sum.

One country may count children on continuing medical monitoring, another first recorded diagnoses during a year, and a third cumulative registrations over multiple years. Even factually correct figures cannot automatically rank countries by true prevalence.

Age bands, diagnostic definitions, reference dates and population denominators should be harmonised, with attention to completeness of registration and people missing from existing data.

**A single international database of identifiable patients is not a prerequisite.** Projects may cooperate through shared protocols, joint methods review and permitted aggregated, de-identified results.

Child health and development data require appropriate legal grounds, ethical review and safeguards against reidentification. Removing a surname does not, by itself, ensure safety.

[Kazakhstan registry trends, 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1520460), [Tajikistan registrations, 2026](https://www.vestnik-ipovszrt.tj/en/detail.php?id=106).

## How can studies be rigorous without exaggerated promises?

Scientific publications are useful when their design and limitations can be examined. Neither a large sample nor a prestigious institutional name substitutes for transparent participant selection and measurement.

### Protocols before collecting data

Research teams should define questions, eligible participants, outcomes and analysis approaches in advance. Where appropriate, protocols and analysis plans can be preregistered.

This helps readers distinguish planned tests from hypotheses developed only after results were seen.

### Appropriate reporting standards

**STROBE** addresses reporting of observational studies, including participant selection, measures, biases, design and outcomes.

**CONSORT**, updated in 2025, provides reporting guidance for randomised trials.

These standards **are not certificates of scientific excellence**. They encourage transparency about what was done, but good reporting cannot rescue a fundamentally unsuitable study design.

[STROBE statement](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.0040296), [CONSORT 2025 statement](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2024-081123).

### Report uncertainty and negative findings

If a study finds that a proposed method did not provide the expected benefit, that should be published. Otherwise, readers may see only successful trials and overestimate effectiveness.

Reports should present effect size, precision, participant attrition, potential biases and applicability alongside statistical significance.

### Check applicability to Central Asia

Even a rigorous foreign trial cannot guarantee the same results in settings with different languages, resources, education and service structures.

Adaptation may require more than translating a few labels. Separate work may be needed on measurement properties, acceptability and accessibility for local participants.

## How might scientific progress be assessed in a few years?

Publication and conference counts help with reporting, but alone do not show that research has become higher quality or more relevant to people.

Possible indicators for a future research programme could include:

| Possible indicator | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Share of projects with accessible protocols and predefined outcomes | Helps assess transparency of planning |
| Number of instruments with published local psychometric evaluations | Measures method quality rather than just translations |
| Share of studies involving autistic people in choosing questions | Reflects meaningful community participation |
| Number of projects replicated in independent settings | Allows examination of reproducibility |
| Reporting of null findings and limitations | Reduces selective presentation |
| Accessibility of findings in Russian, Kazakh and other regional languages | Helps families and specialists interpret evidence |
| Availability of outcomes on everyday participation and quality of life | Links science to people's concerns rather than only organisational metrics |

These are **proposed criteria for discussion, not officially adopted indicators of Central Asian governments**. They do not replace individual study appraisal.

## What roles could universities, services, families and platforms play?

Different partners can contribute in different ways, and their roles must not be confused.

**Universities and research teams** can develop protocols, provide methodological supervision, conduct analyses and publish results.

**Healthcare, education and social organisations** may help formulate questions, map service pathways and participate under the necessary permissions.

**Autistic people and families** can define meaningful outcomes, review the clarity of materials and contribute to research decisions, beyond acting as data sources.

**Independent ethics bodies and data-protection specialists** are important where research involves children or health information.

**Information platforms such as AutismHub** can explain studies, link to original papers and help readers distinguish evidence from unverified promises. Publishing research summaries **does not confer on the platform the status of a research institute or an official international project coordinator**.

## International frameworks can help but do not replace local decisions

The WHO *Comprehensive Mental Health Action Plan 2013-2030* highlights information systems, evidence generation, research and accessible care.

WHO/Europe also reported a launch of **Caregiver Skills Training (CST)** trainer preparation in Kazakhstan in February 2021. This launch announcement does not show that training is equally available in every region in 2026 or that its local effects were tested in a nationwide comparative trial.

[WHO action plan](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240031029), [WHO announcement on CST in Kazakhstan](https://www.who.int/europe/news/item/05-03-2021-who-europe-launches-caregiver-skills-training-in-kazakhstan-to-support-children-with-neurodevelopmental-delays).

AI technology merits separate scrutiny. WHO stresses responsible, ethical and accountable use of AI in healthcare. A system trained on behavioural characteristics should not be described as a ready diagnostic tool for children in Central Asia without independent testing of accuracy, bias, safety and local applicability.

[WHO: Artificial Intelligence for Health, 2024](https://www.who.int/publications/m/item/artificial-intelligence-for-health).

## How to discuss future prospects without false certainty

New multicountry studies, locally validated assessment methods and research into support outcomes **may** emerge in Central Asia over the next few years. Published evidence cannot predict exactly when, which initiatives will receive funding or whether a particular approach will prove effective.

Three kinds of statement should remain separate:

- **Verified fact:** a study was published, with identified participants, methods, results and limitations.
- **Well-founded research direction:** published evidence reveals a testable question and an appropriate study could be designed.
- **Hypothetical future outcome:** a programme will improve service access or a technology will benefit a specific group. This remains a hypothesis until properly examined.

Research need not promise a dramatic breakthrough. An important result may simply show that an existing questionnaire is inaccurate, national counts cannot be compared or an expensive technology failed to provide the expected benefit.

## What this research series offers readers

Our articles have examined registration statistics, regional variation, early identification, family experiences, parental wellbeing, education, technologies and limits of the evidence.

Readers can apply the same questions to any new autism-related claim:

**Who participated? What was measured? How was the study designed? How precise are the results? Can they apply to other families and countries? What does the study not establish?**

For the existing gaps, see [What does autism research in Central Asia still need to investigate?](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/en/research/autism-research-gaps-central-asia/).

**The promise of regional research lies not in building the largest article catalogue but in producing verifiable knowledge that autistic people, families, professionals and organisations can use when making decisions.**
