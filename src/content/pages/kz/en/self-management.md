## What is self-management?

Self-management encompasses ways in which people participate in planning, performing and tracking their own actions. For example, a child packs school supplies using a familiar list and asks for help if they cannot find a textbook. The adult does not need to remind them about every item.

This is a way of supporting specific skills, not a distinct autism therapy. Self-management differs from broader self-determination, which includes the right to make decisions about one's life, express preferences and choose support. It also does not replace co-regulation with an adult.

The aim is to expand the person's ability to manage everyday life. Some people need ongoing assistance from another person to do this. That need does not remove their right to make their own choices.

## Main components of self-management

| Component | Everyday example |
| --- | --- |
| Independent choice | Choose which accessible task to start with. |
| Goal setting | Decide together which items the child would like to learn to pack in a schoolbag. |
| Self-monitoring | Check whether all the necessary items have been packed. |
| Self-recording | Tick off an item that has already been packed. |
| Self-instruction | Consult a picture or short note about the next action. |
| Self-evaluation | Say what was comfortable and where help was needed. |
| Self-reinforcement | Acknowledge achievement of a personal goal and choose an enjoyable activity. |

It is not necessary to use every component at once. A list is sufficient for one person, while another may prefer spoken instructions or joint planning. Self-reinforcement must not become a system in which water, food, rest, communication or necessary supports have to be earned.

## How to choose a meaningful goal

Start with a task that is useful to the child. Discuss it in a way that is accessible to them: words, gestures, choosing objects, pictures or AAC. Consider interests, refusal and the preferred way of performing the task. If the child finds it difficult to express a choice, offer several clear options and observe their response.

Formulate an observable goal. Instead of 'become more organised', choose 'pack the necessary items using a list before leaving'. Instead of 'control yourself', choose 'ask for help or a break using an accessible method'.

Before starting, check why the task is currently difficult. If items are out of reach, the schedule is unclear or the child is tired, more control will not solve the problem. Adapt the conditions. Seek appropriate professional assessment for new pain, sudden loss of a skill or a substantial deterioration in wellbeing.

## Tools for independence

| Tool | What it can help with |
| --- | --- |
| Checklist | Check which items have been packed or actions completed. |
| Visual schedule | Find out what comes next and which changes are expected. |
| Timer | Plan time or receive advance notice of a change in activity. |
| Calendar | See upcoming events and choose when to prepare. |
| AAC | Ask for help, clarify, choose, report discomfort and refuse. |
| Phone reminder | Start a planned action at a suitable time. |

Choose a tool that the person finds comfortable to use. A checklist may consist of photographs, familiar symbols, words or objects. Size, contrast and placement should suit the child's needs. A timer should help with orientation, not force the child to hurry. If its sound is unpleasant, try a silent or other appropriate signal.

An activity schedule becomes a self-management tool when the person can consult it independently or participate in using it. If an adult constantly points out where to look and what to do, it is still shared use with support.

When choosing a digital tool, first consider simple local device functions. Applications that record the child's image, track their location or transmit sensitive information to third parties are unnecessary. An ordinary calendar or paper list is often sufficient for planning.

## How to gradually reduce adult control

1. Choose one useful and safe task together.
2. Observe an everyday situation and establish what the child can already do and where assistance is needed.
3. Choose an accessible tool and demonstrate how to use it. If necessary, create a list or schedule together.
4. Offer the tool in a natural situation. Assist where necessary and respond to requests for help.
5. When the child starts consulting the support independently, reduce unnecessary adult reminders. If difficulties arise, restore accessible assistance or change the tool.
6. Assess whether the action has become more accessible and comfortable for the child. Check use of the strategy in other suitable settings without unnecessary tests.

General principles of goal selection, teaching strategies and observing outcomes are described in [AFIRM materials](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED595396). This sequence is adapted for families and is not a mandatory research protocol.

There is no universal number of attempts, days or seconds to wait. Assistance may be increased again when needed. Do not remove necessary care, supervision or communication aids merely to reduce prompting scores. Further reading: [prompting and prompt fading (in Russian)](/kz/ru/parents/prompting-and-prompt-fading/).

## Practical examples for families

### Pack a schoolbag

Choose together several items needed for the next day. Place the list in an accessible location near the bag. The child can tick off an item after packing it or check the list at the end. Initially, the adult helps consult the list, then reduces only the reminders the child no longer needs.

Example list: check the timetable, pack the required notebooks and textbooks, take a pencil case and water bottle. The list changes depending on the school day. Do not require every item to be completed without assistance if some objects are heavy or inaccessible.

### Morning hygiene

The child uses a familiar sequence: wash their face, brush their teeth and dry their face. Where possible, they can choose a comfortable order. If an adult helps clean the teeth thoroughly or complete another difficult step, that assistance remains. Independently ticking an item does not establish that the action was performed safely or adequately.

### Prepare materials for an activity

Choose paper, pencils and other safe materials together. The child checks what they will need and communicates if an item is missing. This may be more convenient than constant instructions. If the order of preparation does not affect the outcome, allow a different approach.

### Organise schoolwork

An adolescent can mark deadlines in a calendar, choose the order of accessible tasks and plan breaks. Instead of reminding them about each item, offer an agreed time to discuss difficulties together. Help establish a realistic workload. If the demands are excessive, reconsider them rather than adding more control.

## Self-management during overload

Some people benefit from being able to indicate their state in advance or choose a break. Offer a convenient way to communicate, such as a gesture, AAC or a simple card with options 'continue', 'need help', 'need a break'. Such a card is not a medical scale and is not mandatory.

A child may not always recognise or communicate overload in advance. Adults must notice signs of discomfort, reduce demands and provide help regardless of whether the child has used the selected tool. Do not require them to complete a scale or say a specific phrase before allowing rest. Do not use self-management strategies to suppress safe stimming.

If overload is related to noise, pain, bullying or unpredictable demands, change the conditions and ensure safety. Discuss significant difficulties with an appropriate specialist. Self-management does not replace adult care.

## Independence with necessary support

A person may independently use a calendar, visual schedule, video instruction or AAC while requiring another type of assistance. For example, they may choose clothing independently but ask for help with a fastener. Asking for help can be an important expression of independence, not a failure.

Distinguish independent access to a tool from use after an adult reminder. If the person consults a familiar list independently, there is no need to remove it. Support may remain shared and ongoing where necessary.

Do not use the absence of prompts as the sole criterion for safe personal care, independent mobility, handling medication, or using hot or sharp objects. For these tasks, supervision is determined by risks and individual abilities, not success in a teaching table.

## Safety, choice and the right to refuse

| Supporting independence | What to avoid |
| --- | --- |
| Choosing a useful goal and method | Imposing goals for compliance. |
| A convenient tool | Compulsory completion of a table despite fatigue or refusal. |
| Accessible assistance | Prohibiting requests for help for the sake of formal independence. |
| Opportunity for rest | Requiring the child to earn a break. |
| Respecting communication methods | Removing AAC and forcing spoken language. |

Do not assess a child by the number of checkmarks, eye contact or ability to conceal autistic traits. If the child refuses, stop the teaching attempt, discuss an alternative and retain necessary support. Do not turn observation into constant testing. Records should help the family adapt conditions, not be used for punishment.

## How to assess outcomes, generalisation and maintenance

In everyday situations, observe whether the child accesses the tool independently, what they can do, what assistance is needed and whether they are comfortable continuing. Account separately for requests for help and refusals. If a situation did not occur, do not record it as an uncompleted task.

| Situation | Tool use and outcome | Next decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monday preparation | Adult reminded the child about the list, child packed with assistance. | Leave the list near the schoolbag. |
| Tuesday preparation | Child opened the list independently and asked for help finding a textbook. | Keep help accessible. |
| Timetable changed | List was no longer current, joint planning was needed. | Update the list. |
| Child was tired | Postponed preparation, adult helped. | Reconsider timing and workload. |

These are fictional situations, not an established learning rate. It is not necessary to create an equal number of attempts for comparison. Record separately how much adult assistance is needed and how convenient the tool itself is.

Generalisation means using the strategy in new suitable settings, for example when the school timetable changes. Maintenance means the strategy remains available over time. Assess this naturally while retaining necessary supports. One successful day does not guarantee future independence.

Reconsider the plan if the tool requires more effort than the task, monitoring causes stress, the child does not accept the goal, prompts increase or conditions change. Ask the specialist what kind of independence the programme develops, how the child's choice is considered, which supports will remain permanent and how generalisation and maintenance will be assessed. Knowledge of self-management techniques alone does not establish a specialist's professional qualification in Kazakhstan.

## What does the research say?

The [review by Hume and colleagues (2021)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8510990/) included self-management among 28 practices meeting the authors' criteria. The literature covered 1990-2017. [Scheibel and colleagues (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rasd.2023.102294) analysed 18 studies, 62 single-case designs involving 53 autistic students. A substantial proportion of the data concerns self-monitoring in educational tasks. This does not demonstrate equal effectiveness of all components or applicability to every everyday situation.

### Understanding the findings for daily living skills

The [meta-analysis by Aydin, Sulu and Ari-Arat (2025)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38709359/) was first published online in May 2024. The authors included 14 articles and one dissertation. Self-monitoring was used in 11 studies, and other components appeared substantially less frequently.

![Components in 15 studies: self-monitoring 11, self-instruction 3, self-recording 2, self-reinforcement 2, independent choice 1, self-evaluation 1. Categories overlap.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/diagrams/self-management-components-en.svg)

Self-monitoring: 11 of 15 studies. Self-instruction: 3. Self-recording and self-reinforcement: 2 each. Independent choice and self-evaluation: 1 each. Some studies used multiple components. This is the frequency of investigation, not a comparison of effectiveness.

![Of 15 studies, nine met WWC standards fully or with reservations, including one without reservations. Seven assessed maintenance and five assessed generalisation.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/diagrams/self-management-evidence-limits-en.svg)

Of 15 studies, one met What Works Clearinghouse (WWC) standards without reservations, eight with reservations and six did not meet them. Generalisation was assessed in 5 of 15 studies and maintenance in 7 of 15. These numbers represent the presence of measurements, not the number of successful outcomes. Even among studies with these measurements, the quality of individual phases differed. Both charts are based on the results section of the [full text by Aydin and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10803-024-06355-w).

The authors reported a large overall effect using Tau-U and a moderate effect using PCES. These are different statistical estimation methods, not percentages of people who acquired a skill. The meta-analysis examined personal and domestic skills, but not community living skills such as using transport or managing money. The most recent included studies were published in 2019. It therefore cannot establish a guaranteed reduction in everyday support needs.

### Adolescents, adults and tools

The [review by Liu and colleagues (2023)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36829367/) included 16 studies of technology-based self-monitoring in adolescents and adults. All used single-case designs, and the authors did not formally assess individual study quality. [Koyama and Wang (2011)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21645988/) examined 23 studies of activity schedules, and [Klavina and colleagues (2024)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39600392/) synthesised 18 studies of assistive technologies. These reviews concern different groups and types of support, rather than demonstrating universal effectiveness of an application or schedule.

[O'Neill and Smyth (2024)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37459464/) studied a package of self-management and accessible digital tools for academic tasks in autistic university students. The separate effect of a device cannot be isolated from the package results. [Cristescu and colleagues (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40489-025-00508-y) examined 27 publications on 19 interventions to develop self-determination in young people aged 15-25 with autism and/or intellectual disabilities. This review emphasises the importance of real environments, but self-determination is broader than self-monitoring.

In a [2026 study](https://doi.org/10.1002/jaba.70053) of five participants with developmental disabilities, teaching accurate self-monitoring did not produce the required engagement when self-monitoring alone was subsequently used. This is a small study of a particular package. It does not justify prescribing additional behavioural procedures at home, but reminds us that being able to record actions is not the same as performing the task.
