Children learn new skills gradually. They learn to interact with people, understand language, play, move, use objects, and become more independent.

Developmental milestones can help parents notice these changes. In the CDC system used here, a milestone is a skill that most children, 75% or more, can do by a certain age.

Important: **developmental milestones are not an exam, not an autism test, and not a way to make a diagnosis yourself**. One skill that your child does not yet show does not by itself mean that your child has a developmental condition.

## What are developmental milestones?

Milestones help you pay attention to different areas of development:

- social interaction
- language and understanding
- play, learning, and thinking
- movement and independence.

These areas are connected. It is more useful to look at the overall pattern and changes over time than to draw a conclusion from one item alone.

## How to use an observation sheet

You do not need to test your child or ask them to perform every action in sequence.

Observe what happens naturally:

- during play
- at mealtimes
- while dressing or bathing
- on walks
- when interacting with family members
- while reading or looking at books
- around other children.

For each milestone, you can choose one of three options:

**✅ Observed** - your child shows this skill.

**○ Not observed yet** - you have not yet noticed this skill.

**? Not sure** - it is difficult to tell, or you have seen it only once.

There are no right or wrong answers. The site does not calculate a score, result, or automatic conclusion. The marks are only for your own observations and for conversations with a professional.

## Look at real-life situations

Everyday examples help explain what a milestone means.

For example, instead of only saying "follows a simple direction," imagine this situation: you say "Give me the ball" without pointing to the ball or holding out your hand, and your child follows the request.

An example is not the only correct way to show a skill. Your child may demonstrate the same skill in a different situation.

## What if you choose "Not observed yet"?

One such answer does not diagnose anything by itself.

It can be useful to write down:

- which skill you have not yet observed
- what your child does instead
- whether the skill appears sometimes
- in which situations it appears
- in which situations it does not
- which similar skills your child already uses.

If the question remains or you are concerned about your child's development, discuss your observations with a doctor or another appropriate professional.

You do not need to wait until several concerns appear at the same time.

## If your child stopped doing something they used to do

A separate concern is when a child previously used a skill regularly and then stopped using it.

For example:

- used words and stopped using them
- performed familiar actions and no longer does them
- their usual way of moving changed
- stopped using another previously established skill.

Tell a healthcare professional about loss of a previously acquired skill.

## What to write down before speaking with a professional

You do not need to use medical terminology. Specific everyday examples are often more useful than general descriptions.

Instead of "He has speech problems," you could write: "He uses individual words but does not yet combine two words. When he wants a drink, he usually points to the cup."

Instead of "She does not play with children," you could write: "At the playground she plays next to other children and watches them, but rarely joins their shared play."

Before an appointment, you can write down:

- what your child already does well
- exactly what you are unsure or concerned about
- when you first noticed it
- how often it happens
- whether it happens at home, in preschool, or in other places
- what helps your child
- whether any skills have been lost
- which questions you want to ask.

Each age-specific sheet includes space for these notes. The site does not send your marks or notes to a server and does not intentionally save them.

## What this material cannot determine

Developmental milestones cannot tell you on their own:

- whether your child has autism
- whether your child has a developmental delay
- why a particular skill has not appeared yet
- whether your child needs a specific type of therapy
- which diagnosis might explain the observed differences.

Other methods are used for those questions, including professional assessment and, when appropriate, standardized screening tools.

## When to discuss your child's development

Consider speaking with a professional if:

- something about your child's development concerns you
- one or more skills are not appearing and this raises a question for you
- you are unsure whether a particular skill is developing
- your child has lost a skill they previously used regularly
- a teacher or another person who knows your child well has also noticed a change.

You do not need to identify the cause yourself before asking for advice.

## What to remember

Use milestones not to give your child a score, but to notice development more clearly.

A simple sequence is:

**observe -> record -> discuss**
