## Is autism inherited?

When a child receives an autism diagnosis, parents often wonder whether heredity is involved. Could another child in the family also be autistic? Would a DNA test provide an answer?

Genetic factors do make a substantial contribution to autism. This has been demonstrated in studies of families and twins, as well as large investigations of genetic variation.

However, autism is not inherited according to one rule that applies to every family. There is no single gene that always produces the same outcome.

Some genetic characteristics are inherited from parents. Others arise for the first time in a child and are not found in the parents.

Consequently, a child may be autistic even when neither parent has an autism diagnosis.

Two terms are important to distinguish:

- **Genetic** means that particular DNA characteristics are involved in how the body develops.
- **Hereditary** means that a genetic characteristic has been passed from parent to child.

These are not interchangeable. A genetic contribution to autism does not mean that a parent necessarily passed on a disease, nor does it enable anyone to predict how a child will develop.

## What are genes and how are they related to autism?

Almost every cell in our bodies contains DNA, a molecule that stores hereditary information.

Particular regions of DNA are called genes. Genes contribute to cellular function and the development of the body, including the nervous system. For instance, certain genes help regulate the formation of nerve cells and connections between them.

People's DNA sequences vary slightly. These differences are called genetic variants.

Most genetic variants are a normal part of human diversity and do not cause illness. Some, however, can influence how the nervous system develops.

### Why is there no single "autism gene"?

Different autistic people may carry different combinations of genetic variants.

Scientists investigate many genes involved in brain development, but carrying a variant in one of them does not necessarily mean a person will be autistic.

Two broad types of genetic variation are relevant.

**Common variants.** These are present in many people. Any one of them usually has a small effect, but their combined contribution can be associated with autism likelihood.

**Rare variants.** These occur far less frequently. Certain rare variants can substantially influence development and may be associated with particular genetic conditions.

Not every rare variant is harmful, however, and not every DNA finding explains a child's characteristics.

### What does 80% heritability mean?

A [large international study from 2019](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2019.1411) estimated the heritability of autism at approximately 80%.

This does not mean that 80% of an individual child's autism was caused by genes and the remaining 20% by environmental factors.

The researchers estimated how much genetic differences help explain variation between people in large populations. This figure does not identify the cause of autism in one child or calculate the probability for a future child in a particular family.

For a fuller explanation, see [Autism biology: what science knows today](../autism-biology-what-science-knows/).

## Why might a child be autistic when neither parent is?

The same genetic characteristic does not always have the same effect in different people.

A child may inherit a DNA variant from a parent who has no autism diagnosis. This is not necessarily a diagnostic mistake. The effect of a variant depends on its properties and how it interacts with other biological factors.

Some DNA changes also arise for the first time. Scientists describe these as *de novo* variants, meaning "newly arisen".

Such a change can appear while eggs or sperm are forming or in early embryonic development. It may be detected in a child but absent from the parental DNA samples tested.

The presence of a newly arisen variant is not, by itself, evidence that it causes autism or has any medical significance.

The absence of autism diagnoses among parents and other relatives therefore does not rule out a genetic contribution. Conversely, having an autistic parent does not mean all of their children will be autistic.

## Why can autism present differently within the same family?

Imagine two relatives who carry the same genetic variant.

One may have substantial communication difficulties and other developmental differences. The other may have less pronounced characteristics or may not meet the diagnostic criteria for autism at all.

Why can this happen?

Even when a DNA variant affects nervous system development, its effects may depend on many other genetic and biological circumstances.

Some variants are known to have **reduced penetrance**, meaning that not everyone who carries them shows the associated characteristic or condition. Their expression can also vary between carriers.

This helps explain why relatives with similar genetic findings may differ in speech, behaviour, abilities and support needs.

Autism can sometimes occur as part of a known genetic syndrome. In that situation, a child may have additional medical characteristics. Identifying the condition can help clinicians recognise associated risks and decide whether other health assessments are warranted.

Yet even an identified genetic condition cannot predict the entire course of a child's development.

## What is the likelihood of autism in younger siblings?

This is an important question for families who already have an autistic child.

Studies show that younger siblings of autistic children have a higher average likelihood of autism than children in the general population.

In a [2024 study published in *Pediatrics*](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2023-065297), researchers followed **1,605 younger siblings** of autistic children.

By the final developmental assessment, approximately **20.2%** of participants had received an autism diagnosis. In other words, around 20 out of every 100 younger children in this research group were diagnosed.

This does not mean that the probability for every individual family is exactly 20%.

### Why can the likelihood differ between families?

Several factors can affect study estimates.

For instance, the likelihood in this study differed according to the younger child's sex and the number of older children in the family who already had an autism diagnosis.

These differences were observed in groups and cannot be used to provide a precise prediction for an individual family.

When a particular genetic condition is identified, recurrence may sometimes be assessed more specifically. This requires knowing which variant has been identified, whether it is present in a parent and what is known about the inheritance of that condition.

These questions are best discussed with a medical geneticist.

### Should the development of a younger sibling be monitored more closely?

Yes. Where a family already has an autistic child, it is reasonable to discuss developmental monitoring for younger children with a paediatrician.

A clinician may pay particular attention to communication, social interaction, speech and other developmental characteristics.

Monitoring does not mean that the younger child is assumed to have autism. The purpose is to recognise possible difficulties promptly and arrange further assessment and support if needed.

## What can genetic testing show?

Some parents hope a DNA test will reveal the exact cause of their child's autism.

In some situations, genetic testing can identify a particular condition associated with the child's developmental characteristics.

For example, a child may be autistic and have additional medical features that together suggest a genetic condition. The findings may help a clinician clarify that condition, plan appropriate medical follow-up or discuss implications for relatives.

The central point is that **a genetic test is not an autism test**.

Autism is diagnosed through developmental history, communication and behavioural characteristics and a comprehensive clinical assessment. Genetic findings do not replace this process.

### Does every autistic child need genetic testing?

There is no single set of tests that is equally necessary for every autistic child. Clinical recommendations can differ across settings.

The clinician considers developmental history, family history, additional medical findings and the purpose of the proposed testing.

Genetic consultation may, for example, be particularly useful if a child has congenital differences, intellectual disability or features suggesting a specific inherited condition in addition to autism.

This does not mean genetic assessment would never be relevant for other children. Decisions are individual and should reflect appropriate current clinical guidance and available services.

Before testing, families can ask: what information are we trying to obtain, and would the result change medical follow-up?

## How should genetic test results be understood?

Suppose a laboratory report identifies a particular DNA variant. This alone does not mean the cause of autism has been found.

Specialists consider how well the variant has been studied, whether it is linked to a known condition and how well it fits the child's medical presentation.

Different kinds of results are possible.

### A variant with established medical significance is found

The report may classify a variant as **pathogenic** or **likely pathogenic**.

This means there is evidence linking it to a particular disease or condition. Even so, the result must be considered alongside the child's developmental history and health.

It does not necessarily explain every aspect of behaviour or allow an exact prediction of the future.

### A variant of uncertain significance is found

Sometimes the laboratory identifies a DNA change but cannot confidently determine whether it affects health.

This is called a **variant of uncertain significance (VUS)**.

A VUS should not automatically be considered the cause of autism. The interpretation of some variants may change as new research becomes available.

### The test finds no significant genetic changes

A negative result does not rule out autism.

Nor does it mean that genes played no part in the child's development. Different tests detect different kinds of variation, and scientific knowledge of genetics is incomplete.

Results should therefore be discussed with a specialist who can explain the capabilities and limitations of the test used.

Commercial tests promising to determine the "genetic severity of autism" or select an educational programme precisely from DNA require particular caution. Such capabilities are not adequately established for autism as a whole.

## What can parents discuss with a clinician?

Genetic information can be useful, but its meaning always depends on the individual circumstances.

Families with questions about inheritance or the need for testing can discuss the following:

1. Are there other health or developmental characteristics that make genetic assessment useful?
2. What could the proposed test reveal, and what will it not show?
3. Could the result change medical monitoring or the need for other assessments?
4. Might an identified genetic condition have implications for other family members?
5. Would consultation with a medical geneticist be helpful before another pregnancy?

Testing does not have to be pursued simply to obtain an explanation at any cost.

Even when no genetic cause is found, a family can still understand a child's development, support communication, consider medical needs and help the child learn new skills.

Genetics helps researchers and clinicians understand autism more fully. No genetic variant, however, describes a whole person.
