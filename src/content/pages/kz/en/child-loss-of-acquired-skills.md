Parents sometimes notice that a child no longer uses words, gestures, play actions or movements that were once part of everyday life. In medicine this may be described as **developmental regression** or loss of previously acquired skills. It is important to distinguish an established ability disappearing from a child not yet having acquired that skill.

**If you think your child has lost an established skill, contact a healthcare professional without prolonged delay.** Do not wait to collect video evidence or complete an entire observation sheet. Sudden serious neurological symptoms require emergency help.

## 1. What does it mean to "lose a skill"?

Developmental regression refers to a skill previously learned and reliably available becoming absent or substantially less accessible.

Two points matter:

1. **The skill had been acquired.** The child regularly used a particular word to make a request, pointed, walked independently or completed a familiar task.
2. **There was a clear change relative to this particular child's past abilities**, not simply a difference from another child's developmental milestones.

Communication, play, social interaction, movement and self-care can all be affected. A family report or one recording cannot identify the precise cause.

### Example: a word that never appeared versus a word that disappeared

A child who has never said "water" and a child who said "water" independently and repeatedly but has now stopped using it have different developmental histories.

Both situations may justify consulting a doctor. In the second, describe **when the word was previously used and when it stopped appearing**.

## 2. What changes might parents notice?

| Area | Previously established skill | Change worth reporting |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Speech** | Used familiar words independently to request things. | Those words disappear or are used much less often. |
| **Gestures** | Pointed, waved goodbye or made clear requests. | No longer uses familiar gestures and does not replace them with another understood method. |
| **Social interaction** | Regularly invited relatives to join a familiar game. | Previously typical initiative is now absent and the context needs assessment. |
| **Play** | Consistently performed a familiar sequence of play actions. | No longer carries it out under comparable conditions. |
| **Gross motor skills** | Walked or stood confidently in a familiar way. | Develops weakness, gait change or loss of ability to perform a movement. |
| **Daily living** | Held a spoon or helped with dressing. | Now needs more help with familiar tasks. |

This table identifies possible areas of observation. It is **not a diagnostic checklist**. A child declining to perform an action once is not proof of regression.

## 3. How loss of skills differs from a temporary difficulty

Sometimes a child can still perform an action but does not use it in particular circumstances. Illness, exhaustion, noisy environments, unfamiliar people, pain or changed opportunities for communication can contribute.

**However, suspected loss must not be dismissed as ordinary tiredness without seeking medical advice.**

| Observation | What to ask | What must not be concluded |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Did not say a familiar word today. | Is it used at another time or with another person? | "The child definitely forgot" or "is refusing on purpose". |
| Plays less after illness. | Is activity returning and are there other symptoms? | "This never needs medical assessment". |
| No longer responds when called. | Hearing, ear illness, other adults' reports? | "It must be autism" or "deliberate ignoring". |
| No longer performs a previously easy movement. | Onset, weakness, pain, asymmetry or injury? | "Just practise the movement more". |

Parents need not prove loss of a skill before seeking care. If you are unsure whether it was consistently acquired, tell the clinician.

## 4. When medical help is needed

These are general safety principles, **not a personalised triage algorithm**. Contact local health services when uncertain.

### Emergency situations

Urgent or emergency assessment is needed when a child suddenly develops:

- significant weakness in an arm, leg or one side of the face
- loss of consciousness or marked change in responsiveness
- seizures, particularly first-time or prolonged seizures
- breathing difficulty
- serious injury, pronounced confusion or another acute potentially life-threatening symptom.

NICE NG127 recommends immediate assessment for a **new gait abnormality** and sudden or rapidly progressive weakness affecting the limbs or face. New unsteadiness should not be left for routine monitoring even without obvious weakness. NHS resources also identify seizures and impaired consciousness among emergency warning signs.

**Do not wait for a routine appointment, film an episode instead of helping, or repeatedly test every skill before seeking emergency care.**

### Loss of skills without acute symptoms

Even if a child appears otherwise well, a loss of previously established speech, social or motor skills warrants **timely professional assessment**.

CDC advises acting early when previously attained skills are lost. NICE distinguishes loss of language and social skills before age three, loss of language after age three and motor regression at any age. These age categories inform professional referral in the UK. They do not allow families to determine an individual child's urgency.

Tell the clinician the child's age, what changed and when.

## 5. Loss of skills does not automatically mean autism

Some children later diagnosed with autism show changes or loss of previously acquired speech and social communication in early childhood. **Regression alone does not confirm autism.**

Depending on symptoms and history, a clinician may consider:

- changes in hearing that alter responses to spoken language
- neurological conditions, including some associated with epilepsy
- other medical or genetic conditions
- illness, injury, pain or significant sleep changes
- environmental and communication factors that may mimic a lost ability.

These are **possibilities for medical assessment**, not a list of likely diagnoses for a particular child. Most cannot be reliably investigated at home.

It is also inappropriate to attribute new skill loss automatically to vaccination, parenting or bilingualism without evidence and medical evaluation.

1. **Contact a healthcare professional.** Say that a previously established skill is no longer being used. Call emergency services for acute danger signs.
2. **Describe the ability precisely.** Instead of "stopped developing", explain "used to say 'more' during snacks every day, now rarely does".
3. **Describe when the change began.** If unsure, say "approximately two weeks ago" or "since starting the school year".
4. **Describe where it occurs.** At home, preschool, during play, with different adults and in different languages.
5. **Mention other changes.** Illness, hearing concerns, sleep problems, weakness, pain, unusual movements or periods of unresponsiveness.
6. **Bring any existing notes**, old video clips or educators' observations if readily available.

No prior video is necessary. Do not delay care to make new recordings or put the child through repeated tests.

## 7. Three useful examples of how to describe a change

### Example 1. Previously familiar words disappear

**Before:** "From July through September the child said 'more' and 'give' independently when requesting another turn."

**Now:** "Since early October those words are rarely heard and requests are usually expressed through gestures."

**Tell the clinician:** when the words began, whether they were used without prompting, which languages were involved and whether hearing or health changed.

**Do not conclude:** that autism caused the change or the child is "too lazy to speak".

### Example 2. Walking has changed

**Before:** "The child walked confidently between rooms without support."

**Now:** "Over several days the child started stumbling and cannot walk the usual distance."

**Tell the clinician:** time of onset, weakness or pain, injury, illness or sensory changes.

**Important:** NICE NG127 calls for immediate medical evaluation of a new gait abnormality, even if obvious weakness is absent. Sudden or rapidly progressive weakness also needs immediate assessment.

### Example 3. The child stops showing objects

**Before:** "For several months the child regularly pointed to passing cars to share interest on walks."

**Now:** "Pointing to interesting things has become much rarer even on familiar walks."

**Tell the clinician:** whether other gestures disappeared, what family and preschool staff observe, and any hearing or health changes.

**Do not conclude:** that this observation alone confirms or excludes autism.

## 8. What may happen at a medical appointment?

Depending on the skill involved and associated signs, a clinician may review development before and after the change, discuss hearing and speech, evaluate general and neurological health, and consider referral for hearing tests, paediatric neurology or other developmental services.

**Not every child needs the same blood tests, electroencephalography (EEG), MRI or genetic testing.** Investigations depend on clinical indications, not on reading this article.

UK NICE guidelines describe referral routes that do not automatically apply in Kazakhstan. For Kazakhstan service navigation see [where to seek help for developmental concerns](../../help-kazakhstan/child-development-concerns-kazakhstan/).

## 9. What to do at home while awaiting an appointment

The immediate family priority is **safety, accessible communication and comfort**, not forcing the skill to return.

- Accept gestures, pictures or AAC if speaking has become harder.
- Do not insist on repeating missing words or movements "to make sure".
- Offer practical help if familiar tasks now need extra support.
- Consider pain, fatigue, sleep, noise and new limitations.
- Report new symptoms promptly and seek emergency help when serious signs appear.
- Do not replace medical assessment with supplements, medication or an intensive unprescribed programme.

Continue safe enjoyable games and interactions the child still chooses. A change in one skill does not mean the child must stop all preferred activities.

## 10. Questions to ask a healthcare professional

1. Which developmental changes need evaluation first?
2. Is separate assessment of hearing, speech, movement or other functions indicated?
3. Which signs mean we should seek further help sooner?
4. Do we need another specialist and why?
5. What can family members and educators observe without testing the child?
6. How can we preserve communication and daily participation?
7. How and when will we receive results and agree on the next steps?

These questions help organise an appointment but do not replace examination.

## 12. Parents' frequently asked questions

### "Can a child stop doing something and then start again?"

Some abilities vary with context or health. But this is not a reason to ignore suspected loss of an established skill. Discuss a significant or persistent change with a clinician.

### "If my child stops talking, does that always mean autism?"

No. Language regression does not define a diagnosis. Hearing, neurological health, development and circumstances may need assessment.

### "If a child uses a gesture instead of a word, is that regression?"

It may be a meaningful change, but **gestures are valid communication, not automatically a deterioration**. Clarify whether the word was previously used reliably, whether circumstances have changed and which communication method is preferred. Describe this to a professional.

### "Must I repeatedly test the ability to be certain?"

No. Repeated or coercive testing can be distressing and does not provide a reliable medical conclusion. Describe the concern and seek advice.

### "What if the doctor recommends waiting?"

Ask how the loss of a previously established skill was considered, which warning signs warrant an earlier return, whether further assessment is necessary and when follow-up will occur. Recontact a clinician or seek another medical opinion if concerns persist or the condition changes.

## 13. Sources and limits of these recommendations

CDC recommends acting early when previously acquired developmental skills are lost. NICE includes language, social and motor regression among reasons for clinical referral. AAP explains why unusual neurological symptoms and developmental changes may warrant paediatric neurology assessment. NHS resources describe acute situations requiring urgent care.

**There is no universal home test** that can determine all causes of regression or establish a diagnosis from a few observations. Clinical referral pathways differ by country.

## Key takeaways

**If a skill your child previously used reliably disappears or markedly worsens, tell a healthcare professional without lengthy delay.** Loss of a skill does not automatically mean autism. Sudden neurological or other dangerous symptoms require emergency services. Notes can help an appointment but must never replace or delay clinical evaluation.
