## Why is neurodiversity discussed alongside autism?

People differ in how they perceive information, learn, communicate and respond to the world. The concept of **neurodiversity** helps us discuss these differences without assuming that there is only one “correct” way to think or behave.

For parents, this perspective can help them see their child as more than a diagnosis: a person with their own interests, abilities and needs. At the same time, neurodiversity **does not erase real difficulties, disability or the need for medical and educational support**.

## Neurodiversity, neurodivergence and neurotypicality: what is the difference?

These terms are often confused. It helps to consider each separately.

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Neurodiversity** | Variation in nervous systems and ways of processing information among a group of people. | Children in a classroom have different developmental and perceptual characteristics. |
| **Neurodivergent person** | Someone whose nervous system functions in ways that differ significantly from what is considered typical. | An autistic person. In broader use, ADHD and some other differences may also be described as neurodivergent. |
| **Neurotypical person** | Someone whose development and information processing generally match common expectations. | A child with no identified marked neurodevelopmental differences. This is not a judgement of their abilities or worth. |
| **Neurodiversity-affirming approach** | An approach that regards differences as part of human diversity while recognising the need for support. | A school adapts communication practices and the sensory environment so children can participate in accessible ways. |

A key distinction is that *neurodiversity* refers to variation **within a group**, while *neurodivergence* can describe **an individual**. Neither term is, in itself, a medical diagnosis. There is no universally agreed exhaustive list of all forms of neurodivergence (National Autistic Society).

## Is autism an illness, a difference or a disability?

There is no single short answer that fits every context. International diagnostic classifications describe autism as a neurodevelopmental condition. In everyday life, many people also consider autism part of their identity. Some autistic people identify as disabled because they face substantial barriers or need significant support.

Three perspectives are useful:

- **Clinical perspective:** helps identify developmental characteristics, co-occurring conditions and needs for professional support.
- **Social perspective:** draws attention to environmental barriers such as noise, inaccessible communication, inflexible rules and prejudice.
- **Personal experience:** helps us understand what matters to an autistic person and how they want to describe themselves.

These perspectives can work together. For example, a child can be given an accessible way to communicate while a separate medical condition is treated and restrictive school rules are changed. Accepting a person and providing necessary support **are not contradictory**.

## “Autistic person” or “person with autism”?

Both expressions are used in English. This distinction is often called *identity-first language* (“autistic person”) and *person-first language* (“person with autism”).

According to the National Autistic Society, many autistic people prefer language that treats autism as part of their identity. But preferences **are not universal**. The most respectful approach is to **ask the individual which wording they prefer**. If they do not use spoken language, consider other accessible ways for them to express a choice.

Do not impose one expression on an entire community or argue with someone about how they should describe themselves.

## Which expressions can give a misleading impression?

Some familiar expressions oversimplify or distort a person's experience.

| Avoid as a blanket description | Why | A more precise alternative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Suffers from autism” | Assumes that every autistic person necessarily suffers because of autism itself. | “Autistic person” or the expression the person prefers. |
| “Normal child” contrasted with an autistic child | Implies a hierarchy of people's worth. | “Neurotypical child” or “non-autistic child”, when the comparison is relevant. |
| “High-functioning” or “low-functioning” | One label cannot describe all skills and situations. | “Uses spoken language and needs help with planning and daily living”, or another specific description. |
| “Doesn't understand anything because they don't speak” | Speaking and understanding are not the same. | “Does not use spoken language. Their communication and understanding need individual assessment.” |
| “We need to make the child look normal” | Shifts attention from wellbeing to meeting others' expectations. | “We need accessible communication and support that helps the child participate in everyday life.” |

Medical, educational and legal documents sometimes use formal diagnostic terms. This does not mean the same style is necessary in everyday conversations with families.

## What does respecting neurodivergence look like in practice?

Imagine a child covering their ears at a school event and not answering a question immediately. There may be several explanations, including excessive noise, a need for more time to process speech, tiredness or another aspect of how the child feels.

A respectful approach does not mean ignoring difficulties or discouraging professional questions. It means first exploring what makes participation hard, then considering accessible adjustments: reducing noise, allowing more time, offering a break or accepting another way to respond.

Such adjustments may help, but **every specific support method must still be assessed for its safety, goals and evidence**. Calling a service “neuro-affirming” does not, by itself, establish that it works.

## Can we accept autism and still help a child develop skills?

Yes. Support does not have to aim at making a child appear non-autistic. It may focus on functional communication, independence, taking part in activities, reducing pain and distress, and enabling a child to express their preferences.

Respecting identity does not remove complex support needs. Some people require intensive help throughout their lives. That does not make them less valuable, nor does it mean that serious difficulties should be hidden behind exclusively positive language.

## How can we speak with the child and others?

- Describe specific strengths and difficulties rather than focusing only on the diagnosis.
- Where possible, ask how the child experiences situations and which words they prefer.
- Explain useful accommodations, such as predictability or accessible communication, without sharing unnecessary private information.
- Do not assume a person's intelligence, empathy, talents or future on the basis of autism.
- Distinguish respect from agreeing with every action: safe boundaries can be maintained while protecting the person's dignity.

## Key points to remember

Neurodiversity is a way to discuss human differences, **not a separate diagnosis or a substitute for individual assessment**. Respecting autistic people's preferences is compatible with evidence-based healthcare, accessible education and practical support.
