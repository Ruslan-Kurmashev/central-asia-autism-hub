After an autism diagnosis or developmental assessment, parents may be offered many kinds of support: speech and language therapy, a psychologist, a special educator, medical appointments, communication programmes, behaviour-related support, sensory activities, diets and other approaches.

A programme's name alone cannot tell you whether your child needs it.

A more useful sequence is:

> **First identify the child's need. Next select a professional with the right skills. Then agree in advance how you will assess outcomes.**

## Five questions before starting sessions

Before agreeing to a programme, try to get answers to five questions:

1. What specific difficulty or need are we addressing?
2. Why is this professional suitable for the task?
3. What exactly will happen during sessions?
4. How will we know whether the support is helping?
5. When will the plan be reviewed?

If you cannot get understandable answers, do not rush into paying for a long course.

<span id="step-1"></span>
## 1. Identify the child's most important need

An autism diagnosis does not, on its own, establish which sessions a child needs now.

Two children with the same diagnosis may have very different needs. For example:

- a child struggles to communicate that they want a drink, food, a break or help
- significant sleep problems have developed
- a child can speak but finds school demands difficult
- a child struggles to eat, dress or use the toilet independently
- situations arise in which the child hurts themselves or others
- there is a sudden behaviour change or loss of previously acquired skills.

Start by asking:

> **What currently makes it hardest for the child to stay safe, communicate, learn, be independent or participate in everyday life?**

### When to see a doctor first

If pain, seizures, sudden loss of skills, severe changes in sleep or eating, sudden changes in health or other significant physical symptoms occur, consider possible medical causes.

Do not automatically explain a child's behaviour as autism alone.

For behaviours that create serious difficulties, NICE recommends considering communication challenges, physical illness and pain, anxiety and other co-occurring conditions, physical and social environments, changes in routine and other factors.

Instead of asking:

> "How can we stop this behaviour?"

ask:

> **"What might be causing this behaviour, and what need might the child be expressing?"**

If you are still considering which observations to discuss with a professional, see [When to discuss a child's development with a specialist](/kz/ru/parents/kogda-obsudit-razvitie-rebenka-so-specialistom/).

<span id="step-2"></span>
## 2. Choose a professional for the specific task

These are possible starting points:

| Main difficulty | Where to start |
|---|---|
| Pain, seizures, skill loss or sudden change in condition | An appropriately qualified medical practitioner |
| Speech and communication | A speech and language professional, speech therapist or special educator, depending on the goal |
| Learning | A teacher, special educator or educational and psychological support professionals |
| Daily living skills | A professional competent in teaching the specific functional skills |
| Behaviour that seriously disrupts life | First assess possible causes and the function of the behaviour |
| Significant sleep, eating or other physical problems | Begin with medical assessment |

This table is not a diagnostic algorithm or universal treatment pathway. One child may need support from several professional disciplines.

### If the concern involves behaviour

Look for someone who can do more than promise to "remove unwanted behaviour". A suitable professional should be able to:

- define the difficulty clearly
- understand the situations in which it occurs
- consider medical, communication-related and environmental causes
- set a goal that benefits the child
- measure changes
- revise an ineffective plan.

For behaviour-related interventions, NICE recommends functional assessment, clearly defined target behaviours, links to quality of life, measurement before and after intervention and a defined review period.

<span id="step-3"></span>
## 3. Check the professional's qualifications

It is reasonable to ask:

> What is your basic professional education?

> What qualifications do you hold?

> What specific training do you have for the problem we are bringing?

> What experience do you have with similar needs?

Many certificates from short courses do not, by themselves, establish professional qualifications. Additional training can be useful but does not always replace relevant formal education.

### If the professional is a psychologist

Kazakhstan's Law on Psychological Activity has been in force since 2 September 2026.

The law establishes requirements for psychological services and principles of professional competence and scientific grounding. People receiving psychological support, or their legal representatives, must be informed about its aims and methods.

Before starting, ask about:

- education in psychology
- professional qualifications
- the proposed goal
- the methods being used
- why this particular approach was chosen
- how informed consent is documented
- how private psychological practice is formally arranged.

The law requires an agreement for the provision of psychological services in private psychological practice.

#### What about the state register of psychologists?

The law provides for a state register of psychologists. A transitional period applies: psychologists have one year from the law's entry into force to complete registration. Certain certification provisions begin on 1 January 2028.

During this transition, the absence of a register entry should not be the sole measure of a professional's quality. Education, qualifications, competence and the proposed service can already be evaluated.

### If the professional is a speech therapist or special educator

Kazakhstan's model qualification requirements for teaching positions set rules for the relevant roles in educational organisations. Special educators and speech therapists must meet requirements for relevant teaching qualifications or recognised professional retraining.

These rules apply to particular positions in educational settings. Do not judge a private service solely by its advertised title.

Ask:

- what foundational education the person has
- which specialty appears on their diploma
- which additional training they completed
- what experience they have with the child's particular needs.

### If a medical service is offered

Medical activity in Kazakhstan is licensed. A separate professional certification system also governs admission to clinical practice.

If a centre provides medical diagnosis, treatment or procedures, check:

- which organisation provides the service
- whether it has a medical activity licence
- whether the licensed scope covers the service
- who will perform the medical intervention
- whether that person has the necessary authorisation for clinical practice.

You can check licences and permits through the official eGov [verification of permits and licences](https://egov.kz/cms/ru/services/proverka-razresheniy-i-licenziy).

It is important to distinguish healthcare from psychological and educational support. A speech therapist, teacher or another professional providing a non-medical service does not automatically need a medical licence as the universal measure of their work.

<span id="step-4"></span>
## 4. Agree on a specific goal

A good goal answers this question:

> **What will change in the child's everyday life if support works?**

Compare:

| Too vague | More specific |
|---|---|
| "Develop speech" | "The child can ask for water or help in a way they can use" |
| "Eliminate problem behaviour" | "Understand difficulties during transitions and teach the child to ask for a break" |
| "Develop independence" | "The child completes 3 out of 5 dressing steps independently" |
| "Improve socialisation" | "The child participates in a chosen shared activity in a comfortable way" |

Useful goals generally concern safety, communication, independence, learning, participation or quality of life.

### If the aim is only to change outward behaviour

If a programme targets increased eye contact, stopping repetitive movements or changing another outwardly unusual behaviour as a goal in itself, ask:

> **What concrete benefit would this bring the child?**

Give greater priority to goals connected with safety, communication, independence, participation and quality of life.

<span id="step-5"></span>
## 5. Decide in advance how to measure outcomes

"He or she is doing better" is too vague to evaluate.

Record the starting situation before beginning. For example:

- the child independently asks for help about once a day
- the child wakes during five nights out of seven
- leaving home for preschool frequently takes about 30 minutes
- the child independently completes one of five steps in a routine.

Then you can compare the situation after a period of time.

A simple diary may be enough:

**what happened -> when -> where or in what situation -> what happened beforehand -> what happened afterwards.**

### Agree on a plan review from the start

Before sessions begin, ask:

> **When will we discuss whether this programme is helping?**

At review, check:

- whether there are measurable changes
- whether something important to the child improved
- whether the skill works outside the session room
- whether things have become more difficult for the child
- whether the goal remains suitable
- whether the approach or delivery should change.

If the only explanation for lack of progress is "add more sessions", ask what evidence supports that decision and why the plan itself is not being reconsidered.

<span id="red-flags"></span>
## Warning signs

One sign alone does not always mean that a professional is unsuitable. Several together warrant particular care.

### Guaranteed outcomes

Examples include:

> "After 20 sessions the child will definitely speak."

> "After three months the child will be like a typical child."

> "100% results."

In Kazakhstan this is especially important for psychological services: the Law on Psychological Activity directly prohibits psychologists from guaranteeing a specific outcome of psychological help, including in advertising and other communications.

### Promises to "cure autism"

Responsible support should focus on the child's needs, health, communication, skills, independence, participation and quality of life rather than promise to "remove autism".

### The same plan for every child

If children of different ages, communication abilities, development and health all receive essentially identical programmes, ask how individualisation is done.

### Goals are unclear

A professional should explain what they are doing, why, what goal is being worked on and how the outcome is measured.

### Outcomes are not measured

If nobody knows what happened before or after the intervention, objective assessment is difficult.

### Every difficulty is attributed to autism

Pain, sleep problems, anxiety, physical illness, communication barriers and environmental factors can influence behaviour.

### Working outside professional competence

Be especially cautious if a non-medical provider prescribes medicines, proposes changing dosages without a physician, recommends avoiding necessary medical assessment or offers their own programme as a replacement for medical care.

Kazakhstan's Law on Psychological Activity specifically prohibits psychologists from conducting medical diagnosis or prescribing medicines. A psychologist must decline work outside their professional competence.

## What if a centre asks for a large advance payment?

A large prepayment is not, on its own, proof of poor-quality service.

What matters is that you understand before paying:

- the child's need being addressed
- the goal
- how outcomes will be measured
- when the plan will be reviewed
- the price of the chosen stage
- the terms for ending the programme.

If you are offered dozens or hundreds of sessions before these points are discussed, request a clearer plan first.

<span id="priorities"></span>
## What to prioritise

If recommendations feel overwhelming, a simple practical sequence can help.

### 1. Health and safety

Pain, seizures, skill loss, serious sleep or feeding problems, risk of injury and sudden health changes need attention first.

### 2. Communicating basic needs

Ways to ask for help, water, food, the toilet, a break or an unpleasant activity to stop may be especially important.

Communication does not mean spoken language alone. The method should be accessible to the child.

### 3. Everyday independence

Examples include eating, dressing, hygiene, toileting and participating in routines at home.

### 4. Participation in education and daily life

If preschool, school, walks, transport or family activities are difficult, identify skills or environmental adjustments that could help.

### 5. Other individual goals

After the most important needs are addressed, less urgent goals can gradually be added.

This is not an official clinical priority scale. It is a practical way of organising many recommendations.

## Skills should work in everyday life

A useful skill should gradually transfer from the professional's office to the child's everyday settings.

A new way of asking for help, for instance, is useful not only in sessions but at home, at school or elsewhere.

WHO Caregiver Skills Training provides one possible approach: parents and other caregivers use everyday games, household activities and routines to support communication, engagement, positive behaviour and daily living skills. The main WHO CST package is designed for families of children aged 2-9 with developmental delays or disabilities.

This does not mean parents should become full-time therapists for their children.

A helpful question for the professional is:

> **What can we do in daily life to support this goal without overloading the child or family?**

<span id="questions"></span>
## Ten questions to ask a professional before the first session

1. What education and qualifications do you have?
2. What training relates specifically to our concern?
3. What exact need of the child would you address?
4. Why is that the priority now?
5. What will happen in sessions?
6. What evidence supports the approach?
7. What is the child's baseline now?
8. How will progress be measured?
9. When will we review the plan?
10. What will we do if the expected changes do not appear?

If the programme is paid, also ask:

> **What will the proposed stage cost, what is included, and what are the termination conditions?**

## Key point

Do not begin with the question:

<blockquote class="article-choice-warning">
  <p>"Which autism therapy is best?"</p>
</blockquote>

Start with three questions:

<ol class="article-choice-steps" role="list">
  <li>What does my child specifically need now?</li>
  <li>Which professional is competent to address that need?</li>
  <li>How will we know whether the support really works?</li>
</ol>

This helps families choose support based on the child's needs rather than marketing, method names or the number of sessions offered.
