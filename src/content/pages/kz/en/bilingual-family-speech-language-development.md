Families in Kazakhstan often use Russian, Kazakh and sometimes a third language. Parents may wonder whether hearing two languages causes delays, whether language mixing needs correction, and whether an autistic child should use only one language.

**Language access and meaningful communication matter more than a rigid language rule.** Evidence does not support automatically removing a family's home language because a child has communication differences. At the same time, real concerns about understanding or communication must not be dismissed as "just bilingualism".

## 1. What counts as a bilingual environment?

A multilingual family does not need to assign exactly one language to each adult. Common arrangements include:

- Parents speaking Kazakh at home while part of the child's learning takes place in Russian.
- Russian-speaking parents and a Kazakh-speaking grandmother or grandfather.
- Adults switching between languages depending on a topic or conversation partner.
- Two languages at home and a third at nursery.
- A child understanding both languages but usually replying in one.

**Bilingual does not mean equally fluent in both languages.** Comprehension, vocabulary, pronunciation and comfortable topics vary across settings, ages and people. ASHA highlights that language preference can change over time. None of these patterns alone establishes impairment.

## 2. Do two languages cause a speech delay?

**Bilingualism itself does not cause a language or speech disorder.** ASHA and AAP make this distinction clearly.

Consider two different scenarios:

- **Language difference:** a child has learned the Kazakh name for an object but not yet the Russian word. This can reflect differing exposure.
- **Possible developmental difficulty:** the child has persistent trouble understanding or conveying messages even in a familiar language and situation. Hearing, communication access and developmental history must also be considered.

Families cannot reliably make a diagnosis by counting words. Seek an appropriately qualified speech-language professional familiar with multilingual development, with interpretation support when necessary.

### Myth: "Start with one language and add the second only after speech develops"

There is no universal scientific basis for removing a home language to speed up speech. Doing so may reduce a child's access to grandparents and other relatives. Families can choose the pattern that feels natural, but autism or language delay alone does not justify an automatic one-language restriction.

## 3. Why a child may mix Russian and Kazakh

A child might use a Russian word in one part of a sentence and a Kazakh word in another because those words are most accessible at that moment. This is often called *code-mixing* or *code-switching*. It can be a normal aspect of multilingual development and does not in itself indicate confusion, autism or a disorder.

For example, a child says "myach" in Russian and "dop" in Kazakh in different contexts. Or answers a Kazakh question in Russian. A responsive adult may say:

- Child: "Доп!"
- Adult in Russian: "Да, мяч. Хочешь покатать?"
- Adult in Kazakh: "Иә, доп. Допты домалатайық".

Respond to **what the child is communicating**, rather than correcting every word or demanding translations.

## 4. Books, songs and family stories

Supporting language does not require expensive products. Everyday interactions around the child's interests are useful.

### Picture books

Let the child choose a page. Name an interesting object, comment on an event or simply share attention. Reading the printed text is optional. One adult might say "Машина едет", while another says "Көлік жүріп барады". The child does not have to recall or translate both phrases.

### Songs and family games

Sing familiar songs in either family language. Add movement if the child enjoys it, pause to invite a response and lower the volume or change activities if the child is uncomfortable.

### Family photographs

Talk about people and places the child knows. Words connected with their actual life may be more useful than unfamiliar lists from a test.

### Walking together

Name something the child notices, such as a bird, bus, tree or snow. Avoid turning every walk into a stream of questions. Conversation also happens through pointing, shared attention and choice.

## 5. When a child uses few words or does not speak

Communication is not limited to spoken words. A child may request or refuse using gestures, pictures, pointing, choosing an object, a device for augmentative and alternative communication (**AAC**), body movement or another accessible method.

**Never withhold essential food, water, rest or help until a child speaks in Russian or Kazakh.** If the child points to a picture meaning "water", respond to the request rather than insisting on "су" or "вода" first.

If two languages are important to family life, discuss vocabulary, interface and partner adaptation with an AAC professional. Available tools vary in how well they support Kazakh and Russian. Research on bilingual AAC is limited, but that is not a reason to refuse communication access.

## 6. What autism research says about bilingualism

A 2023 systematic review included **22 publications** and found no overall evidence that exposure to more than one language harms autistic children's language and communication skills. Other reviews generally point in the same direction.

Important limitations remain:

- Studies vary by age, languages, outcome measures and methodology.
- Children with intellectual disability and complex communication needs were often excluded.
- Research specific to Kazakh-Russian language combinations remains limited.
- Evidence does not identify one best language schedule for every autistic family.
- Decisions about support should not be based only on how many languages a child hears.

The appropriate conclusion is that there is **no evidence-based reason to automatically prohibit bilingualism in autism**, but individual outcomes cannot be promised.

## 7. Assessing a child who uses two languages

Testing vocabulary only in Russian can underestimate what a child knows in Kazakh. Simply adding every word in both languages is also not a universal norm because translated equivalents, experience and meaning must be considered.

A speech-language professional should select assessment methods compatible with the child's languages and background.

### What to tell a speech-language therapist or doctor

- When and where did the child begin hearing each language?
- Which languages do parents, grandparents and other relatives use?
- What languages are used at preschool?
- What does the child understand and how do they express needs in each language?
- What gestures, words, phrases or AAC methods are available?
- Have established skills developed or disappeared?
- Are there hearing concerns, ear infections or other health changes?
- Can assessment include an appropriately skilled bilingual clinician or interpreter?

ASHA cautions against applying monolingual standardised scores to children who were not represented in the test's normative sample. Family observations, language samples, dynamic assessment and an interpreter can all be relevant. Parents should not be required to administer and score specialist tests by themselves.

## 8. Language difference or reason to seek assessment?

| Observation | What it does not prove | A useful response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Says "су" but not "вода". | Does not by itself prove language delay. | Consider experience and vocabulary in both languages. |
| Mixes Russian and Kazakh. | Does not independently suggest confusion or autism. | Respond to the meaning. |
| Understands a grandparent better than an educator. | Does not identify the cause. | Explore language, familiarity, wording and setting. |
| Uses pictures or gestures rather than words. | Does not mean there is no communication. | Support accessible communication. |
| Persistent difficulties even in familiar situations. | One observation cannot diagnose a disorder. | Arrange multilingual language and hearing assessment. |
| Stops using well-established words or gestures. | Should not be explained away as bilingualism. | Contact a healthcare professional without lengthy delay. |

If the child loses previously established skills, see [our guide to skill loss](../child-loss-of-acquired-skills/). Do not delay care to prepare records.

## 9. What bilingual families should avoid

- Do not forbid a familiar family language without a compelling individual reason.
- Do not require the child to translate every word.
- Do not compare the child only with monolingual peers.
- Do not correct each mixed-language phrase when the message is clear.
- Do not postpone evaluation of persistent difficulties by saying "it is only bilingualism".
- Do not withhold food, water, toilet access, help or rest to elicit speech.
- Do not use a homemade vocabulary list as a diagnostic scale.
- Do not assume that English, Kazakh or Russian is inherently therapeutic for autistic children.

## 11. Frequently asked questions

### "Can mum use Russian and dad use Kazakh?"

Yes, when that arrangement is natural for the family. There is no required "one adult, one language" system. Responsive and enjoyable communication is more important than a rigid schedule.

### "My child answers a Kazakh question in Russian. Should I correct them?"

Not necessarily. Respond to the message first. An adult may model a Kazakh response naturally without requiring the child to repeat or translate it.

### "A therapist tells us to stop using Kazakh. What should we ask?"

Ask what assessments and research support that recommendation, whether all languages were evaluated, how the child's relationship with relatives was considered, and whether communication support can be provided without excluding the home language. Seek an appropriately qualified second opinion if needed.

### "What if my child speaks very little?"

Continue natural family communication and support gestures, pictures and AAC. But do not defer professional speech-language and medical assessment if you are worried about understanding, speech or hearing.

### "Can picture cards and apps help a child learn Kazakh?"

They may be useful additions if accessible and enjoyable. They cannot replace live interaction and should not be presented as a proven treatment or obligatory programme.

## 12. Evidence limitations and practical conclusion

ASHA, AAP and RCSLT support keeping home languages and assessing multilingual children in all relevant languages. Reviews of autistic children do not demonstrate a general bilingual disadvantage, but evidence is especially thin for complex communication needs and specific language pairs.

**Families do not need to choose between their home language and developmental support.** Speak in languages you use comfortably, notice all accessible communication and seek professional advice when needed. Language should connect a child to family and daily life, not become a continual two-language examination.
