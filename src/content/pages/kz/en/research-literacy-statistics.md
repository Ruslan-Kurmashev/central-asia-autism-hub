## 1. Why should parents understand the numbers in a research paper?

A headline saying that a measure improved by 50% sounds impressive. But it could describe a change from 2 to 3 arbitrary scale points or an increase in a proportion from 20% to 30%. Without a baseline value, a method of measurement and a comparison, the percentage does not tell us what happened.

All the figures in this article are made up for educational purposes. They do not describe the actual effectiveness of autism support.

## 2. The most important word: denominator

Suppose ten of twenty participants could independently communicate that they needed a break in one educational situation. That is 10/20, or 50%. But if only twenty of a hundred invited families took part, it would be incorrect to say that half of all families benefited.

We also need to know what counted as one opportunity: a single question from an adult, a whole day or a single school transition. Different denominators tell different stories.

## 3. Percentages and absolute numbers

Imagine two fictional groups of 100 people each, in which an event occurred in 10 and 5 participants, respectively.

| Illustrative group | Event | Out of 100 | Absolute frequency |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | Recorded | 10 | 10% |
| B | Recorded | 5 | 5% |

The difference is 5 percentage points. Compared with the first group's frequency, the second group's is half as high, a relative decrease of 50%. But this does not mean that the event was prevented in 50 people out of 100. The difference concerns only five out of a hundred.

Without describing the outcome, time period and method of comparison, neither percentage can be interpreted as the effect of an intervention.

## 4. Relative risk and odds ratio

Relative risk (RR) compares the probabilities of an event in two groups over a defined period. In the illustrative table, RR for group B relative to group A = 0.05/0.10 = 0.5.

The odds ratio (OR) is calculated differently. When the event is common, OR may differ substantially from RR. Therefore, OR = 2 cannot be read as "the probability is twice as high" without further conditions.

Parents will find it more useful to see the absolute numbers, follow-up period and relative estimate together.

## 5. What is a mean?

If three pupils ask for help 1, 2 and 9 times, the mean is 4. But most participants do not have a result of 4. The mean can conceal differences, especially in small groups or when there are outliers.

The median here is 2. Authors should choose an appropriate way to describe the distribution and report how much the results varied between people.

## 6. Effect size: how large is the difference?

Statistical significance is not the same as the magnitude of change. Effect size describes the size of a difference or association. It can be expressed as a difference between means, a risk ratio or a standardised difference (*g*, *d*).

For instance, a standardised effect size of 0.3 does not mean a 30% improvement. Its interpretation depends on the scale, spread of results and research context. There is no universal threshold at which the same effect automatically becomes important for every autistic person.

## 7. Understanding uncertainty in a diagram

![AutismHub's educational graph of point estimates and wider or narrower uncertainty intervals. The values are illustrative and do not represent autism therapy.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/research/research-literacy-statistics-diagram-en.svg)

*The point represents the estimate from a sample and the horizontal line represents its confidence interval. The interval shows the precision of the estimate, not a promised result.*

A paper might report a difference between means of 3 points, with a 95% confidence interval from 1 to 5. Under the statistical assumptions made, the observations are compatible with a range of values. The length of the interval conveys the precision of the estimate.

The precise interpretation of a 95% CI is that, if the study were repeated many times and the assumptions held, the interval construction procedure would cover the true value in approximately 95% of those repetitions. In the usual frequentist framework, it is not correct to say that the already calculated interval has a 95% probability of containing a fixed true value.

## 8. What does a p-value mean?

If a model assumes that the effect under consideration is absent and all the model's assumptions hold, the p-value describes the probability of obtaining data at least as extreme as those observed, according to that model.

It is not the probability that the hypothesis is true, the probability that the data occurred by chance or the percentage of children who will benefit from a programme. The American Statistical Association stresses that scientific decisions cannot be made using a single threshold of p = 0.05.

## 9. Why p less than 0.05 does not mean practical benefit

In a very large study, even an extremely small difference can have a low p-value. In a small sample, a substantial difference may be accompanied by a wide interval and statistical uncertainty.

It is more useful to ask how noticeable the effect is in real life, whether participation improved, whether there were harms and how reliable the evidence is.

## 10. A statistically non-significant finding does not prove there is no effect

If p = 0.12, this does not mean that the probability of no effect is 88%, or that there is definitely no effect. There may be too little data, imprecise measurements or genuinely no convincing difference.

Look at the effect size, confidence interval, study design and prespecified outcomes. Sometimes the correct conclusion is that the study does not allow us to distinguish these possibilities with confidence.

## 11. Why participant dropout matters

A study began with 100 people, but only 60 completed the final questionnaire. If the answers of the forty people who are missing differ from those who remained, the estimates may be biased.

Find out how many people took part at the start, how many completed the study, which data were missing and how the authors dealt with them.

## 12. How to read a result in half a minute

1. Find the original counts and the denominator.
2. Establish what was measured and after how long.
3. Look at the absolute difference and effect size.
4. Examine the confidence interval and potential errors.
5. Ask who the change matters to and whether there were adverse consequences.
6. Do not draw a conclusion from an asterisk or a p-value alone.

Conclusion: statistics help us describe uncertainty honestly. The more transparent the numbers and methods, the lower the risk of mistaking an impressive headline for a reliable conclusion.
