Augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) includes ways of communicating that supplement or replace speech when speech alone is not enough for everyday communication. AAC may include gestures and manual signs, pictures and symbols, communication boards, writing, applications and speech-generating devices.

**Augmentative communication** supports someone who already uses some speech. **Alternative communication** provides a way to communicate when speech is absent or cannot reliably convey a message. A person may use several methods together.

## Accessible communication is not a reward for completing a task

AAC helps people express wishes, refuse, report pain, ask questions and share ideas. Access should not be restricted to secure compliance or made conditional on "good behaviour".

[ASHA](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/professional-issues/augmentative-and-alternative-communication/) states that there are **no mandatory age, intelligence or preliminary language thresholds** for considering AAC. Systems are selected individually, taking account of the person, family and everyday circumstances.

Being able to request an object is only one goal. The person also needs a way to say "no", report pain, ask for help and take part in conversation. If AAC is available only during sessions, adults ignore messages or the selected method causes significant distress, the approach should be reviewed.

## Who may benefit from AAC?

AAC should be considered when a person cannot reliably express needs, choose between options, refuse, ask for help or a break, convey emotions, ask questions, describe events or communicate with others.

**Having some speech does not rule out AAC.** A child may speak at home but use symbols at a noisy school, during sensory overload, at a medical appointment or with unfamiliar people. AAC can also help when speech is difficult for others to understand.

## Does someone need to be "ready" for AAC first?

No. There is no requirement to demonstrate a particular level of intelligence, pointing, picture use, understanding of cause and effect or speech before AAC is introduced.

ASHA recommends a *zero-exclusion approach*. When existing ways to communicate do not meet a person's needs, AAC can be considered regardless of age, cognitive profile or reason for communication difficulties.

AAC should not be treated as a last resort after unsuccessful speech therapy.

## Does AAC interfere with speech development?

Research has not shown that AAC prevents the development of spoken language. A 2021 systematic review included 25 single-case studies and three group studies. Overall, AAC use was associated with improvements in spoken language, though speech improvements generally did not exceed gains in AAC use itself.

This does not mean a device will necessarily make a child start speaking. **AAC's primary purpose is to provide communication access now**, rather than delaying it out of fear it might impede speech.

## Types of AAC

### Unaided AAC

The person communicates without a separate tool, for example through natural gestures, facial expressions, body movement, manual signs or fingerspelling.

### Low-tech AAC

Non-electronic tools are used, such as photographs, symbols, cards, communication boards and books or written words.

### High-tech AAC

Electronic tools include communication applications on a tablet or phone, text-to-speech software and dedicated speech-generating devices.

People may combine approaches according to the situation and the communication partner. An electronic device is not automatically better than cards, and cards are not always simpler to use.

## Which AAC approach is best?

**There is no universally best system.** PECS is not a required step before a speech-generating device. Some people use an application at school, gestures at home, text in online conversation and a communication board where an electronic device is impractical.

The right system is not necessarily the most expensive or popular. It is the one that supports a particular person in the situations that matter to them.

## How is an AAC system selected?

Assessment begins with **how the person already communicates and what messages they need to convey**. A diagnosis alone does not determine the system.

Consider several areas:

- **Communication and language.** Understanding of spoken language, existing speech, reading and writing, needed vocabulary, family language and other languages used.
- **Access.** Vision, hearing, movement, how the person selects symbols and sensory characteristics.
- **Everyday life.** Communication tasks at home, school, medical appointments and other important places.
- **Preferences and partners.** The user's interests and whether other people are prepared to understand and support different forms of communication.

ASHA describes AAC assessment as an ongoing process. Trying more than one approach before making a final decision can be useful.

## AAC must remain available

AAC is a means of communication, not a reward for completing an exercise. It must remain accessible whenever the person has something important to say, including outside a therapy session.

A device should not be kept exclusively by the professional, removed as punishment, withheld until the person speaks a word or permitted only after finishing an assignment. When a primary electronic system is temporarily unavailable, another accessible communication option should ideally be provided.

## Communication partners matter

AAC outcomes depend on more than the user. Parents, educators and professionals can make interaction easier by allowing response time, recognising gestures, respecting refusal, demonstrating symbols without pressure and not overwhelming the person with questions.

Create opportunities for **self-initiated communication**, not only repetition after an adult. If a system works only with one professional in a clinic, the skill may not carry over into everyday life.

[Read more about communication partner training](/kz/en/parents/communication-partner-training/)

## Generalisation matters

Choosing the correct symbol during a session is not necessarily independent communication. A child might select "BREAK" after a professional's instruction but fail to use it when genuinely tired or overwhelmed.

Check whether the person can use AAC **without constant prompting**, with different people, at home and school and in unfamiliar situations. Use beyond the session is an important measure of practical value.

## Problems may involve more than the device

AAC as a communication approach is generally not a high-risk intervention. An unsuitable system or poorly organised support can nevertheless obstruct communication.

For example, a device may be repeatedly unavailable, the vocabulary may only include requests for food, there may be no way to say "no" or "it hurts", or adults may use too many prompts. Difficulties also arise when a person lacks vocabulary for personal interests or has a device removed for "bad behaviour".

When reviewing AAC, ask not only **which application is installed**, but also **whether the person can use it to express thoughts and needs independently**.

## How to know whether AAC helps

Before introducing or changing a system, agree on **1-3 goals** that matter to its user. Examples include independently requesting help or a break, refusing, reporting pain, asking questions, commenting or talking to peers.

First record how the person currently communicates. Then observe independent messages, the number of prompts needed, the range of communication purposes and use with different people at home, school and elsewhere. Also consider whether AAC is convenient and whether the approach creates substantial discomfort.

**Do not reduce the outcome to button presses or the percentage of correct answers in a session.** The key question is whether communication has become more accessible, varied and independent in everyday life.

## When should an AAC system be reviewed?

Needs change with age, experience and circumstances. Review the system if it is too slow, inconvenient in school or other important settings, frequently unavailable, or no longer offers words that reflect the person's interests and activities.

Changes in vision, hearing, movement, reading or writing, the availability of easier access methods and persistent device avoidance may also prompt review. If someone avoids the device, first explore why rather than forcing its use.

**AAC is not a once-and-for-all choice.** Vocabulary, access methods, equipment and the organisation of support may need to change.

## Questions to ask the professional

1. **Goal and system selection.** What communication need are we addressing, why this method and which alternatives were considered?
2. **Accessibility.** How were vision, hearing, movement, sensory needs and independent access to symbols taken into account?
3. **Vocabulary.** Can the user refuse, report pain, ask questions, comment, express emotions and discuss personal interests?
4. **Outside sessions.** How will home and school support AAC, and will it remain available throughout the day?
5. **Monitoring and review.** What outcomes will be tracked, how will prompts decrease and when will the plan be revisited?

## Warning signs

Be cautious if someone says a child is "too young", has "insufficient intelligence", must master cards first or will stop developing speech if AAC is used. Such claims are inconsistent with the zero-exclusion approach.

A further concern is when someone proposes removing the device to "encourage speech" or allows it only during therapy. **Access to communication should not depend on compliance or task completion.**

## Considerations in Kazakhstan

AAC may be part of educational, healthcare, psychological or social support, including private services. However, calling someone an **"AAC specialist" does not itself establish qualifications**.

Check foundational education, professional role, experience and competencies relevant to the service. Where a service is regulated, verify the specific requirements applying to the professional and organisation.

**An AAC or PECS course certificate is not a substitute for core professional qualifications, and does not by itself demonstrate the method's effectiveness.** Evaluate the professional's preparation, the goal and the rationale for the proposed support.

## Communication situations

### When understanding spoken language is difficult

The difficulty may involve understanding information rather than producing speech. Assessment of comprehension, shorter and clearer instructions, additional response time, visual supports, written cues and aided language input may help. Suspected hearing loss requires a separate hearing assessment.

### When a person speaks but finds conversation difficult

Difficulties can involve starting a conversation, staying on topic, taking turns, asking for clarification or talking with peers. Support may include speech-language therapy, practice in natural settings, peer participation and changes to communication environments.

The goal should not be to compel someone to mask autistic characteristics to appear more typical.

[Speech and language support in autism](/kz/en/parents/speech-language-therapy/)

### Echolalia and repeated speech

Echolalia may serve a communicative or self-regulatory function. Before trying to change repeated speech, explore when it occurs, what the person may be communicating and whether others understand it.

*Gestalt Language Processing* and *Natural Language Acquisition* should not be equated with echolalia. Claims about fixed NLA stages and a specific treatment protocol still require stronger supporting evidence.

### When speech is difficult to understand

Unclear speech and difficulties understanding or using language are not the same thing. Speech sound disorders, speech motor difficulties, childhood apraxia of speech and other conditions may be involved. Appropriate professional assessment is needed.

AAC can supplement speech when it is not reliable enough for communication.

### Communicating pain or during medical care

It is especially important to be able to say **what hurts and where**, express fear or feeling unwell, request a break and refuse an action. AAC should therefore remain available not only during sessions, but also at home, school and medical appointments.

New loss of communication skills or a sudden change in speech needs assessment of the underlying cause.

## Putting AAC modelling into practice

### Modelling is not a command to imitate

In aided language modelling, an adult speaks while pointing to a few relevant symbols on a board or device. [ASHA](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/professional-issues/augmentative-and-alternative-communication/) describes this as *aided language input*.

For example, an adult says "Let's go outside" while selecting "OUTSIDE". This demonstrates how language can be used. It does not require the child to press the same button immediately. If the child is compelled to copy every example, the activity involves prompting or direct instruction rather than modelling alone.

The person may look at the symbol, respond later, choose another word or not respond at that moment. **The adult's task is to make language accessible, not to demand repetition after each example.**

### What can be modelled?

Do not limit AAC to "want", "give me" and "more". Everyday examples can show how to communicate different intentions:

- **Refusal and choice.** "No", "I don't want to", "stop".
- **Requests for help.** "Help", "I can't do it", "I need a break".
- **Comments and emotions.** "Funny", "big", "I like it", "it hurts", "scary", "tired".
- **Questions and clarification.** "Where?", "Who?", "I don't understand", "again".
- **Self-advocacy.** "Too loud", "I want to leave".

These are examples, not a compulsory vocabulary list. Choose words that reflect the person's age, interests, languages and daily situations.

### The partner must not take away the device

An adult may find it convenient to hold a device while modelling. Nevertheless, a person's main communication system must remain accessible to them. Do not keep it after demonstrating a symbol or make access conditional on practising.

### Parents do not have to become therapists 24/7

AAC modelling can happen during ordinary play, meals, walks, reading, trips or school activities. Every interaction does not need to become a teaching exercise.

**Support should be manageable for the family and leave space for natural conversation and rest.**

### Families using more than one language

In Kazakhstan, a child may hear Kazakh, Russian, English and other languages on the same day. AAC should support the languages needed at home, school and with family members.

Ask how the person communicates with parents, grandparents, peers and educators. An English-only system may not meet these needs. Research on multilingual AAC is still limited, so decisions should reflect actual language environments rather than a default language.

### Must every word be modelled, and how often?

There is no need to select a symbol for every spoken word. When saying "Let's go outside", an adult may model "GO" or "OUTSIDE" if appropriate to the system and situation.

There is no established universal rule to demonstrate a symbol "20 or 100 times". Frequency and intensity should be individualised. Demonstrating a symbol must not become a demand for immediate imitation.

### How to assess AAC modelling

Choose a meaningful goal, such as independently asking for help, refusing, commenting or communicating with different partners. Check whether communication becomes more independent, how many prompts are needed, whether it carries over beyond sessions and whether there is marked discomfort.

The number of symbols demonstrated by adults does not, on its own, prove communication has improved.
