A child starts shouting when a class begins a writing task. It would be easy to assume that the child simply does not want to work. But perhaps the instructions are unclear, the room is too noisy, the child is in pain, or they do not know how to ask for a break. Similar-looking behaviors can have different causes.

**Functional behavior assessment (FBA)** is a structured way to explore these situations. A professional gathers information about what happens before and after the behavior, compares settings, and develops a hypothesis that can be checked against further observations. The findings guide support, which is then evaluated to see whether it actually helps the child.

FBA does not diagnose a condition, and it is not a therapy in itself. Its purpose is to inform decisions rather than label a child as "stubborn" or "manipulative".

## When might FBA be useful, and when might it not be needed?

Consider discussing FBA with a qualified professional if behavior creates a risk of injury, causes significant distress, seriously interferes with communication, learning or everyday life, or is being considered as a target for a specific intervention. First ask who is experiencing difficulty, how, and what the child may be communicating.

Not every unusual action needs to be changed. Rocking, hand movements, repeated sounds and other forms of self-regulation should not be suppressed simply because other people find them unfamiliar. If an action is safe and does not cause meaningful difficulty for the person, intervention may not be necessary.

Assessment should not begin with a label. Statements such as "he does it for attention", "she is manipulating us" or "he just wants to escape work" remain guesses until there is enough evidence. Even after assessment, a proposed function is a working hypothesis that can be revised.

## Check health and environmental factors first

A change in behavior should not automatically be attributed to autism. Pain, constipation, sleep difficulties, medication side effects, anxiety, communication barriers and sensory overload can all affect how a child responds. Sudden or marked changes, especially with signs of illness, warrant medical assessment.

[NICE guideline CG170](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations) recommends considering noise, lighting, social relationships, changes to routines, lack of predictability, possible bullying or abuse, as well as the child's health. It is worth checking whether the child has an accessible way to say "it hurts", "I don't understand", "help", "no" or "I need a break".

For example, if difficulties arise mainly in the school dining room, examine noise, crowding, waiting and peer interactions. If they occur after a sleepless night, consider the child's wellbeing. Changing conditions or addressing pain may be more important than any behavior-training program.

## What happens during a functional behavior assessment?

An FBA brings together several kinds of information.

1. **Describe what can be observed.** Instead of "behaved badly", record something specific, such as "covered their ears, shouted and left the room". Vague labels make comparisons difficult.
2. **Gather relevant background information.** Speak with the child in an accessible way and with parents or teachers. Consider health, past experiences and changes in circumstances. Interviews and questionnaires can generate hypotheses, but they cannot prove a function.
3. **Observe everyday situations.** Look for when the behavior occurs and when it rarely occurs. Record what happened immediately beforehand, what followed, how adults responded and which conditions might be involved.
4. **Establish a baseline.** Depending on the situation, note how often or how long the difficulty occurs, its intensity, effects on daily life and the support needed. Use comparable measures later to evaluate changes.
5. **Develop and review a working hypothesis.** Compare observations and identify possible adjustments to the environment, communication or task. Revise the explanation if the evidence does not support it.

An observation in one clinic room may not capture what happens at home or school. Situations in which the child manages a similar task comfortably are also informative. The difficulty might relate to the length of the task, the noise or the way instructions are given rather than to the task itself.

## The ABC diary: a practical example

ABC records the sequence around an event: *Antecedent* (what happened before), *Behavior* (what the person did) and *Consequence* (what happened afterwards).

Imagine a lengthy writing task in a noisy classroom. The child appears unsure how to begin. An observation might read:

1. **A, before.** The teacher handed out worksheets and asked the class to write independently for 20 minutes. The room was noisy.
2. **B, behavior.** A few minutes later, the child shouted, pushed the sheet away and threw a pencil.
3. **C, afterwards.** An adult paused the task and helped the child move to a quieter place.

This record shows **what happened**, not **why it happened**. The task stopped, the noise decreased and adult assistance became available at the same time. It would be wrong to conclude immediately that the child shouted "to avoid work". Providing a needed break is not automatically a mistake or a reward for "bad behavior".

For a home record, note the date, place, what happened before, the actions you saw, what other people did and how the child's state changed. Describe observable facts rather than assumed motives. Do not create stressful or unsafe situations just to complete a diary entry.

**An ABC diary is not a test that establishes the function of behavior.** Repeated entries may help a professional notice patterns and decide what needs to be investigated next.

## Turning observations into a support plan

Suppose the child has difficulty mainly during lengthy writing tasks in a noisy classroom but participates more comfortably with a short, clear instruction in a quiet room. One possible hypothesis is that noise, task difficulty and a lack of an accessible way to request help are contributing to distress.

This is not a definitive conclusion. A professional must consider other explanations, including health and relationships in the classroom. If further observations are consistent with the hypothesis, support might include a quieter workspace, shorter instructions with an example, an appropriate task level, and a way to ask for help or a break using words, gestures or augmentative and alternative communication (AAC).

[Functional communication training (FCT)](/kz/en/parents/fct-functional-communication-training/) may form part of a support plan. A child should not be required to use one particular card or word if they already communicate their need clearly in another way. An understandable refusal or expression of pain must be acknowledged.

The aim is not simply fewer episodes of shouting. What matters is whether the child feels more comfortable, can communicate their needs, is safer, and can take part in activities that matter to them.

## FBA and functional analysis are not the same

**FBA is the wider assessment process.** It can include interviews, health and educational information, observation, ABC records and patterns across settings. Together, these support a working hypothesis.

**Experimental functional analysis (FA)** is a more specialised procedure. Trained professionals systematically change specific conditions to investigate causal relationships. FA is not required for every FBA and is not something parents should try to reproduce at home.

[Melanson and Fahmie (2023)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36892835/) reviewed 326 studies of experimental functional analysis, reporting 1,333 outcomes from research published between June 2012 and May 2022. This is evidence concerning FA, not proof that every questionnaire, ABC diary or FBA identifies a cause with certainty.

Experimental analysis also has limitations. It may be difficult to recreate everyday conditions in a controlled setting, and some arrangements can be dangerous. Safety, safeguards and a person's ability to stop participating must take priority over collecting more data.

## What does the research show, and what are its limits?

In their [2020 systematic review](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1243786), Jeong and Copeland compared interventions selected using FBA with those selected without such assessment. The review included 24 single-case-design studies. Across the included studies, FBA-informed interventions showed greater reductions in the behaviors being measured.

However, the authors identified important methodological weaknesses. None of the studies met every assessed What Works Clearinghouse quality criterion. It would therefore be misleading to promise that FBA always reveals one correct cause or that a subsequent intervention will definitely work for an individual child.

A function-informed approach can help guide support, but **outcomes depend on the quality of assessment, the goals selected, the person's circumstances and their response**. A reduction in behavior frequency is not a substitute for assessing comfort, communication and quality of life.

## Consent, confidentiality and safety

Before assessment, a family should understand why information is collected, who will observe the child, which records will be kept and who can access them. The child should also receive an accessible explanation where possible, with attention to their wishes, distress and signs of refusal.

The assessment plan should include **stopping criteria**: when to end an observation because of significant distress, danger or deterioration, and when medical attention is needed. Records, videos and health information should be protected appropriately. Permission to film or share a record must not be assumed.

**Parents should not deliberately provoke dangerous behavior at home.** Do not increase noise, keep a child in a painful or frightening situation, remove essential communication supports, withhold necessary help or create conditions that could cause self-injury in order to test a hypothesis. High-risk situations require a professional safety plan.

[NICE NG11](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng11/chapter/recommendations) also gives detailed guidance on functional assessment, but its population is people with learning disabilities (intellectual disabilities) whose behavior challenges. Its recommendations should not automatically be applied to every autistic child. NICE CG170 is directly relevant to the support of autistic children and young people. These are UK guidelines, not binding rules for service provision in Kazakhstan.

## How can you tell whether the assessment helped?

At the end of an FBA, families should receive a clear account of **what was observed, what can and cannot yet be concluded, what changes are proposed and how progress will be assessed**. A statement such as "the function is escape" without evidence or a plan is not enough.

Do not measure success only by how frequently an episode occurs or how long it lasts. Is the child more comfortable and calmer? Can they ask for help, refuse something or say that they are in pain? Have adults changed unsuitable conditions? Can a new communication approach be used at home and school? Have fear, avoidance or additional demands emerged?

Review the plan when difficulties persist, new problems arise, wellbeing deteriorates or the proposed function is not supported by observations. Changes to health, routine, school or communication may also call for reassessment.

## Choosing a professional and considerations in Kazakhstan

In Kazakhstan, functional assessment may be used within psychological, educational, medical or multidisciplinary support. The title "FBA specialist" or a short-course certificate does not by itself demonstrate professional qualifications or legal authority to provide a regulated service. Ask about basic training, professional role, relevant experience, limits of competence and responsibility for medical questions.

Sudden changes in a child's condition or suspected pain should be medically assessed. Communication difficulties may require a speech and language therapist or another appropriately qualified communication professional. When problems occur mainly at school, teachers and relevant support professionals should be involved. Complex or dangerous situations may require coordinated input from several disciplines.

Questions to ask before starting FBA:

1. **What will be assessed, and why?** Why is this behavior a concern, and how are the child's views considered?
2. **Which possible causes have been considered?** Has the team looked at health, communication, sensory conditions and relationships?
3. **What data will be gathered?** Will there be direct observations and comparisons with situations where the behavior does not occur?
4. **How will safety be protected?** Are there stopping criteria and clear rules for storing records?
5. **What will the family receive afterwards?** Which changes are proposed, how will benefit be assessed and when will the hypothesis be reviewed?

Ask further questions if a professional claims to determine a function after a few minutes, treats a single questionnaire as definitive proof, ignores pain or environmental factors, proposes unsafe provocations or defines success solely as compliance.

**The central point:** FBA is useful when it helps identify conditions that make life difficult and guides safer, more accessible support. It is a tool for working together, not for forcing a child to comply.
