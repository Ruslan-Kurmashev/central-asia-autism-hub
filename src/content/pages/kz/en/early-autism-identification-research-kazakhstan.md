## Why does early identification matter for Kazakhstan?

Parents often notice particular aspects of development before they think about the word "autism": how their child communicates, responds, plays, uses gestures and interacts. Some children develop unevenly, and in others changes become apparent later.

Timely professional assessment helps families understand a child's needs, discuss possible explanations for developmental differences and find appropriate support. **Early assessment is not an attempt to diagnose every child with a language delay or unusual behaviour.**

Kazakhstan has published studies of screening tools in local settings. Their results require careful interpretation: **a positive screen and a confirmed autism diagnosis are different outcomes**. Below we examine two studies from Kazakhstan, international research on M-CHAT-R/F accuracy and questions that families commonly face.

## Three stages that should not be confused

| Stage | What happens? | What can be learned? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Developmental observation** | Parents and professionals notice communication, play, movement, learning and daily behaviour | Which concerns should be discussed with a clinician |
| **Screening** | An age-appropriate brief standardised tool, such as M-CHAT-R/F, is used | Whether a child may need further assessment |
| **Professional assessment and diagnosis** | A qualified specialist considers developmental history, observations, behaviour and other relevant information | How to understand the child's needs and whether diagnostic criteria are met |

**No screening questionnaire can replace** a professional assessment or, on its own, confirm or rule out autism.

## What is M-CHAT-R/F?

**M-CHAT-R/F** (*Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers, Revised with Follow-Up*) is a brief two-stage screening instrument based on parents' reports of their child's usual behaviour.

Its primary intended age range is **16-30 months**. It starts with the M-CHAT-R questionnaire and uses an additional Follow-Up interview for certain results.

For families with children aged 16-30 months, AutismHub provides a [first-stage M-CHAT-R page with a result and explanatory guidance](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/en/tools/mchat-r/). The website does **not** conduct the Follow-Up interview. When Follow-Up is indicated, the full process is not complete.

That distinction matters because **initial questionnaire results** and **results after Follow-Up** can differ in their false-positive rates.

### Why does the follow-up interview change the result?

A parent may interpret a question differently from the researcher or recall a rare behaviour rather than what usually happens. The Follow-Up interviewer clarifies the circumstances behind an answer.

A raised score at the initial stage may therefore change after the interview. This does not mean a parent answered incorrectly. The second stage was designed to refine interpretation.

Even when the Follow-Up result remains positive, **not all children are later diagnosed with autism**. Other developmental differences or delays may also warrant attention.

[Official M-CHAT-R/F information](https://www.mchatscreen.com/).

## Study 1. Russian and Kazakh M-CHAT-R/F versions, 2022

In 2022, **Karina Nukeshtayeva, Marina Lyubchenko, Bauyrzhan Omarkulov and Nailya Delellis** published *Modified checklist for autism in toddlers, revised, with follow-up application in Central Kazakhstan* in the *Journal of Clinical Medicine of Kazakhstan*.

Researchers from **Karaganda Medical University and Central Michigan University** examined Russian and Kazakh questionnaire versions in Central Kazakhstan.

### How was the study conducted?

Data were collected in **October-November 2020**. The final analysis included **171 children aged 24-48 months**, with a mean age of 30.75 months.

An important limitation is that **some children were older than the main 16-30 month age range** in which M-CHAT-R/F was originally developed and validated.

The study assessed the **internal consistency** of questionnaire items, rather than independently establishing diagnostic accuracy.

| Measure | Result |
| --- | ---: |
| Participants | **171 children** |
| Low risk under the authors' classification | 141 (82.5%) |
| Medium risk | 23 (13.5%) |
| High risk | 7 (4.1%) |
| Russian version Cronbach's α | **0.87** |
| Kazakh version Cronbach's α | **0.93** |
| Russian version Guttman lambda-6 | 0.94 |

*Source: [Nukeshtayeva et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.23950/jcmk/11574). These figures describe the study group, not the prevalence of autism among all children in Kazakhstan.*

### What is Cronbach's alpha?

If a questionnaire has several items addressing related aspects of behaviour, responses may show a degree of internal consistency. Cronbach's alpha measures a property of the questionnaire items **as a set**, not how accurately a test distinguishes autistic from non-autistic children.

Thus, **α = 0.93 does not mean 93% diagnostic accuracy**. Neither can the results establish that both language versions are fully validated across all ages, regions and clinical settings.

Testing diagnostic accuracy would require independent professional assessment among children with positive and negative results, including analysis of false positives, false negatives, sensitivity and specificity.

### What can we conclude from the 2022 study?

The study is a useful **initial examination** of locally used language versions. It supports further investigation but **does not demonstrate population-wide sensitivity or specificity** of either version in Kazakhstan.

The authors suggested that screening might contribute to earlier identification and lower the age at diagnosis. This is a hypothesis about potential benefits, **not a longitudinal demonstration** that the age of diagnosis actually decreased.

[Read the 2022 study](https://doi.org/10.23950/jcmk/11574).

## Study 2. Screening of 526 children in Karaganda, 2024

In 2024, **Karina Nukeshtayeva and colleagues** from Karaganda Medical University and Central Michigan University published a study in *Clinical Epidemiology and Global Health* on autism-screening risk among children attending psychophysical developmental screening.

### Where and whom did researchers study?

The study was carried out at **three primary healthcare facilities in Karaganda**. Families were invited while visiting general practitioners or paediatric neurologists.

The researchers analysed **526 children**, using M-CHAT-R/F to examine screening risk.

### What were the findings?

The paper's **abstract** describes **27.4% of participants as high risk**, but the detailed table of **initial M-CHAT-R** results separates risk categories:

| Initial screening category | Children | Percentage of 526 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Low risk | **382** | **72.6%** |
| Medium risk | **76** | **14.4%** |
| High risk | **68** | **12.9%** |
| **Medium plus high risk** | **144** | **27.4%** |

Consequently, **27.4% is the sum of medium and high risk at the initial stage**, not the high-risk category alone. The abstract's wording does not accurately reproduce the table's categorisation. The **Follow-Up interview is a separate stage**, and its results should not be mixed with initial risk groups without explanation.

These are **screening outcomes**, not confirmed diagnoses.

They **do not mean** that:

- 27.4% of all Karaganda children are autistic
- 27.4% of all children in Kazakhstan are autistic
- every positive-screening participant subsequently received an ASD diagnosis
- a random sample of children elsewhere would produce the same percentage.

### Why might the proportion be high?

The children were not randomly sampled from the whole population. They attended particular health facilities for developmental screening. This group may already include a higher proportion of families with developmental concerns.

This is **selection bias**, where recruitment influences the observed findings.

The authors also reported associations between screening results and child age, sex, parental age and existing developmental conditions. These are associations with **screening risk**, not proof that those characteristics cause autism.

[Read the 2024 paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cegh.2024.101703).

### Why are 4.1% and 27.4% not automatically contradictory?

The first study included 171 children and the second 526. Age distribution, recruitment, reasons for attending and research aims differed. The stages and definitions of risk categories would also need careful comparison.

**It is incorrect to conclude that autism prevalence in Karaganda rose from 4.1% to 27.4% in two years.** Both figures describe screening outcomes in selected samples, not comparable population prevalence.

## What does international research show about M-CHAT-R/F accuracy?

Local studies are valuable, but they do not replace larger diagnostic-accuracy studies. International meta-analyses help explain the instrument's potential and limitations.

### Sensitivity and specificity

A 2023 *JAMA Pediatrics* meta-analysis by **Wieckowski and colleagues** included **50 studies and 51 samples** across original and revised M-CHAT versions, with different follow-up procedures.

| Measure | Pooled estimate | Plain-language meaning |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Sensitivity | **0.83** (95% CI 0.77-0.88) | The tool identified many, but not all, children later confirmed to have ASD in the studied samples |
| Specificity | **0.94** (95% CI 0.89-0.97) | Many children without ASD screened negative, but false positives were possible |

*Source: [Wieckowski et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2022.5975). These pooled values concern multiple studies and versions, **not demonstrated accuracy in Kazakhstan**.*

**Sensitivity** is the proportion of children with the condition who screened positive. **Specificity** is the proportion without the condition who screened negative.

Performance differed substantially across studies and depended on sampling, diagnostic confirmation, Follow-Up and how children with false-negative screens were investigated. The pooled sensitivity **is not an individual 83% guarantee**.

### How often is a positive screen followed by a confirmed diagnosis?

A separate 2023 meta-analysis by **Aishworiya and colleagues** in *Pediatrics* included **15 studies, 18 samples and 49,841 children** from ten countries.

The pooled **positive predictive value (PPV)** for M-CHAT-R/F was **57.7%** (95% CI 48.6-66.8%).

PPV asks what share of children with a positive screen later have the condition confirmed. **57.7% is not an individual child's probability of receiving an autism diagnosis in Kazakhstan.** PPV depends on the frequency of the condition in the group, pathways to assessment and sampling.

The authors noted limitations in confirming the outcomes of children who screened negative, since many did not undergo a full diagnostic assessment.

[Read Aishworiya et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2022-059393).

## How does early identification work in Kazakhstan's system?

A [2026 Ministry of Health announcement](https://www.gov.kz/org/dsm/press/news/details/1192936?lang=ru) describes a pathway for children aged **16-30 months** using M-CHAT-R. A similar screening age range appears in [Order No. 385 of 31 August 2022 on special education organisations](https://www.gov.kz/memleket/entities/control-almaty/documents/details/442065?lang=ru).

The Ministry announcement uses language suggesting "diagnosis with M-CHAT-R", but according to the authors and intended purpose **M-CHAT-R/F is a screening tool**, not a means of establishing a clinical diagnosis on its own.

The announcement also describes psychiatric diagnosis of ASD from age three in the relevant state pathway. **This description does not mean that children younger than three cannot undergo professional developmental assessment or that support should be postponed.** International clinical guidance recognises that experienced professionals can often assess and diagnose autism reliably around age two.

Parents should clarify the current local referral pathway with a qualified professional, **without delaying discussion or appropriate support because of administrative age criteria**.

## What should a family do after screening?

The following describes general points for discussion with a clinician, not an individual diagnostic pathway or treatment plan.

### If the result is positive

A positive screen **does not establish a diagnosis**. Keep the result and discuss it with a paediatrician or another qualified professional. Ask how to arrange further developmental assessment.

If the child needs communication, play or daily-living support, discuss available options rather than postponing all help until a formal diagnosis is confirmed.

### If the result is negative but concerns remain

A negative result **does not guarantee that autism or another developmental difference is absent**. It reflects one procedure at one stage of development.

If parents are concerned about language, interaction, play or loss of established skills, they should discuss the observations with a professional regardless of questionnaire results.

### If the child is outside the 16-30 month age range

M-CHAT-R/F was designed mainly for toddlers. Some studies used it with older children, but that does not demonstrate unchanged accuracy at any age.

A professional may select a more age-appropriate form of assessment instead of applying the tool outside its intended range.

### What information is useful before a consultation?

Families can record what raised concerns, where the observations occur, what the child does well, preferred ways of communication and what has changed with time.

These notes are **conversation aids**, not an independent risk scale or diagnostic test.

See the AutismHub [home observation guide](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/en/parents/how-to-observe-child-development-at-home/).

## What evidence is still needed from Kazakhstan?

Understanding nationwide screening performance requires studies across regions and languages, not just a few clinics. Important questions include:

- **Language and cultural validity.** Do families interpret Russian and Kazakh questions as intended?
- **Diagnostic accuracy.** How often do both positive and negative screens match an independent professional assessment?
- **Representativeness.** Are children from rural areas and outside specialist services included?
- **Referral pathways.** How long does it take to reach professional assessment after a positive screen?
- **Practical outcomes.** Does screening improve timely access to support, rather than merely increase questionnaire counts?
- **Follow-up over time.** How do results change as children develop?

A diagnostic-accuracy study should ideally ensure that assessors are not influenced by a child's screening result, and should also account for children with negative screens.

**High internal consistency is useful, but is not a substitute for sensitivity, specificity or evidence of benefit to families.**

## What is established and what remains uncertain?

**Known:** research in Kazakhstan has examined Russian and Kazakh M-CHAT-R/F versions, including studies of 171 and 526 children. The Ministry describes use of the instrument in its early identification pathway.

**Supported internationally:** M-CHAT-R/F can help identify children who need further assessment, but performance varies and a positive screen does not establish autism.

**Not established by local studies:** nationally valid sensitivity and specificity for both language versions, the national proportion of positive screens in representative populations or the causal effect of a particular screening pathway on age at support.

**Conclusion:** early identification is useful when results lead to accessible professional assessment and appropriate support. **The purpose of screening is to identify a need to understand development, not to assign a diagnosis from a questionnaire.**
