Everyday life offers many small opportunities for communication. A child may reach for a cup, bring over a toy, turn towards a familiar sound, point at a dog, move a hand away, say a word or select a symbol on a communication device.

Each moment is an opportunity to understand the child better and support their expression of needs, preferences, thoughts and feelings. This does not require a separate flashcard session or scheduled word repetition.

**The central principle is not to demand one particular form of response, but to make communication accessible, understandable and reciprocal.**

If you have concerns about your child's language comprehension or speech, first see [“Speech and language development: what parents should notice”](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/ru/parents/razvitie-rechi-i-ponimaniya-u-rebenka/). This article focuses on everyday interactions, not age-based milestones.

## 1. What does supporting communication mean?

**Communication** is more than speaking words. It includes requesting, refusing, expressing emotions, indicating pain or discomfort, sharing experiences and drawing someone's attention to something interesting.

For example, a child approaches the window, smiles and points to a bird. An adult might respond, “Yes, a bird! It's flying.” That acknowledges the child's initiative. There is no need to immediately test the child with “What colour is it?” or “Say bird.”

In another example, a child pushes their plate away. Before requesting the words “I don't want it,” acknowledge the refusal: “You don't want to eat that.” Depending on the child's age and abilities, you can gently ask whether they would like a break or a different food.

The aim is not to produce as many responses as possible, but to **notice and respond to the child's messages**.

## 2. Seven straightforward principles for interaction

### 1. Notice what interests the child right now

Notice where the child goes, what they play with, examine or listen to. Join an activity that already interests them rather than continually redirecting them to an adult-selected “learning task.”

For example, a child spends time moving pebbles between containers. An adult might sit nearby and calmly comment, “Another pebble” or “You put it here.” If the child does not want company, give them space.

### 2. Respond first, then add language

If the child points at a cup, respond to their message: “Water? Here is some water.” If they say “car,” you might say, “The red car is moving.” Do not require them to repeat the full sentence.

Treat a gesture or a picture as seriously as a spoken word.

### 3. Allow enough time to respond

After speaking or offering a choice, pause naturally. A response may take time and may be nonverbal: moving a hand, looking at an object, using a gesture or picture, saying a word or changing an action.

There is no universal pause length. Consider the child's individual processing time, movement and communication methods.

### 4. Comment more often than you test knowledge

Instead of a stream of questions such as “What's this? What colour? And this?”, describe what is happening: “The ball is rolling,” “You opened the box,” “The dog is running.”

Questions are not necessarily harmful. They can be useful when the child is interested, understands them and has an accessible way to answer. The point is to avoid turning conversation into a continuous examination.

### 5. Offer genuine choices

“Would you like an apple or a banana?” can be accompanied by showing the items, symbols or gestures. Accept any clear, accessible choice.

The choice must be real. Do not withhold water, food, help, rest or toilet access until a child says a particular word.

### 6. Support different forms of communication

Some children combine speech, gestures, pictures, written words or augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) devices.

If a child selects “more” on a device, an adult may answer, “More music!” and continue the song when possible. Do not require the spoken word as an additional condition.

There are no universal prerequisite skills for AAC. For complex needs, consult a speech-language therapist or an AAC specialist about selecting methods and devices.

### 7. Consider fatigue, noise and the right to refuse

Communication can be more difficult when a child is tired, overwhelmed by noise or surrounded by unfamiliar people. Sometimes reducing demands, providing quiet and allowing time is the most useful support.

A child has the right not to join a game, not to answer a question and to refuse. Eye contact should not be demanded as proof of attention.

## 3. Examples during everyday family routines

These ideas can be adapted to the child's age and abilities. They are not individual prescriptions or mandatory sequences of exercises.

| Situation | How an adult might support communication | Responses that can be accepted |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Breakfast | “Would you like porridge or yoghurt?”, while showing available options. | Pointing, a gesture, looking towards the food, a picture or a word. |
| Getting dressed | “First socks, then shoes,” with a short explanation while acting. | Extending a foot, choosing clothing, asking for help or a break. |
| Playing with toy cars | “The car is moving,” “It stopped!”, following the child's interest. | Moving the car, smiling, gesturing or making a sound. |
| Going for a walk | “Look, a bus,” if the child has noticed it. | Stopping, pointing, commenting or simply looking. |
| Bath time | “Is the water comfortable?” “Want another toy?”, respecting comfort and consent. | Choosing a toy, pulling away, indicating “more” or “enough.” |
| Shared reading | Naming chosen pictures, pausing and letting the child turn pages. | Pointing, turning pages, making a sound or saying a word. |
| Bedtime | Describing familiar steps, with pictures if helpful. | Choosing a book, asking for a hug or quiet time. |

### If the child is not yet using spoken words

Use short, clear language with familiar objects, gestures or pictures, then **respond to the message** the child is already able to communicate.

For example, a child brings an empty water bottle. An adult says “Water,” fills the bottle and returns it. Water should not be withheld until the child repeats the word.

### If the child uses individual words

When a child says “ball,” the adult might respond “Big ball” or “The ball is bouncing” without demanding repetition. This models additional language in a meaningful situation.

### If the child speaks in phrases

You can talk about what interests the child: “You noticed a building crane. What is it lifting?” If questions tire the child, simple comments or observing together are enough.

For a child who uses more complex language, discuss not only object names but also their interests, experiences, needs and questions without turning everything into a lesson.

## 4. What if the child does not respond?

There is no single explanation for a lack of response. The child may not have heard, understood or wanted to answer. They may be busy, in pain or experiencing sensory overload. They may need more time or a different response method.

A calm sequence to try:

1. Check that the child is comfortable and has no urgent needs.
2. Use shorter, clearer language and show an object or picture if helpful.
3. Pause and offer an accessible way to respond.
4. Accept any understandable response, including refusal.
5. If communication is difficult now, reduce demands and try again at a better time.

Do not repeatedly call the child's name, turn their face towards you by touching them or hold back a needed object to provoke a response.

If a child frequently does not respond to spoken language, or you are concerned about comprehension, discuss hearing and speech-language assessment with a professional.

## 5. If the child uses AAC or pictures

Visual supports and AAC may help a child express needs, make choices, comment on events and participate in conversation. AAC is not limited to requesting cards and should not be used solely as a way to “earn” access to objects.

Practical ideas:

- Keep the communication method accessible wherever the child needs it in everyday life.
- Model how to communicate “more,” “finished,” “help,” “I don't want that” or an interesting observation.
- Respond to the message rather than insisting on switching to speech.
- Ensure the child can communicate refusal, discomfort, the need for a break and a request for help.
- Together with a professional, check whether the system suits the child's motor, visual, language and sensory needs.

See also [“Communication and AAC”](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/ru/parents/kommunikatsiya-i-aac/) and [“Communication partner training”](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/ru/parents/communication-partner-training/).

## 6. Bilingual families: which language should we use?

If a family speaks both Russian and Kazakh, this is not in itself a reason to abandon either language. The child should be able to communicate with loved ones in the languages familiar to the family.

An adult can use the language they speak comfortably, name objects and actions during familiar routines and read favourite books. A child may use words from different languages within the same conversation.

Tell professionals about **all languages** the child hears and uses. Knowing a particular word only in one language is not, by itself, evidence of a delay.

See [“Speech and language development”](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/ru/parents/razvitie-rechi-i-ponimaniya-u-rebenka/) for more about development and languages.

## 7. Choose one helpful step for this week

There is no need to attempt every suggestion. Choose one familiar, relaxed situation that the child already enjoys.

For example:

**Situation:** The child enjoys building a toy railway.

**What the adult will try:** Join for a few minutes, comment on an interesting action and allow time for a response.

**What to notice:** Whether the child invites participation, shows an object, requests that play continue or refuses, and what conditions help them feel comfortable.

**What is not the goal:** Making the child say a set number of words, maintain eye contact or follow a series of instructions.

Later, the family can discuss what helped and what got in the way. Even without new words, it may become easier for a child to communicate a request, refusal or interest. This observation alone does not demonstrate that the strategy caused a change or was effective.

## 8. A brief guide for parents

| Instead of this | Try this |
| --- | --- |
| “Say it properly or you won't get it.” | Respond to an accessible request and calmly model the word if useful. |
| “Look me in the eyes.” | Remember that a child may listen and participate without eye contact. |
| “What's that? And that? And that?” | Comment on what already interests the child. |
| “You're ignoring me.” | Consider the environment, understanding, hearing, demands and available response methods. |
| “Pictures stop children from talking.” | Consider AAC as a way to broaden communication, involving a specialist if necessary. |
| “We must practise all day.” | Use a few natural communication opportunities without overwhelming the family. |

## 9. When home support is not enough

Talk to a paediatrician and an appropriate speech-language professional if you are concerned about the child's comprehension, communication, hearing or development of ways to express messages.

Do not delay an assessment, particularly if:

- The child has lost previously established words, gestures or other skills.
- The child seems not to hear speech or certain sounds.
- It is hard for the child to communicate pain, discomfort or basic needs.
- Communication difficulties substantially affect everyday life.
- Parents or teachers notice lasting changes that raise concerns.

Sudden major changes in a child's condition or other acute medical symptoms require timely, and sometimes urgent, medical attention.

## 10. What guidelines support, and what they do not prove

WHO, UNICEF and ASHA resources support responsive communication. WHO Caregiver Skills Training uses family routines and play as opportunities for learning and participation for children with developmental delays or disabilities. ASHA describes approaches families can discuss with professionals, including waiting, commenting, following the child's interests and offering natural choices.

However, parent-focused programmes and coaching approaches have not all been researched to the same extent. **Using these ideas alone cannot guarantee the development of spoken language or a particular outcome by a particular date.** When targeted speech-language intervention is needed, agree on an approach with a qualified professional, taking the child's goals, preferences and family context into account.

This article is not the WHO CST programme, an individual clinical recommendation or a substitute for speech-language and medical assessment.

## Key takeaways

The main resource for communication is not a special toy or exercise kit. It is a way for the child to express a message and receive a clear, respectful response.

**Notice an initiative -> respond to the message -> add understandable words or visual support -> allow time -> accept any accessible response.**

Support the child's ability to communicate needs, share interests and refuse. Everyday family interaction should help the child and family, not turn ordinary life into constant testing.
