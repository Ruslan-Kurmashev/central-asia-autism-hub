## Is there one cause of autism?

No. Autism is related to brain development and encompasses a diverse range of characteristics. Research indicates that different genetic and biological factors can influence the likelihood of autism, often in different combinations. For most people, it is not possible to identify a single event, gene or circumstance that "caused autism" (WHO, 2025, MedlinePlus Genetics).

Parents often ask about causes after an assessment or diagnosis. It helps to distinguish two concepts. A **cause** explains why a condition develops, whereas a **risk factor** means only that a characteristic or circumstance is statistically associated with a greater likelihood of that condition. These are not the same.

## What role does genetics play?

Genetic factors play a substantial role. Researchers study many genetic variants involved in the early formation and functioning of the nervous system, including the development of neurons and their connections. Often, the picture involves many variants acting together, each of which may have only a small individual effect.

In some cases, autism occurs alongside a particular genetic syndrome. In other cases, no specific genetic cause can be identified even when an autism diagnosis is well established. This does not make the diagnosis any less valid (MedlinePlus Genetics).

**Heredity does not mean inevitability.** Having an autistic family member may increase the likelihood of autism being identified in other relatives, but it cannot predict the development of any particular child. Some genetic changes also occur for the first time rather than being inherited from either parent.

## What do we know about pregnancy and early development?

Observational studies have associated autism likelihood with factors including older parental age, certain pregnancy or birth complications, premature birth and some maternal health conditions. However, these associations cannot be treated as explanations for an individual case. Many children with those experiences are not autistic, while many autistic children had none of those factors (WHO, 2025, CDC, 2026).

**How a study was conducted** also matters. An observed association might be partly explained by inherited factors, other accompanying circumstances or differences in how data were collected. Two things occurring together do not necessarily mean that one caused the other.

## What should not be considered a cause?

- **Parenting and family relationships.** Autism is not caused by a lack of love or by strict or permissive parenting.
- **Vaccines.** In December 2025, after reviewing studies published through August 2025, the World Health Organization reaffirmed that childhood vaccines do not cause autism.
- **A particular food.** Scientific evidence does not support the claim that autism is caused by a specific food or an ordinary diet (WHO, 2025).

A common mistake is to attribute newly noticeable developmental differences to an event that happened shortly beforehand. The order in which events occur is not, by itself, evidence of causation.

## Can the cause be identified in an individual child?

Sometimes an assessment identifies a specific genetic condition, but this does not happen for every child. Whether genetic counselling or testing may be appropriate should be discussed with a qualified professional in light of developmental history, medical information and any co-occurring characteristics. There is no universal genetic test that can confirm or rule out autism in every child.

Autism diagnosis relies primarily on a comprehensive assessment of development, communication and behaviour. Finding or not finding a specific genetic cause does not replace that assessment.

## What is helpful for families to know?

After a diagnosis, understanding the **child's individual support needs** matters more than looking for someone to blame. In everyday life, it is more useful to discuss accessible ways of communicating, learning preferences, sensory needs and conditions in which the child feels more comfortable.

Families may have different questions about heredity, pregnancy and health. These can be discussed with a qualified clinician without assuming that parents should have prevented something.

## What remains unknown?

Scientists continue to investigate interactions between genetic variants and factors affecting early development. Not every identified association is causal, and findings from one population may not apply fully to another. This article explains the current state of scientific knowledge, but cannot identify the cause of autism in a particular person.
