## 1. Why do scientists use different types of study?

To find out how many people have a particular need, researchers do not necessarily need to conduct an experiment. To understand how development changes with age, they need to observe people over time. To test whether a programme actually leads to improvement, a suitable comparison is useful.

The right design depends on the question. Scientific papers cannot therefore be ranked on a single universal ladder from good to bad.

## 3. Cross-sectional study: a snapshot in time

Imagine that in October researchers surveyed 400 families about educational conditions. They can describe the responses of the families surveyed at that particular time. Some responses might indicate that schools lack quiet spaces.

But if the questionnaire was circulated in a closed parent group, respondents may differ from families across Kazakhstan. A cross-sectional design often cannot establish what came first: an environmental adjustment or a change in participation.

## 4. Cohort study: observation over time

Researchers might follow a group of children from the ages of two to six, regularly recording how they communicate and participate in daily life. This is one way to study individual developmental trajectories.

A cohort study is not necessarily an experiment. Families choose their own schools, clinics and living conditions. Differences between them may affect the results.

A real example: Hviid and colleagues (2019) used large national registries to examine the association between MMR vaccination and autism. It was a cohort study, not an RCT. Researchers did not allocate children to receive or not receive vaccination. An overview of the study is available in [AutismHub's research explainers (in Russian)](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/ru/research/topics/research-reviews/).

## 5. Case-control study: starting with the outcome

Suppose scientists want to study a rare condition. They first select a group of people with the condition (cases) and a comparable group without it (controls), then investigate previous exposures or characteristics.

This design can be efficient for rare outcomes. However, how the controls are selected is particularly important. Participants' memories and records of past events may be incomplete. A difference between the groups does not in itself prove that the characteristic in question caused the outcome.

## 6. Randomised controlled trial (RCT)

In an RCT, individuals (or sometimes classes and schools) are allocated at random to the acceptable conditions being compared. This reduces systematic differences between groups on average, especially when the sample is sufficiently large and the procedure is carried out properly.

Imagine two safe forms of school adjustment. Researchers specify measures in advance, such as a pupil's participation, ability to express refusal and comfort. They compare outcomes after a defined period.

Randomisation and blinding are not the same. Randomisation concerns how groups are assigned. Blinding means that particular participants or assessors do not know which condition has been assigned. In educational research, families often know which support they receive, so complete blinding may not be possible. That does not make an RCT useless, but additional measures are needed to reduce assessment bias.

## 7. Why an RCT does not guarantee the truth

Even with random allocation, participants may drop out, protocols may not be followed, measurements may be inaccurate or only favourable outcomes may be reported. If the person delivering the sessions is also the only assessor, their expectations may influence the scores.

Conversely, research without an RCT may be strong when the question concerns population prevalence, rare adverse effects or the lived experiences of autistic people.

## 8. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses

A systematic review specifies the question, search methods and eligibility criteria in advance, then assesses the limitations of the included studies. If studies differ substantially, the review may conclude with a narrative synthesis.

A meta-analysis is the statistical combination of compatible estimates. A scientific journal may publish a meta-analysis, but combining studies with very different participants and outcomes without justification can produce a misleading pooled figure, however impressive it looks.

A paper containing the word "meta-analysis" is not necessarily better than any well-conducted primary study.

## 9. CONSORT and STROBE reporting standards

CONSORT 2025 helps authors report randomised trials fully. STROBE addresses reporting of observational studies. These documents help readers identify missing information, but their checklists do not certify effectiveness or serve as risk-of-bias assessments.

## 10. Matching the research design to the question

| Question | A reasonable starting point |
| --- | --- |
| How often do families report barriers to access? | A representative cross-sectional study |
| How do communication skills develop at different ages? | A longitudinal or cohort study |
| Did a particular intervention have an effect? | A well-designed comparative study, often an RCT |
| What experiences and needs do autistic adults describe? | Qualitative interviews with transparent methods |
| What do all high-quality studies on the question show? | A systematic review |

Qualitative interviews address questions about meaning and experience. They should not be dismissed as unimportant simply because they do not estimate a numerical effect.

## 11. Checking whether findings apply to Kazakhstan

Even a large study conducted abroad may not take account of local languages, AAC devices, access to services, curricula or ways of registering diagnoses. Before applying a finding, ask where the study took place, who participated, how the outcome was measured and whether the conditions resemble our own.

## 12. Three exercises for the reader

1. Researchers surveyed 600 parents in just one online chat. What can you say about representativeness?
2. Researchers followed a group of schoolchildren for eight years. How does this differ from an experiment?
3. Researchers combined published studies. Was the search systematic, and were there sufficient grounds for performing a meta-analysis?

The main point: the name of a study design indicates what kind of question the research can answer. It does not remove the need to examine its methods and limitations.
