## What research tells us about the challenges families face

Discussions of autism often focus on assessment, communication differences and ways to support a child. Families also need answers to practical questions: how long does it take to find a specialist, what expenses will arise, can parents combine paid work with caregiving, and what support is available at school?

Research carried out in Kazakhstan allows these questions to be discussed using evidence rather than assumptions. Some studies surveyed hundreds of parents, while others used in-depth interviews. Several themes recur: **access to specialists, costs, employment, education, psychological support and a lack of clear information**.

None of the surveys discussed here represents every family raising an autistic child in Kazakhstan. Throughout this article, we therefore distinguish **what study participants reported** from **the circumstances of all families nationwide**.

## First, who took part in these studies?

| Study | Participants | Main subject |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An and colleagues, online 2018, journal issue 2020 | 17 parents in two cities | Experiences with healthcare, social and educational services |
| Alibekova and colleagues, 2022 | 146 parents, including 136 complete responses to symptom scales | Stress, anxiety, depressive symptoms and social support |
| Nukeshtayeva and colleagues, 2024 | Parents interviewed by the research team | Time taken and difficulties encountered in obtaining an assessment and diagnosis |
| Akhtayeva and colleagues, published online 2024 | 327 parents and 10 psychologists | Family wellbeing and psychological services |
| Imamatdinova and colleagues, 2025 | 390 parents from several regions | Expenses, employment and access to public and private services |
| Kayupova and colleagues, 2026 | 239 parents in the Karaganda Region | Financial difficulties and associated characteristics |

Different sampling methods reveal different aspects of families' experiences, but **the percentages from these studies cannot simply be pooled**.

## 1. Financial burden: results from a survey of 390 parents

In August 2025, **Aziza Imamatdinova and colleagues** from Kazakhstan published a study in *BMC Public Health*.

The study involved **390 parents** of autistic children from urban and rural areas in different regions of Kazakhstan. The questionnaire was developed by an interdisciplinary team, with contributions from parents.

Three findings deserve attention:

![Chart of responses from 390 parents in Kazakhstan: insufficient state support 73.3%, leaving work 25.4%, changing employment or working hours 18.8%.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/research/kazakhstan-asd-family-support-survey-2025-en.svg)

*Figure 1. Original AutismHub chart based on Imamatdinova and colleagues, 2025. These figures describe one survey sample. They are separate, potentially overlapping responses and must not be added together to make 100%.*

### Why a single government benefit may not cover a family's needs

**73.3% of participants** felt that government financial support was insufficient.

This is **the parents' assessment**, not proof that no assistance existed or that everyone qualified for the same benefit. The responses also do not show precisely which state measures were available to each family.

The researchers described additional expenses for education, specialist services and support from private organisations. Families wanted clearer information about their rights and available services.

In the study's table, **57.7% of participants reported additional expenses exceeding 100,000 tenge per month**, while 7.4% reported no such expenses. These are self-reported costs in a particular sample at that time, **not a required monthly budget for every autistic child**.

An expensive programme or a high number of sessions **does not guarantee** scientifically demonstrated benefits for a child.

### How family needs can affect employment

According to the survey responses:

- **25.4%** said they had had to leave their job.
- **18.8%** had changed jobs, the kind of work they did or their working hours.
- **63.3%** reported that only one parent in their household was employed.

These answers describe different aspects of family life. Having only one employed parent does not automatically mean that the other parent stopped working solely because of the child's support needs.

Lost earnings and additional expenses may reinforce one another. However, the study was **cross-sectional** and cannot establish how much any specific event caused a change in income.

[Read the original BMC Public Health study](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-025-23764-3).

## 2. A newer 2026 study: almost 91% reported financial difficulties

In August 2026, **Gaukhar Kayupova, Aigul Muzafarova, Nailya DeLellis, Karina Nukeshtayeva and Marina Lyubchenko** published a study of financial burden in the Karaganda Region in *Frontiers in Public Health*.

The researchers analysed responses from **239 parents** of children under 18, collected in 2023 through a regional psychiatric dispensary. Of 285 questionnaires distributed, 239 were included in the analysis.

| Finding | Survey participants |
| --- | ---: |
| Reported financial difficulties | **90.79%** |
| Reduced working hours | 30.13% |
| Left employment | 21.76% |
| Said they were not receiving government financial support | 64.02% |

*Source: [Kayupova and colleagues, 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1846395). The responses describe this sample, not all parents in Kazakhstan.*

### Why we cannot say that 91% of Kazakhstani families experience financial hardship

There are three important limitations.

First, the study took place in **one region**, with participants recruited among accessible families connected to the healthcare system.

Second, **90.79% of participants lived in urban areas**, making generalisation to rural communities particularly difficult.

Third, financial difficulty was assessed by a specially designed yes/no question. The adapted questionnaire had not undergone a full formal assessment of reliability and validity.

The accurate statement is therefore: **approximately nine in ten parents surveyed in this Karaganda Region study reported financial difficulties**.

### What differences between urban and rural areas mean

The authors identified a statistical association between urban residence and reports of financial difficulty. In one model, the odds ratio was **6.30**, but the confidence interval was wide: **1.42-27.95**.

This **does not mean** that moving to a city causes financial difficulties to become 6.3 times more likely. The observed association may reflect differences in access to paid services, income, decisions to seek support and other family characteristics.

Only **22 families** were included in the rural subgroup, which adds considerable uncertainty to such comparisons.

[Read the 2026 publication](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1846395).

## 3. Why obtaining a diagnosis can take years

In 2024, **Karina Nukeshtayeva and colleagues** published a study of parents' experiences of autism diagnosis in *Astana Medical Journal*.

The reported mean ages in the study group were:

| Stage | Child's mean age |
| --- | ---: |
| Parents first noticed differences that concerned them | **1.91 years** |
| First request for professional help | **2.19 years** |
| Diagnosis received | **4.51 years** |

*Source: [Nukeshtayeva and colleagues, 2024](https://doi.org/10.54500/2790-1203-2024-3-122-19-26). These are figures for study respondents, not official nationwide averages for the age of diagnosis.*

Importantly, **the precise average waiting time for each child cannot be calculated simply by subtracting these mean ages**. That would require information about individual time intervals for participants.

Parents described difficulties obtaining referrals, lack of information and specialists, as well as logistical and psychological barriers.

### What this means for a family

When questions about a child's development arise, it can help to write down specific observations, note the dates of appointments and keep copies of the reports received. This information can inform discussions with a clinician.

**Waiting for a formal diagnosis should not mean postponing every form of available developmental or everyday-life support.** Professional assessment is intended to understand a child's needs, not only to issue a document.

AutismHub has a separate [guide to observing a child's development at home, available in Russian](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/ru/parents/kak-nablyudat-za-razvitiem-rebenka-doma/).

## 4. Education: a school place is not the same as effective support

In 2018, **Sofiya An, Chee Kai Chan and Botagoz Kaukenova**, researchers associated with Nazarbayev University, published a study of the experiences of **17 parents in two major cities**. The journal issue appeared in 2020.

In focus groups, families spoke about difficulties accessing not only medical services but also special and inclusive education, communicating with institutions and finding qualified support.

A study of this kind can identify **recurring themes in parents' experiences**. It cannot tell us what percentage of all schools in Kazakhstan experience any particular problem.

Later researchers continued examining cooperation between healthcare, education and social services. For example, a 2023 conference abstract reported interviews with **60 parents**, who described waiting for public services, needing to use private specialists and wanting accessible educational information.

A conference abstract typically provides less methodological detail than a full journal article. It is useful for identifying research questions, but should not be treated as having undergone the same scrutiny as a large completed study.

[An and colleagues' study of families in Kazakhstan](https://doi.org/10.1080/1034912X.2018.1499879).

### Questions worth asking a school

Families may find it helpful to ask:

- which adaptations to teaching tasks and the school environment are actually available
- how staff identify the child's individual educational needs
- who coordinates communication between the family and teachers
- how support goals are agreed and how changes are monitored
- where written information about support arrangements can be obtained.

**A diagnosis alone does not determine one appropriate form of schooling.** Education decisions should take account of the individual child's abilities, needs and rights.

Practical questions about schools, the PMPK assessment service and support are covered in [Autism and school in Kazakhstan, available in Russian](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/ru/help-kazakhstan/autism-school-kazakhstan/).

## 5. Parents' psychological wellbeing: what research shows

Financial pressures and difficulties obtaining services may coincide with emotional distress. However, it would be wrong to assume that every parent of an autistic child experiences the same stress or needs the same support.

### The 2022 study: 146 parents

**Raushan Alibekova and colleagues** studied **146 parents** of autistic children recruited through organisations and a rehabilitation centre in Astana. **136 complete responses** were available for analysis of psychological symptoms.

The symptom questionnaire captured severity ranging from mild to extremely severe:

| Measure | Among the 136 participants with complete responses |
| --- | ---: |
| Stress symptoms | **61.0%** |
| Anxiety symptoms | 52.9% |
| Depressive symptoms | 53.7% |

*Source: [Alibekova and colleagues, Global Mental Health, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1017/gmh.2022.51). These are self-reported questionnaire symptoms, **not clinical diagnoses** of anxiety or depressive disorders.*

The study also found that parents who perceived less acceptance and support from their community reported psychological difficulties more often. Greater support from friends and being employed were statistically associated with fewer symptoms on some measures.

These are correlational findings. They do not mean that employment or support from friends **will necessarily eliminate stress**, or that unemployment is an established cause of it.

### The 2024 study: 327 parents and 10 psychologists

**Nazgul Akhtayeva and colleagues** surveyed 327 parents and interviewed **10 primary healthcare psychologists**. The paper was first published online in 2024.

The authors examined not only difficulties but also positive aspects of family life and satisfaction with support. They identified a need for better preparation of professionals working with parents of autistic children.

These results should not be interpreted as a measure of the competence of every psychologist in Kazakhstan. The interviews involved only a small group of professionals, and parents were recruited among families using the relevant services.

[Akhtayeva and colleagues, Archives of Medical Science](https://doi.org/10.5114/aoms/190772).

## 6. Why navigating different institutions can be difficult

In everyday life, a child's needs may involve several services at once:

| Area | Example question |
| --- | --- |
| Healthcare | Who assesses developmental differences and any related medical needs? |
| Education | How will adaptations, support and meaningful participation in learning be organised? |
| Social assistance | Which forms of assistance are available to the family when the eligibility conditions are met? |
| Family life | Which everyday activities matter most to the child and parents? |
| Psychological support | How can a parent access professional help when they need it? |

When families have to connect separate decisions themselves, administrative work increases. They need to collect records, find out how services operate, repeat the child's developmental history to different professionals and reconcile recommendations.

Research highlights a need for better coordination, but **does not establish one standard pathway that applies to every region and family**.

The next section therefore offers general ways of organising information, not legal instructions or an individual medical plan.

## Practical steps: using research findings in family decisions

### 1. Make a short map of current needs

There is no need to start with a long list of every available technique. It is more helpful to identify a few real priorities: communication, participation at school, sleep, eating, independence, safety, visiting everyday places and access to support.

You might write down: **"What is currently making daily life difficult? What already helps? Which one or two goals do we want to discuss with a professional?"**

### 2. Sort expenses into necessary, optional and insufficiently supported

A simple list can help show where money is going:

| Category | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Assessments and consultations | Date, specialist, reason for the visit and amount paid |
| Educational support | Service, intended goal and payment period |
| Transport and assistance | Trips, parents' time and additional costs |
| Everyday adaptations | What problem an item is intended to address and whether it is being used |
| Programmes with uncertain evidence | What benefit is promised, whether independent studies exist, risks and price |

This is **a personal budgeting tool**, not a national financial standard or advice to give up necessary services.

It is useful to record not only a session's price but also its purpose: what difficulty it should help address, and how the family and specialist will recognise meaningful improvement.

### 3. Check which public and educational services are available

Before paying for private services, it is worth checking whether comparable or suitable support is available through public healthcare, education or social services.

However, the absence of information on an institution's website **does not prove a service is free**, just as a programme appearing in an official document **does not guarantee immediate access** in a particular city.

For Kazakhstan, consult local services and current rules. AutismHub provides a [Help in Kazakhstan section, available in Russian](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/ru/help-kazakhstan/).

### 4. Do not judge the quality of a service only by its price or name

High prices, persuasive advertising, promises of a "complete recovery" or the label "proprietary method" **are not evidence of effectiveness**.

Before signing an agreement, it may help to ask:

- who will provide the service and what their qualifications are
- which specific needs and goals it addresses
- whether high-quality independent studies support its proposed benefits
- how progress is documented without putting pressure on the child
- how consent, safety and respect for the child's communication are ensured
- whether the price and conditions for ending the service are clear.

See also [What to check before paying for a service in Kazakhstan, available in Russian](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/ru/help-kazakhstan/check-service-before-payment-kazakhstan/).

### 5. Keep essential documents and questions together

You can keep referrals, professional reports, school recommendations, information about prescribed care and the contacts of responsible organisations in one folder.

A brief timeline of appointments helps families remember important questions and discuss changes with a new specialist. **Do not post a child's medical records in public chats, forums or social networks.**

### 6. Include the needs of parents and other family members

A parent's need for rest, emotional support or an opportunity to work does not conflict with caring for their child. Suitable family decisions should consider the needs and real circumstances of everyone involved.

If stress or anxiety symptoms substantially interfere with everyday life, parents can discuss their own support needs with a qualified professional. A research article is not a basis for diagnosing yourself.

## What research can change in support systems

Research does not provide a universal ready-made programme. Instead, it helps frame concrete questions for services and policymakers.

**Financial support.** What share of necessary expenses do families pay themselves? How do costs differ between urban and rural settings?

**Diagnostic pathway.** How long does it take from the first request to a comprehensive assessment, and what prevents timely access?

**Education.** Do children receive the adaptations needed to participate in learning, rather than merely being offered a place in an institution?

**Psychological help.** Can parents access qualified support without excessive financial or administrative barriers?

**Measuring change.** Do access, quality of life and family satisfaction improve following particular organisational reforms?

The studies described here demonstrate why these questions matter. **They do not prove** that any single proposed reform has already improved nationwide outcomes.

## What is established, and what remains unclear

**Supported by the evidence:** several separate studies of parent samples in Kazakhstan repeatedly describe substantial expenses, changed employment, barriers to services and the need for more accessible support.

**Supported with limitations:** some sampled groups reported high levels of financial difficulty and psychological symptoms. These are not national prevalence estimates, but results from selected participants.

**Not established:** the exact proportion of all families in Kazakhstan who lack adequate financial assistance, the average cost of necessary support for an autistic child nationwide, or a causal effect of a particular service on parents' wellbeing.

**Main conclusion:** an effective system must address **not only a child's diagnostic needs but also the family's real circumstances**, including specialist access, education, expenses, employment, emotional wellbeing and clear information.
