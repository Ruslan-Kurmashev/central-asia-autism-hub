A child watches an adult place one building block on top of another and tries to do the same. A few minutes later, the adult notices that the child has arranged the blocks in a long line and copies that action. Both can continue the play in their own way.

In the first situation, the child imitates the adult. In the second, the adult imitates the child. Both can be part of shared play, but not every interaction of this kind is a specialised intervention.

Imitation means reproducing an observed action, gesture, sound or way of using an object. Reciprocal imitation involves an exchange of actions in which the adult also notices and copies the child's initiatives. Reciprocal Imitation Training (RIT), by contrast, is a specific teaching approach studied mainly in young autistic children.

## Types of imitation

A child may copy body movements, gestures, actions with toys, play sequences, sounds or words. These abilities do not necessarily develop at the same rate. For example, a child may find it easy to copy how a toy car is rolled but have difficulty copying a gesture or a sound. This does not mean that the child has no capacity for imitation.

Imitation can help children learn new ways to play and carry out everyday activities by watching another person. It is not the same as obedience. Helping a child learn a useful action through demonstration is different from expecting the child to copy everything on command.

Sometimes a child reproduces the meaning of an action without copying its exact form. An adult taps a block against a box, while the child taps another object on a table. This may be a meaningful attempt at imitation. Research assessments, however, often use defined criteria to score accuracy. A parent's judgement of practical usefulness and a standardised assessment score may not be the same.

## What reciprocal imitation looks like in play

Reciprocal imitation can occur during ordinary play. The illustration shows three examples with the same mother and child.

![Three play scenes: a mother shows how to stack rings on a peg, then copies her child's play with a toy car, and finally they play with toy cars beside a bridge.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/editorial/diagrams/imitation-reciprocal-three-scenes-approved.webp)

1. **The child imitates the adult.** The mother puts a ring on a stacking toy. The child watches and tries the same action.
2. **The adult imitates the child.** The child rolls a toy car. The mother takes a second car and copies the child's play.
3. **Shared play develops.** The adult and child play with cars beside a bridge. The adult may suggest a new action, and the child can copy it or choose another idea.

This depicts everyday play, not a clinical RIT protocol.

What matters here is not perfect movement matching but the opportunity to notice each other's actions and take part in a shared activity. The adult both offers examples and responds to the child's initiative.

This can be ordinary play without a therapeutic goal. If a family or professional chooses to work deliberately on imitation, they should first identify what opportunity the child is expected to gain and why it would be useful.

## What Reciprocal Imitation Training involves

RIT was developed as a naturalistic intervention to support imitation during social interaction and play. In the studied versions, an adult imitated the child's actions, described them, demonstrated new actions with objects or gestures, and created further opportunities for the child to respond. Outcomes were assessed using specific measures of elicited and spontaneous imitation.

The original RIT studies also used prompts, including physical prompts. It would therefore be inaccurate to portray the historical protocol as entirely free play with no demands or prompting. When considering any modern programme, families should separately discuss the child's willingness to participate and right to refuse or take a break, and which prompts are acceptable. Coercion should not be justified by a method's name.

Not every game in which a parent copies a child's movement is RIT. Nor do all naturalistic developmental behavioural interventions (NDBI) use the same protocol. A programme's label alone does not explain how the child will be supported.

## What RIT research has found

### A pilot randomised trial in 2010

In Ingersoll's study, the final analysis included 21 young autistic children, 11 in the RIT group and 10 in the control group. Following the intervention, the RIT group showed greater improvements in both elicited and spontaneous imitation. [Ingersoll, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20155309/).

This is an encouraging result for the specific skills measured. However, the sample was small and baseline elicited imitation scores differed between groups. The trial does not establish that RIT works equally well for all children or automatically improves speech or other areas of development.

### What a 2021 review found

Paparella and Freeman reviewed ten studies of interventions intended to improve motor imitation in young autistic children. Four examined RIT, three video modelling, two applied behaviour analysis (ABA) methods and one a naturalistic developmental behavioural approach. Only one included study used a randomised controlled design, and one used a quasi-experimental before-and-after design. Most of the others used single-participant research designs.

The authors generally found positive changes in imitation of actions with objects and gestures, but emphasised the need for more rigorous studies with larger samples. This was a review of several approaches to teaching motor imitation, not a meta-analysis limited to RIT. [Paparella and Freeman, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/B978-0-323-85031-5.00010-4).

## Generalisation of imitation and other developmental outcomes

A small study by Ingersoll and Schreibman (2006) involving five children reported that imitation carried over to new settings, alongside changes in play, language and joint attention. A comparison by Cardon and Wilcox (2011) involving six boys reported improvements of varying degrees under both RIT and video modelling. These studies used small participant groups and multiple-baseline designs, so they cannot establish reliably that one method is superior to the other.

In a randomised study by Ingersoll (2012) with 27 children, the focused intervention group showed greater changes in initiating joint attention and certain social-emotional measures than controls. However, mediation analysis did not confirm that improved imitation explained those changes. Social-emotional outcomes were based on parent reports, and the small sample limits generalisation.

These findings must not be turned into a promise that teaching movements will necessarily lead to speech. Motor imitation, vocal imitation, joint attention and language are related aspects of development, but they are not interchangeable outcomes. [Ingersoll and Schreibman, 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16568355) · [Cardon and Wilcox, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20697791) · [Ingersoll, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22146934).

## Can parents use reciprocal imitation?

A small study by Ingersoll and colleagues published in 2007 involved three families. Parents learned RIT strategies, and their children showed increases in spontaneous imitation. Skills were also assessed outside the original teaching sessions and one month later. These findings support considering parent participation but do not establish a universal effect or show that every family needs a formal programme. [Ingersoll and Gergans, 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16603337).

In everyday play, a parent can begin simply by noticing what the child is doing, joining the activity and, if the child is interested, suggesting a small variation. This is an example of responsive interaction, not instructions for independently delivering a clinical RIT protocol.

Parents do not have to turn every game into a lesson. If a child becomes tired or does not want to imitate an action, that is a reason to change or end the activity, not increase the demands.

## Communication, gestures and AAC

Shared play does not require spoken language. A child may communicate a wish to repeat an action, choose an object or finish play using a gesture, a picture card, a communication device or another form of augmentative and alternative communication (AAC).

Motor imitation must not be a condition for access to AAC. A communication device that helps the child should not be removed in order to practise sounds or words.

## How to tell whether imitation is useful for a child

Before setting a specific teaching goal, identify its practical purpose. For example, a child may independently try a new way of using a toy during play after seeing an adult or peer demonstrate it. This is more useful than the general aim of “improving imitation”.

When observing progress, distinguish between:

1. Copying a new action spontaneously and doing so after being told to “copy me”.
2. Imitating without assistance and imitating after an additional adult prompt.
3. Using the skill with unfamiliar objects, different people and in different settings.
4. Retaining the skill over time.
5. The skill's practical contribution to play, learning, communication or daily activities.

Copying one gesture successfully does not prove that the skill has been learned and generalised to everyday life. Conversely, not copying one movement precisely does not imply a lack of interest in shared play or an inability to learn in another way.

## When to reconsider the approach

A separate imitation programme may be unnecessary if the child already learns meaningful actions by observing others or through other accessible methods. Reconsider a plan if the exercises have no clear purpose, cause ongoing distress, suppress the child's own initiative or focus mainly on obeying “copy me” commands.

Do not demand exact movement matching merely to obtain a score, restrict communication or take away free play. These are principles of respectful, individualised support, rather than independently demonstrated effects from the RIT efficacy studies listed above.

## Questions for a specialist in Kazakhstan

Imitation-related activities may form part of early intervention, speech and language support, educational support or psychological services. An exercise such as “do what I do” does not show whether RIT or another programme is being used.

Before starting, ask:

1. Which skill is being taught, and why is it useful for this child?
2. Is the specialist using a specific RIT protocol or another teaching method?
3. What training has the specialist completed, and is supervision available?
4. What prompts are used, and how are refusal and breaks respected?
5. How are spontaneous imitation and compliance with an instruction distinguished?
6. Is generalisation checked with other actions, objects, people and settings?
7. When and how are goals revised if there is no progress?

A certificate or an internationally recognised method name, by itself, does not demonstrate professional competence, safety or the effectiveness of a particular programme.

## Key points to remember

Imitation can help children learn useful actions by observing others. Reciprocal imitation allows adults to respond to a child's actions rather than only demonstrating their own. RIT applies these principles within a specific intervention.

Research suggests that RIT may help some imitation skills in young children, but samples are small and controlled studies are limited. Improved imitation does not guarantee the emergence of speech or broad gains in social development. The central question is whether the new skill helps the child act independently and participate in activities that matter to them.
