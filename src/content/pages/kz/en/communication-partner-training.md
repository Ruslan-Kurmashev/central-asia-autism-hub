Communication depends on more than a child's abilities. It also depends on whether parents, teachers, family members and professionals notice messages, respond to them and leave space for the child to initiate an interaction.

**Communication Partner Training** helps adults and peers support everyday communication. Skills may include allowing time for a response, recognising gestures and other communication methods, modelling AAC and reducing unnecessary prompts. The purpose is not to turn parents into therapists. It is to help a person express needs, share thoughts and join conversations more freely.

## Why a communication partner's behaviour matters

Communication difficulties do not always mean that a child cannot express a thought. Sometimes an adult asks the next question before the child has answered, misses a gesture, accepts speech only or immediately supplies the response.

Imagine a child reaching for a communication board to request a break. An adult is in a hurry and selects the symbol on the child's behalf. A message has apparently been conveyed, but the child had no opportunity to express it independently. Waiting and responding to the child's own selection makes communication more accessible.

In such situations it is not only the child's skills that need attention. The partner's habits matter too.

## What do communication partners learn?

Training should reflect the individual's needs rather than a fixed set of exercises. Adults often practise noticing communication attempts, pausing, following the child's interests, offering choices, combining AAC with speech and repairing misunderstandings.

It is also important to accept refusal, avoid turning every conversation into a test and gradually reduce prompts when the person can act independently. Communication partner training is not one universal protocol suitable for every family.

## Allow time for a response

Some people need longer to understand spoken language, find a symbol on a device or formulate a message. If the adult immediately repeats a question, suggests an answer or speaks on the child's behalf, independent initiation can be lost.

Ask a question once, allow a calm pause and watch for a response through speech, gesture, gaze or AAC. **There is no universal rule to wait exactly five or ten seconds.** The pause should suit the individual and situation, and must not delay necessary assistance.

## Conversation is not a constant test

Questions such as "What is this?" or "What colour is it?" and instructions to "Say..." may have a place in teaching. But if almost every interaction consists of such prompts, the child has little chance to choose a topic.

Everyday conversation can also involve commenting on events, talking about a shared interest, asking a question, joking, disagreeing or responding with a gesture. The child does not need to answer every remark made by an adult.

## Recognise different forms of communication

A person can communicate through speech, gestures, manual signs, facial expression, body movement, writing, pictures, communication boards or electronic AAC systems. These methods can be combined.

An adult who responds only to spoken words may miss a perfectly understandable message. At the same time, do not assume you know what a child means when the message is ambiguous. Instead, clarify using an accessible method.

## How partner training relates to AAC

Partners can learn to demonstrate symbols, allow time for responses, ask open questions and leave a device with its user after modelling.

A 2015 meta-analysis brought together 17 single-case experimental studies involving 53 AAC users. Overall, the findings suggested benefits of partner instruction for particular communication outcomes. However, the participants and settings differed, and most studies included children younger than 12 with autism or other developmental disabilities. **These findings do not guarantee equal benefits for every person.**

## What research says about parent-mediated approaches

A systematic review by Zhao and colleagues included 31 randomised trials of parent-mediated interventions for children younger than three. Twenty-six studies were included in the meta-analysis. The authors reported a small overall effect (Hedges' g = 0.20) and positive outcomes for parent responsiveness (g = 0.23), parent-child interaction (g = 0.35) and social communication (g = 0.18). The pooled analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant improvement in language skills.

The authors rated certainty of evidence as moderate or low. Different parent-mediated programmes should not be treated as equivalent to one specific course in AAC partner training.

Claims that such training will necessarily "make a child start talking" are therefore not supported by these findings.

## Adult skill and child outcome are not the same

A professional may successfully teach a parent to pause and notice non-speaking signals. This is a valid outcome of adult training, but it does not yet show that communication has become easier for the child.

Assess **whether partner behaviour changed** separately from **whether the child benefited in practice**. For example, does the child initiate more interactions independently, communicate needs more clearly or use a new method with other people?

In some studies adults learned the proposed strategies more effectively, but the principal child communication outcomes did not differ significantly between groups. Reports of successful parent training are therefore not enough on their own.

## What NICE recommends

NICE guidance for supporting autistic children describes social-communication interventions involving parents, caregivers and educators. It focuses on joint attention, engagement, reciprocal interaction and adults' understanding of, and responsiveness to, a child's communication.

Professionals can demonstrate appropriate ways of interacting. Peer support may also help school-age children communicate.

These are international clinical recommendations, not a mandatory list of programmes for Kazakhstan.

## Parents do not need to be therapists all day

Support should fit family life. There is no need to turn every game, walk or dinner into a structured session with targets and response counts.

Parents can use a learned strategy during ordinary conversation while preserving time for rest and communication without teaching demands. **More homework does not automatically mean greater benefit.**

## Refusal and asking someone to stop matter too

Meaningful communication includes much more than asking for desired objects. People need accessible ways to say "no", "stop", "I don't want to", "help", "it hurts", "too loud" or "I need a break".

If adults teach requests for items but ignore refusal or discomfort, communication remains restricted. A person's unwillingness to continue an optional activity should not automatically be labelled problem behaviour requiring elimination.

## Coordinating home and school

Parents and teachers can agree on how AAC remains available, which words and communication functions are priorities, how much response time to allow and how refusal will be acknowledged.

Consistency should not become a rigid script. Children can still use different communication methods depending on the situation and partner. Where Kazakh, Russian or other languages are spoken in the family, support should reflect the child's real language environment.

## How to know whether partner training is helpful

Agree on one or more meaningful goals and a baseline before starting. Then assess three related but distinct areas:

1. **Partner behaviour.** Does the adult wait more effectively, recognise messages and use fewer unnecessary prompts?
2. **Child communication.** Are there more self-initiated messages, refusals, comments or requests for help?
3. **Everyday life.** Does communication work beyond sessions, with different people and without significant distress?

The number of messages is not enough. Consider whether the person can express what they genuinely want to say. A change in adult habits alone does not establish a benefit for the child.

## When to review the plan

Discuss the plan with the professional if adults follow all instructions yet communication is no more accessible, prompting is increasing, AAC remains unavailable outside sessions or the skill is not used in everyday life.

Other reasons include persistent distress, ignored refusal, excessive family burden or training focused mainly on compliance with adults' demands. Rather than automatically increasing the number of sessions, investigate what is not helping and why.

## Questions to ask the professional

- **Goal.** What will adults learn, and which everyday difficulty should it address?
- **Approach.** How will AAC, the child's initiative and their right to refuse be supported?
- **Outcomes.** How will changes for adults and children be measured separately, including outside sessions?
- **Burden.** How much home practice is expected, and is it realistic for this family?
- **Review.** When will results be discussed and what will change if there is no benefit?

## Considerations in Kazakhstan

Communication partner training may be part of speech-language, educational, developmental or psychological support, or AAC implementation. A course title does not establish the provider's professional qualifications.

Check foundational education, professional role, relevant experience and any requirements for the particular service. A short-course certificate does not replace core professional qualifications and cannot by itself establish the effectiveness of an intervention.

## In brief

Communication partner training addresses not only what the child can do but also how others listen, wait, respond and create opportunities for communication.

Research supports some approaches, but an adult learning a strategy does not guarantee improved communication for the child. Independence, comfort and meaningful outcomes in everyday life should guide evaluation.
