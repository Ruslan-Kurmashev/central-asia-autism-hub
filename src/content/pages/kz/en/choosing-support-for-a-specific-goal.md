Choosing autism support works better when decisions are based on a specific need, rather than the popularity of a method, a centre's name or promises of "comprehensive therapy".

First identify what currently makes life difficult for the child or family. Then consider possible medical and environmental causes, set a meaningful goal, compare options for that goal and decide in advance how to assess outcomes.

> **The guiding principle: goal first, method second.**

## Step 1. Describe the difficulty as specifically as possible

"Improve behaviour" is too broad to guide a decision.

A more useful description focuses on an observable situation:

- the child shouts when moving from playtime to a lesson
- the child cannot ask for a break
- the child wakes five times a night
- the child eats only five foods
- the child cannot get dressed independently.

A clear description makes it easier to see which type of support is relevant.

## Step 2. Check for possible medical causes

Before choosing a developmental or behavioural programme, consider whether a new difficulty could relate to pain or illness.

Situations requiring separate medical assessment include:

- loss of previously acquired skills
- new seizures or unusual periods of unresponsiveness
- significant pain
- swallowing difficulties
- major weight loss or dehydration
- sudden changes in sleep
- new, severe self-injury.

Seek emergency assistance if there is an immediate threat to life or safety.

## Step 3. Set a functional goal

A useful goal describes a change that matters in everyday life.

Instead of **"develop speech"**, try **"the child can communicate that they need a break."**

Instead of **"improve sensory processing"**, try **"the child can stay in the classroom without severe overload when appropriate adjustments are provided."**

Instead of **"be more social"**, try **"if the child wants to play with peers, they can join and leave a game in a way that works for them."**

## Step 4. Consider the environment and access first

Not every difficulty requires a separate therapy.

Sometimes changing the conditions is a better starting point:

- reduce noise
- make routines more predictable
- provide augmentative and alternative communication (AAC)
- allow more response time
- adapt schoolwork
- arrange a quiet space
- change how instructions are presented.

If the main difficulty arises from the environment, trying to "fix the child" may leave its cause untouched.

## Step 5. Ask for the exact name of the proposed method

Labels such as **"sensory therapy"**, **"neurocorrection"**, **"brain development"**, **"proprietary approach"** or **"comprehensive programme"** can describe very different interventions.

Ask:

- what the approach is actually called
- which components it includes
- who delivers it
- what qualifications are required
- which specific goal it is intended to address
- what will happen during sessions.

Without these details, evidence and risks cannot be assessed properly.

## Step 6. Examine the evidence for your particular goal

Instead of asking **"Does this method work for autism?"**, ask:

> **Is there evidence that this approach improves the outcome we need, among people with similar needs and in comparable settings?**

One method may have been studied for communication but not sleep. Another may help treat a particular co-occurring illness without changing the core characteristics of autism.

[Understanding the evidence behind a method](/kz/en/parents/how-to-assess-evidence-for-an-autism-method/)

## Step 7. Distinguish research findings from confidence in those findings

A positive result from one small study is not equivalent to conclusions from several rigorous independent studies.

Two questions are therefore important:

**What do the studies report?**

**How confident can we be in that conclusion?**

Low confidence does not automatically mean that a method does not work. But preliminary positive findings must not be presented as established effectiveness.

## Step 8. Consider risks, demands and the child's rights

Even potentially useful support may place excessive demands on the child.

Consider:

- physical risks
- substantial psychological distress
- how the child feels about the sessions
- intensity
- travel time
- home assignments
- financial costs
- effects on sleep, school, play and rest
- the demands placed on the family.

Persistent severe distress, pain or refusal should not automatically be labelled "resistance that must be overcome".

## Step 9. Check the professional and the service conditions

Ask what qualifications are required for this particular service and whether the professional has them.

Medical services in Kazakhstan must meet the relevant licensing and scope-of-practice requirements. Check these through official government services.

Requirements for psychological and educational services depend on the legal status of the service, the professional role and the type of organisation.

A course completion certificate by itself does not establish professional authorisation or effectiveness.

[More: Choosing a professional and support for an autistic child](/kz/en/parents/choosing-an-autism-professional-and-support/).

## Step 10. Record the baseline before starting

A baseline describes what happens before support begins.

For example:

- the child independently asks for help once during the school day
- falling asleep takes about 50 minutes
- the child eats six different foods
- dressing requires five prompts
- a specific difficult situation occurs four or five times a week.

Without a starting point, ordinary fluctuations or impressions can easily be mistaken for an intervention effect.

## Step 11. Choose 1-3 outcome indicators

Select a few indicators that directly reflect the goal.

These might include frequency, duration, independence, communication, participation, comfort, sleep or the demands placed on the family.

## Step 12. Set a review date in advance

Support should not continue indefinitely simply because it has already begun.

At the review, ask:

- has the situation improved from baseline?
- is the change meaningful?
- does the skill appear outside the professional's office?
- are there unwanted effects?
- is the child experiencing considerable distress?
- are the demands acceptable?
- is the goal still relevant?

[How to tell whether chosen support is helping](/kz/en/parents/how-to-monitor-autism-support-outcomes/)

## When to continue

Continuing may make sense when a meaningful goal is improving, the benefit appears in everyday life, the child is not experiencing unacceptable distress, and demands and risks remain proportionate.

## When to adjust the plan

The plan may need changing if it helps partly but the intensity, delivery, environment, goal, professional or outcome measurement needs adjustment.

## When to stop or pause

Possible reasons include:

- no meaningful benefit after an adequate period
- significant unwanted effects
- persistent severe distress
- excessive demands
- substantial financial harm
- persistent refusal of non-essential support
- no transfer of gains to daily life
- the original goal is no longer relevant.

Stopping support that is not sufficiently useful is a reasonable decision.

## Warning signs in marketing

Be cautious if a provider:

- promises to "cure autism"
- guarantees rapid results
- proposes one method for almost every problem
- explains lack of progress by insisting on even more hours
- demands a large advance payment before individual assessment
- cannot name the precise protocol
- refuses to discuss risks
- relies mainly on testimonials or "before and after" videos
- presents a licence or certificate as proof of effectiveness.

## Three brief examples

### A child cannot ask for a break

Goal: **the child has an accessible way to ask for a break before becoming severely overwhelmed.**

Possible approaches include AAC, visual supports, training communication partners, functional communication training (FCT) and adjustments to the school environment.

### A child shouts while getting dressed

First investigate possible causes: pain, unpleasant fabric sensations, a complicated sequence, rushing, unclear instructions or wanting to decline.

Different circumstances call for different responses, including medical assessment, clothing adjustments, a visual sequence, occupational therapy or teaching a specific skill.

### A teenager finds it difficult to make friends

First ask whether the teenager wants the situation to change and which goal matters to them.

Support should help achieve a personally chosen goal, not teach someone to hide autistic characteristics to appear outwardly typical.

## Brief checklist before starting

1. **What is the specific goal?**
2. **Have possible medical causes been considered?**
3. **Can the environment or access be improved first?**
4. **What is the exact name of the method?**
5. **What evidence relates to this goal?**
6. **What are the risks, demands, costs and the child's views?**
7. **What will we measure, and when will we review the decision?**

## What matters in Kazakhstan

Before choosing a professional or centre, check three separate things:

**professional qualifications for the particular service,**

**the necessary legal or licensing status,**

**the evidence supporting the method itself.**

These questions should not be confused.
