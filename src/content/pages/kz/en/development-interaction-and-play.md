Play helps children explore the world, express their interests and interact with other people. It creates opportunities for communication, imitation, shared activities and learning skills that can be useful in daily life.

Different approaches may be used to support these abilities in autistic children. Some aim to help a child share interest in an object or event, while others support communication, participation in play or learning within everyday activities. These are not one uniform “play therapy”. Programmes differ in their goals, how sessions are organised and what research tells us about their outcomes.

The starting point is not how closely a child's play resembles that of other children, but which forms of communication and participation are accessible to the child and what matters to them.

## Joint attention: sharing an interest

Joint attention is the sharing of attention to an object, event or action with another person.

For example, a child notices an aeroplane, points to it or communicates about it through AAC, and a parent responds to the same object.

Or an adult draws attention to something interesting, and the child directs their attention towards it.

Joint attention does not mean that a child must “look the adult in the eyes”. Eye contact may occur, but prolonged eye contact is not a prerequisite for sharing attention.

### Responding and initiating

Joint attention is commonly described in terms of two related components.

**Responding to Joint Attention (RJA).** Another person directs the child's attention towards an object or event, and the child notices the signal and shifts attention. For example, an adult points to a cat and the child looks towards the cat.

**Initiating Joint Attention (IJA).** The child independently tries to draw another person's attention to an object, event or activity in order to share interest. For example, the child notices an aeroplane and points it out, brings something interesting, or uses a gesture, word or AAC.

RJA and IJA are related but distinct skills. A child may respond readily to another person's pointing while rarely initiating joint attention, or the reverse.

### An example of joint attention

A child likes toy cars. An adult notices an unusual car and points to it. If the child looks towards the car, this illustrates RJA.

Later the child notices another car and:

- points to it
- brings it to the adult
- says “look”
- selects a message on an AAC system.

This may illustrate IJA.

Joint attention does not require sustained eye contact. Do not physically turn the child's face or hold their gaze. The child can share interest through a gesture, word, action with an object or AAC message.

## Engagement and reciprocal communication

### What engagement means

Engagement means participating in an activity or interaction. For example, a child may:

- take part in shared play
- respond to another person's actions
- initiate interaction
- remain involved in an activity that matters to them.

Engagement should not be judged solely by sitting quietly or looking at an adult. A child may be actively engaged while moving, using AAC, avoiding continuous eye contact or interacting in their own way.

### Exchanging messages and initiatives

Reciprocal communication is an exchange of initiatives and messages between people. It may involve initiating, responding, asking questions, commenting or doing things together.

Communication can occur through speech, AAC, gestures, pictures, play or several methods at once. The number of spoken words alone therefore does not capture reciprocal communication.

### Including AAC in play

Children who use augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) should not be excluded from play-based or developmental interventions because their speech is limited or absent.

A systematic review and meta-analysis by Pope and colleagues included 29 studies of NDBI involving autistic children with minimal spoken language. Language effects in the aggregated data were larger when aided AAC was included, although only three studies directly compared NDBI with and without AAC. [Pope and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38848009/).

The supported conclusion is that AAC can be integrated into naturalistic developmental support. Its exact additional effect within NDBI has been assessed in relatively few direct comparisons.

[AAC: augmentative and alternative communication](/kz/en/parents/aac-communication-guide/).

Speech is not required for shared play. Children may participate through gestures, movements, actions with objects, facial expressions and AAC.

## Play: worthwhile in itself and a context for learning

Play can be a meaningful activity in its own right, a form of interaction, a context for learning or an outcome that an intervention is intended to support.

NICE recommends considering specific social-communication interventions that use play-based strategies to support joint attention, engagement and reciprocal communication. The approach should be matched to the child's developmental level. For preschool children, NICE recommends considering parents, carers or teachers, and for school-age children it recommends considering peer involvement. These are UK clinical recommendations, not Kazakhstan's regulatory rules. [NICE CG170](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg170/chapter/recommendations).

### Respecting the child's interests

If a child enjoys trains, dinosaurs, maps, water, numbers or another interest, it can provide a context for engagement, shared play, communication and learning. The interest should not be deliberately taken away and returned only as a reward.

### When play does not have to be shared

Not all play needs to involve another person. Being able to rest alone, play independently, pursue interests and choose leisure activities can also be meaningful parts of independence.

Not every action should be converted into a teaching trial. If an adult continually asks questions and requests repetitions, play may lose its enjoyment and voluntary character. Choosing the activity, taking a break and playing independently also matter.

### Goals worth discussing

Possible goals include:

- having more accessible activities to enjoy
- inviting another person to join a game
- expressing one's ideas
- offering or taking turns
- developing a shared play scenario
- spending enjoyable free time independently.

The goal does not need to be “play like other children”. The opportunity to play freely, rest and follow personal interests may itself be meaningful. Unusual ways of using toys do not, by themselves, justify demanding “correct” play. A goal should be guided by the child's interests, available abilities and functional benefit.

## Approaches that may be used

### Naturalistic Intervention: learning in everyday situations

Naturalistic Intervention uses real-life contexts to teach a skill. These may include play, meals, walks, home routines, school activities and things the child is interested in.

Hume and colleagues (2021) classified Naturalistic Intervention among practices meeting their evidence-based criteria. However, that review covered research only through the end of 2017. [Hume and colleagues, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8510990/).

A skill can be practised where it is actually needed. For example, a child might use a word, gesture or AAC message meaning “open” when they want to open a toy box. The natural consequence of that request is that the box is opened.

### Distinguishing teaching from free play

Structured naturalistic teaching differs from ordinary free play. A teaching procedure usually has:

- a specific goal
- defined teaching strategies
- ways to provide support
- opportunities for practice
- an outcome that can be measured.

Free play also has value and need not constantly become an intervention.

### NDBI: Naturalistic Developmental Behavioral Interventions

NDBI are a broad class of interventions combining principles of child development, behavioural learning and teaching through natural interactions and daily activities.

NDBI does not refer to one programme. Saying simply “NDBI works” is too general. It is necessary to specify which programme or procedure is used, for what age and goal, at what intensity, by whom, and how the outcome is measured.

### Parent-mediated support

Parent-mediated support involves parents or other carers learning strategies that they can use during natural interactions. NICE recommends considering the involvement of parents, carers or teachers in social-communication interventions for preschool children.

This does not mean that a parent should become a full-time therapist. A parent remains a parent. An intervention should not turn every family exchange into a lesson or impose an unmanageable burden.

### Peer-based support

Peer-based support involves other children, sometimes prepared for the role, in helping create opportunities for interaction, participation, communication and shared activities. NICE recommends considering peer mediation in social-communication interventions for school-age children.

The aim should not be to train peers to make an autistic child appear or behave “normally”. A more appropriate goal is accessible and reciprocal participation.

### Imitation and reciprocal imitation

Imitation can help a child learn new actions and can form part of shared play. It should not become a universal requirement that a child copy an adult on demand.

Reciprocal imitation involves an adult also imitating the child's actions to support interaction, rather than expecting only the child to imitate the adult.

## What the research shows

### Joint attention

A 2026 meta-analysis included 18 randomised controlled trials involving 1,165 autistic children younger than five years.

The pooled effect on joint-attention skills was Hedges' g = 0.53, 95% confidence interval 0.34 to 0.72. This is a moderate average effect, but results varied substantially between studies. Hedges' g is a standardised effect size, not the percentage of children acquiring a skill. It does not predict an individual child's outcome or justify guaranteeing improvement.

Child age and the particular type of joint attention were associated with differences in effect sizes. The authors also reported findings for play, but those additional outcomes require cautious interpretation. [Song and colleagues, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41493690/).

Joint-attention interventions thus have supportive research evidence in young autistic children, but their effects are not uniform across children and programmes.

### NDBI: findings from the 2019 meta-analysis

Tiede and Walton included 27 group-design studies of NDBI involving young autistic children. Their average effect sizes were:

- expressive language: g = 0.32
- play skills: g = 0.23
- social engagement: g = 0.65
- general cognitive development: g = 0.48
- joint attention: g = 0.14, a small, borderline statistically significant effect
- receptive language: g = 0.28.

The authors also reported signs of publication and reporting bias for language outcomes. Results therefore depend on the particular skill being measured. [Tiede and Walton, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31018655/).

### Why assessment context matters

Project AIM separately analysed 27 NDBI studies and 454 outcomes. Effects were larger for outcomes closely tied to what was taught and assessed in contexts similar to the intervention. Effects were smaller for more distal and generalised outcomes.

The authors cautioned that confidence in positive pooled effects was limited by methodological concerns, including some measures with a high risk of assessment bias. [Crank and colleagues, 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33480474/).

A skill demonstrated during therapy therefore does not automatically mean the same skill will appear at home, school or in other contexts.

### What the 2025 review adds

A mixed-methods systematic review published online in 2025 included 25 studies: 16 randomised controlled trials and nine qualitative studies. It combined early intensive behavioural interventions with NDBI, so its findings cannot be attributed to NDBI alone.

The quantitative analyses reported significant average effects for:

- adaptive behaviour: SMD = 0.31, GRADE certainty low
- daily living skills: SMD = 0.36, GRADE certainty low
- language: SMD = 0.42, GRADE certainty moderate
- joint attention: SMD = 0.27, GRADE certainty low.

Many included studies had high risk of bias, and results varied substantially. [Han and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41454358/).

Parents should not be promised that a developmental intervention will improve every area of development.

### Play-based interventions: a 2026 meta-analysis

A meta-analysis by Çuvadar Baş and colleagues included only seven studies. It reported statistically significant average changes in social and play skills, but between-study heterogeneity was very high:

- social skills: I² = 93.730%
- play skills: I² = 94.768%.

The I² statistic describes differences between study results. These percentages do not represent the proportion of children who benefited or the probability of success for an individual child.

The authors also examined subgroups of play-based approaches. With only seven studies overall, subgroup findings should not be treated as a reliable ranking or proof that one programme is superior to another.

The appropriate conclusion is that some play-based interventions have positive findings for social and play outcomes, but the category is too diverse to promise the same effect from every programme. [Çuvadar Baş and colleagues, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42287507/).

### Interpreting limitations

A large average effect in a small meta-analysis does not mean that every child will experience a large improvement. Consider the seven included studies, very high heterogeneity, differences among programmes, different outcomes and the quality of each study.

Data on social or play skills also cannot automatically be used to claim benefits for language, adaptive functioning, quality of life or other unmeasured outcomes.

Questions for a family to ask include:

- What specific programme is being offered?
- What outcome is it intended to support?
- Is it appropriate for the child's age and communication methods?
- How will that exact outcome be measured?
- Is there evidence for this particular programme and goal?

### Pretend play

Some approaches target symbolic or pretend play, such as feeding a toy bear, pretending a box is a car or inventing a story.

However, the presence or absence of a particular type of play should not be used as the sole measure of development, wellbeing or quality of life. If pretend play is selected as a goal, its importance to this particular child should be clear.

Average study findings cannot predict an individual child's outcome. Decisions about support should account for research on the actual programme, the child's age, the selected goal and the assessment method.

## Supporting interaction during ordinary play

If a child enjoys toy cars, an adult may join in, copy an action with a second car, pause, and allow the child to continue or suggest the next step. The response may be a movement, gesture, word or AAC message. The adult responds to the child's initiative rather than requiring one “correct” answer.

### Supporting joint attention

Support often makes use of play, the child's interests, waiting for initiative, modelling, natural situations and shared activities.

An adult can notice what interests the child, join in, point out something interesting, allow time to respond and acknowledge attempts to share interest.

Communication should not become a constant test of “look”, “show me” and “what is it?”. Children need ordinary free play and the option not to join every interaction. If a child does not wish to interact, that choice should be respected. Playing without adult intervention can be a complete and meaningful activity.

## Assessing whether support is useful

### Functional outcomes

Instead of the vague description “became more social”, define a practical outcome. For example, the child:

- initiates a shared activity independently
- points out or communicates about an interesting object
- responds to someone else's initiative
- takes part in several reciprocal exchanges
- uses AAC during play
- has more meaningful play activities available
- uses the skill at home, school and in other contexts.

### What is not automatically a measure of success

Do not assume that seconds of eye contact, suppression of safe stimming, sitting quietly, copying neurotypical gestures or eliminating unusual play are meaningful primary outcomes. Any such measure should have a clear functional purpose for the child.

### Generalising skills

Check whether the skill can be used at home, at a playground, at school, with different adults and peers, and in different activities. If joint attention or another skill only appears with one professional and one toy, generalisation has not yet been demonstrated.

## When to reconsider support and what to ask a specialist

### Family burden

If a programme requires many hours of structured daily practice, constant data recording or turning every interaction into training, the burden on the family should be assessed separately. More hours do not automatically produce better outcomes for every child or programme.

### Reasons to review the plan

Review is warranted if:

- the child repeatedly experiences significant distress
- goals are unclear or disconnected from real needs
- interactions are almost entirely adult-directed
- the child has little opportunity to choose or refuse
- skills do not generalise to everyday life
- the family burden is excessive
- outcomes are measured only during therapy sessions
- the focus is on masking rather than functional participation.

### Main questions for a specialist

1. Which specific goal has been chosen for the child, and why?
2. How are the child's interests, preferences and right to refuse respected?
3. How can the child communicate, including through AAC?
4. What will be measured, and how will generalisation be checked in daily life?
5. What evidence applies to this programme, age group and goal?
6. What burden is expected of the family, and when will the plan be reviewed?

### Considerations in Kazakhstan

Developmental and play-based approaches may be part of speech and language services, psychological or educational support, developmental programmes, occupational therapy and other multidisciplinary care.

A programme name or certificate for a particular method does not itself establish a practitioner's professional qualifications. Check their core education, professional role, area of competence and the applicable requirements for that service in Kazakhstan.
