## Why study autism research from our own region?

When parents seek information about autism, they often find studies from the United States, United Kingdom and other countries. Those studies can be useful and scientifically rigorous. But families in Kazakhstan and neighbouring countries face additional questions: are specialists available, how does assessment work, what barriers do parents encounter, and can established approaches be adapted to local languages and settings?

This makes **regional autism research** important. Its role is not simply to repeat findings from abroad. It should examine local healthcare, education and social-support systems, alongside the experiences of autistic people and their families.

Here, we map selected studies published between 2013 and October 2026 and explain which questions their methods can address.

**This is a selective AutismHub research explainer, not a systematic search of every publication.** The selected studies have identifiable original sources, research questions, designs and limitations. The timeline does not show annual bibliometric totals.

## How systematic investigation of regional research began

In 2023, **Rano Zakirova-Engstrand** of Stockholm University and **Gulnoza Yakubova** of the University of Maryland published *A scoping review of autism research conducted in Central Asia* online. The article appeared in the February 2024 issue of *Autism*.

They examined peer-reviewed empirical studies involving autistic people and families in Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan, Tajikistan and Turkmenistan. They searched English- and Russian-language publications and assessed full-text availability and predefined eligibility criteria.

Their review included **11 studies published in 2013-2021**:

| Country | Studies included in the review |
| --- | ---: |
| Kazakhstan | 10 |
| Uzbekistan | 1 |
| Kyrgyzstan | 0 |
| Tajikistan | 0 |
| Turkmenistan | 0 |

*Source: [Zakirova-Engstrand and Yakubova, Autism, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613231170553). The zeros refer only to this review's eligible sample and search period. They do not mean no research has ever been conducted in these countries.*

The authors grouped research by themes including diagnosis, biology, risk factors, services and support, interventions, lifespan issues and surveillance infrastructure.

**Services and parents' experiences** were especially prominent. The review identified no eligible studies in two areas during its search period: autistic people across the lifespan and epidemiological monitoring infrastructure.

It is essential to distinguish **when a review was published from the period it searched**. The count cannot be presented as the total number of studies in 2026. Later papers included analyses of national medical registries.

### 2013: how parents advocated for educational opportunities

One of the earliest works included in the scoping review appeared in the *Journal of Social Policy Studies* in December 2013. **Markova and Sultanalieva** examined the work of the Ashyk Alem organisation in Kazakhstan and parents' involvement in advancing educational rights for autistic children.

They used **qualitative methods**, including interviews, observation and analysis of relationships among parents, the organisation and government bodies.

Such work can explain individual experiences and processes of social change. It cannot tell us what percentage of all Kazakhstan families face a particular difficulty.

**Main finding:** family-led initiatives may be important for discussing educational access and cooperating with institutions. This was research on civic participation, not a trial of medical or educational therapy.

[Original 2013 publication](https://jsps.hse.ru/article/view/3414).

### 2019-2021: new approaches to interaction, education and technology

In 2019, **Anara Sandygulova and colleagues** at Nazarbayev University published a *Paladyn* study of children's interactions with a robotic system. Nazarbayev University and University Medical Center staff contributed to its development.

The researchers observed **14 autistic children**, some with co-occurring attention or activity differences, conducted repeated NAO robot sessions, gathered feedback and modified the interaction design.

This answers the question: *can the technology be designed to be more usable and acceptable, and what adjustments do children need?*

It **does not answer** whether the robot outperforms established support in improving communication or quality of life. Such a conclusion would require suitable comparative research and clinically meaningful outcomes.

[Sandygulova and colleagues, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1515/pjbr-2019-0027).

The 2024 regional review also covered family support, healthcare and education, biological characteristics and technology. However, the studies within these areas were limited and heterogeneous, so definitive region-wide guidance would have been premature.

## What research appeared in 2024-2026?

After the period covered by the earlier review, studies with different forms of evidence appeared, from small interviews with parents to analyses of thousands of electronic health records.

## Area 1. Early identification and diagnosis

In 2024, researchers published a study of screening at three primary healthcare facilities in Karaganda. **526 children** took part and the **M-CHAT-R/F** instrument was used to identify developmental risk.

The paper's **abstract** labels 27.4% of participants as high risk. Its detailed **initial M-CHAT-R table**, however, lists **76 children (14.4%) at medium risk** and **68 (12.9%) at high risk**. Together **144 (27.4%) were medium or high risk**, which the abstract does not accurately distinguish. The clarifying Follow-Up interview is a separate stage and must not be conflated with initial scores.

These numbers demand particular care: children attended **clinics for psychophysical developmental evaluation**. They were not randomly selected from all children in Kazakhstan.

Screening identifies who may need further assessment. **The figure of 27.4% does not mean that 27.4% of children in Karaganda or Kazakhstan have an autism diagnosis.** The study does not support a population prevalence estimate.

[Original article in Clinical Epidemiology and Global Health, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cegh.2024.101703).

Regional research must also address the translation and cultural adaptation of diagnostic instruments. Translating a questionnaire into Russian or Kazakh does not by itself establish its sensitivity or specificity for the target population.

## Area 2. Family experiences and service access

In 2025, **Aziza Imamatdinova and colleagues** published a study in *BMC Public Health* involving **390 parents** of autistic children from urban and rural areas of Kazakhstan.

Families were asked about service access, financial burden and difficulties seeking help. Of the respondents, **73.3% said government financial support was insufficient**. Some also described changes in employment and additional costs.

This is important evidence for support planning, but the wording must remain precise: **73.3% of surveyed parents**, not 73.3% of all Kazakhstan families with autistic children. Since participants had contact with service organisations, the study may have underrepresented families without such access.

[Imamatdinova and colleagues, BMC Public Health, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-025-23764-3).

### Parent wellbeing and psychologists' work

**Nazgul Akhtayeva and colleagues** surveyed **327 parents** and interviewed **10 primary-care psychologists**. The paper appeared online in 2024 and in a 2025 issue of *Archives of Medical Science*.

They examined different aspects of parental wellbeing and professionals' experiences. Findings suggested a need for better psychological support and professional preparation.

This does not mean all families experience the same emotional burden. The study involved parents connected to specialist support services, and responses depend on family circumstances and the measurement tools used.

[Akhtayeva and colleagues, DOI 10.5114/aoms/190772](https://doi.org/10.5114/aoms/190772).

## Area 3. Public awareness

**Sandugash Kurmanalina and colleagues** studied **410 adult respondents** using Russian- and Kazakh-language questionnaires in 2023. The article was published online in 2024 and in a 2025 issue of the *Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders*.

Only **18.3% of participants**, according to the authors' criteria, demonstrated familiarity with core features and causes of autism.

This is not an estimate for the full population of Kazakhstan. The questionnaire circulated through social networks and professional groups rather than a nationally random sample.

Nevertheless, the study raises a useful applied question: **what information people receive, where they seek answers and how to develop accessible, non-stigmatising education**.

[Kurmanalina and colleagues, DOI 10.1007/s10803-024-06350-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10803-024-06350-1).

## Area 4. Registry statistics and medical epidemiology

### Analysis by Karaganda Medical University

In June 2025, **Karina Nukeshtayeva, Zhanerke Bolatova, Aza Galayeva, Nurzhamal Shintayeva and Gulmira Zhanalina** of Karaganda Medical University published a study of regional and national health records for **2016-2022** in *Frontiers in Psychiatry*.

They examined selected diagnostic categories, including childhood and atypical autism, and identified increases in registrations and differences across regions.

**What it demonstrated:** recorded diagnostic counts changed in medical reporting. **What it did not demonstrate:** that environmental or other regional factors caused those differences, or that true prevalence rose at the same rate.

[Nukeshtayeva and colleagues, 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1520460).

### A national electronic cohort of 9,993 children

In March 2026, *Scientific Reports* published a study by **Kamilla Mussina, Dmitriy Syssoyev, Abduzhappar Gaipov and colleagues**, associated with Nazarbayev University and University Medical Center.

The authors used a nationwide electronic medical database for **2014-2021**. It contained **9,993 autistic children under 18**, of whom **5,912 (59.2%)** had at least one recorded hospital admission during observation.

They also studied associations between co-occurring medical conditions and hospitalisation. For example, admissions were more frequent among children with certain gastrointestinal and neurological conditions.

This does **not prove** that these conditions cause autism. Nor does it establish that a particular treatment reduces hospital admission risk: the study addressed a different question.

[Mussina and colleagues, Scientific Reports, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-46715-3).

### National child registry analysis for 2014-2024

In July 2026, **Amir Baikatov, Ayat Assemov, Dias Alimbetov and Almira Kustubayeva** published a registry study of children **under 16**. The represented institutions included Al-Farabi Kazakh National University, OLYMP and the national health development research centre.

They analysed **214,904 child-year observations** spanning several groups of neurodevelopmental conditions. These corresponded to **68,384 distinct identifiers**.

**The 214,904 observations are not 214,904 individual autistic children.** The same child might appear in multiple years, and conditions other than ASD were included.

The authors described strong increases in recorded ASD measures and associations between regional patterns and diagnostic practices or service organisation.

[Baikatov and colleagues, Frontiers in Psychiatry, 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2026.1880419).

## Research beyond Kazakhstan

### Uzbekistan: mothers' experiences of seeking a diagnosis

In a paper by **Kristie Asaro-Saddler and colleagues**, first published online in June 2025 and included in a 2026 issue, **seven mothers from Uzbekistan** participated.

Researchers conducted an online focus group and thematic analysis. Mothers discussed their understanding of autism and difficulties in the diagnostic pathway, including limited access to trained professionals and services.

Seven participants are too few for numerical generalisations, but qualitative research has a different aim. It can examine **what problems arise and how families themselves make sense of their experiences**, informing later, broader studies.

Their views cannot be treated as representative of all Uzbekistan parents or as a measured national rate of difficulties.

[Asaro-Saddler and colleagues, DOI 10.1177/15407969251343493](https://doi.org/10.1177/15407969251343493).

### Tajikistan: contemporary registration data

In 2026, **S. M. Abdullozoda, S. J. Sirojzoda, Z. A. Umarova and Sh. M. Mirzovalizoda** of the Avicenna Tajik State Medical University and Tajik Research Institute of Preventive Medicine published a retrospective analysis of development-condition registration.

They examined reports for children **aged 0-17 in 2020-2025** and reported **170 cumulative ASD registration cases** across the period, with substantial regional differences.

These results point to the need for better registration. They **are not** a count of all autistic children in Tajikistan and cannot be used to compare true national prevalence directly with Kazakhstan.

[Abdullozoda and colleagues, 2026, original journal page](https://www.vestnik-ipovszrt.tj/ru/detail.php?id=106).

### Kyrgyzstan and Turkmenistan: which studies could be confirmed?

The 2024 historical review found no studies meeting its inclusion criteria for Kyrgyzstan or Turkmenistan. But **absence from one scientific review does not mean an absence of researchers, services, studies or publications altogether**.

This editorial selection has not established a verified catalogue of peer-reviewed empirical studies from those two countries with adequate descriptions of authors, methods and results. We therefore do not name unverified institutions or fabricate findings.

Further research would require searches in local languages, university repositories, national scholarly journals and international indexes.

International research teams also study Central Asia. But an author's workplace, where research was conducted, participants' country of residence and a project's funder are **different characteristics**. A study is not nationally representative by virtue of its authors' citizenship or affiliation.

## How to interpret study sample sizes

The number of participants alone does not determine quality.

Consider three research questions:

1. **'What barriers do mothers describe when seeking a diagnosis?'** Seven detailed interviews can identify themes and hypotheses, not a percentage of all families.
2. **'How many children screened in selected clinics received an elevated risk score?'** A sample of 526 can describe those clinics' attendees, not the wider population.
3. **'How have recorded diagnoses changed nationally?'** Thousands of electronic records can show reporting patterns, but cannot identify children outside those systems.

When reading a paper, ask: **who was studied, how, what was measured and how far do the findings generalise?**

## What is still missing from regional science?

The early scoping review described research gaps and some later studies have begun to address them. Important questions remain.

### Studies of autistic adults

Child health registries tell us little about autistic adults, later diagnosis, higher education, employment, independent living or support after school.

### Longitudinal research

Many papers provide a cross-sectional snapshot by assessing or surveying participants once. Understanding change over time requires **longitudinal studies** that follow the same people across several periods.

### Evaluation of specific support approaches

Studies of family needs are valuable, but they cannot establish how effective an intervention is. Effectiveness research needs suitable comparisons, predefined outcomes, adequately sized samples, bias control and transparent protocol registration.

### Population statistics beyond medical registers

Registers contain data for people known to health services, not every autistic person in society. True prevalence requires well-designed population studies and consistent ASD definitions.

### Research in local languages and international collaboration

Studies need sound adaptation of instruments, published methods and family participation in setting research questions. Cooperation among scientists from different Central Asian countries is another important goal, while maintaining research independence and ethical standards.

These are **proposed priorities** based on observed gaps. They do not mean that any future project is necessarily useful or methodologically sound.

## What these findings mean for families and professionals

Regional studies already show that autism cannot be discussed solely in clinical terms. Real access pathways, family costs, professional preparation and availability of information also matter.

Readers should still check the limits of each claim. Even a paper in a respected journal cannot automatically extrapolate a small sample to national rates or turn an observed association into a demonstrated cause.

Verified local research helps us ask **more precise questions** about support systems. The next step is to turn those questions into sound research that includes autistic people and their families.
