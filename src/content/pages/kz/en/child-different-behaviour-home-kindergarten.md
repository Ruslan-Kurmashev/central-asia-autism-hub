Parents sometimes hear, "At preschool the child follows instructions quietly, but cries and becomes irritable after coming home." Another family may report the opposite: "At home my child talks and plays, but hardly responds to unfamiliar adults."

Parents and educators may see **different aspects of the same child's behaviour**. Neither person must be mistaken. The child is not necessarily changing behaviour deliberately, and the family is not necessarily the cause.

Noise, routines, other people, familiar communication, fatigue, physical health and the ability to request help can all influence participation. The useful question is not "Where is the child good?" but **"Under which conditions can this child feel safe, communicate and take part?"**

## 1. Why home and preschool can feel different

At home, the child may have familiar adults, preferred toys, flexibility and quicker help. At preschool there may be louder rooms, larger groups, transitions and more group instructions. Sometimes the reverse is true: preschool offers a clear schedule and visual prompts, whereas family routines are less predictable.

These differences can affect responses, but no single factor proves the cause of one behaviour.

| What changes | Home | Preschool or school | What to ask |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Familiar people** | Relatives know personal gestures and signals. | Staff and peers may interpret signals differently. | How can the child request help or refuse? |
| **Noise and sensations** | A familiar quiet room may be available. | Group activities, dining rooms and unexpected noise. | Can the child move away from noise? |
| **Routine** | More flexible or sometimes less predictable. | Often more structured. | Are transitions announced in advance? |
| **Language** | Relatives might speak Russian and Kazakh. | A different language may predominate. | Which messages does the child understand here? |
| **Demands** | Dressing, chores and family interaction. | Waiting, group tasks and play rules. | How many instructions arrive at once? |
| **Rest** | Familiar recovery strategies. | A quiet break might be harder to access. | Can the child request a break? |

NICE recommends considering the physical and social environment, communication needs, pain, anxiety, sleep, predictability and routine changes when assessing difficulties.

## 2. When preschool says "everything is fine" but home is difficult

An educator may see a child sitting quietly and completing tasks. A parent sees crying and exhaustion after preschool. **Both observations can be accurate.** Discuss possible contributors rather than assuming a diagnosis or cause.

Useful questions include whether there were many loud activities or transitions, whether the child could ask for a break, food, water or the toilet, whether instructions were understood, and whether there was physical discomfort, fear, peer conflict or pressure to suppress usual communication or movement.

These are **questions, not confirmed explanations**. Crying after preschool does not by itself establish sensory overload, masking or a meltdown.

### What to try after returning home

1. Avoid immediately adding many questions, lessons or obligations.
2. Offer food and water as appropriate and access to a familiar comfortable place.
3. Allow quiet play, rest or proximity to a trusted adult according to the child's preference.
4. Accept communication through words, gestures, pictures or asking to end an interaction.
5. If this repeatedly happens, ask educators later what preceded the transition home.

Do not require a child who is distressed or exhausted to give a verbal account before receiving support.

## 3. When a child communicates at home but remains silent at preschool

Some children readily talk, play or show things with relatives, yet use fewer words or gestures with teachers or strangers. Potential explanations include unfamiliar people, a different language, anxiety, sensory load, difficulty processing instructions, a lack of familiar communication methods or other health and contextual factors.

Do not conclude "The child can talk at home, so must talk at preschool" or "The family exaggerated the child's skills."

Ask which activities interest the child, who helps them feel comfortable, whether short instructions are understood in the school's language, whether gestures, pictures, AAC or actions are accepted, and what supports initiative without demanding speech. Consider hearing, pain and other factors.

Persistent communication difficulties that limit participation deserve assessment by appropriate professionals, potentially including hearing and communication assessment. One observation cannot establish a diagnosis.

## 4. What masking means, and why caution matters

**Masking** can refer to an autistic person consciously or unconsciously hiding or suppressing behaviours and communication differences to meet others' expectations. Examples may include forcing eye contact, suppressing familiar movements, concealing discomfort or imitating social responses. The National Autistic Society describes how this can take effort.

However, not every home-school difference is masking. You cannot diagnose masking from crying or apparent calm. A child may struggle to describe internal experiences, and masking is not a separate medical diagnosis. Research does not yet provide a simple, reliable estimate of masking in every group of children.

A better response is to learn what is difficult, reduce unnecessary pressures, enable communication and take the child's signals seriously.

## 5. Why a child may respond differently to different adults

A child may communicate more freely with an adult who understands their gestures, waits for a response, offers understandable choices, speaks a familiar language and accepts refusal. Another adult may ask more questions, move quickly or communicate differently.

For example, a child might request "su" (water) from their grandmother but point at a bottle when speaking to a preschool educator. This **does not necessarily indicate loss of the skill**. The key question is whether the message is understood and the need is met.

Rather than demanding exactly the same form of communication with everyone, share the child's preferred methods with adults. Also see our articles on everyday communication and bilingual family language development.

## 6. Eight everyday situations and possible adaptations

| Situation | What to find out | What might help |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Exhaustion after preschool** | How was the day, and were rest and communication available? | A calm transition and fewer optional demands. |
| **2. Silent in class, communicative at home** | Language, trust, anxiety, hearing and response options. | Gestures, pictures, AAC and participation without forcing speech. |
| **3. Dresses at preschool but resists at home** | Clothing comfort, speed, instructions and assistance. | Comfortable clothes, more time and one manageable step. |
| **4. Difficulties eating at preschool** | Sound, smell, seating, food and medical factors. | A quieter setting, suitable utensils and professional advice. Choking concerns need medical input. |
| **5. Avoids noisy group games** | Volume, number of children and sensory experience. | Smaller groups, playing nearby or taking a break. |
| **6. "Compliant" at school but crying at home** | Were signs of distress overlooked? | Ask about wellbeing, allow safe refusal and reconsider demands. |
| **7. More comfortable with one educator** | Differences in instructions, pace and familiarity. | Share successful supports with other staff, with family consent. |
| **8. Changes after joining a new group** | New people, rules, language and rest access. | Advance introductions, visual routine and a transition plan. |

These are discussion options, not a universal treatment programme. Adapt support to the individual child's strengths and preferences.

Instead of saying "Good at preschool, bad at home", compare three components.

### Conditions

Where was the child and with whom? How loud was it? What happened just beforehand? Did play have to end suddenly? Did the child understand the language used?

### Support

Could they request help or refuse? Were visual cues, familiar objects, a quiet space and a trusted adult available?

### Participation and wellbeing

What did the child **actually** do or say? What worked? What was difficult? Were exhaustion, pain, fear or refusal apparent?

Discussing these observations can guide changes, but this is not a formal functional behaviour assessment and does not prove causation.

## 8. How to talk with educators without blaming anyone

Both the family and educator can acknowledge that **they see the child in different contexts**.

A parent might say:

> "After preschool our child often cries and covers their ears. I realise you may not see that in the group. Could we look at which parts of the day are difficult and what helps them feel comfortable?"

An educator might say:

> "The child usually takes part in group tasks, but just before lunch they become quieter and step away. Perhaps we can signal the transition earlier and make it easier to request a break."

Five useful questions:

1. When is the child interested and comfortable?
2. What happened immediately before the difficult episode?
3. How could the child request help, refuse or ask for a break?
4. Which approaches have already helped?
5. What small, safe change can home and preschool agree to try and review?

The goal is a shared understanding of the child's needs and strengths, not to decide which adult knows the child best.

## 9. What to agree between home and preschool

Agree on support for participation and safety, **not demands for outward obedience**.

| What to agree on | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Requesting a break | A known word, gesture or visual signal recognised by adults. |
| Warning of a transition | A brief advance explanation if this helps. |
| Comfortable space | Temporary access to a quieter place without punishment. |
| Communication | Accept gestures, AAC and words in familiar languages. |
| Refusal | Do not force hugs, eye contact or optional play. |
| Information sharing | Exchange necessary observations privately and with consent. |

If a child already has an individual support plan, coordinate changes with responsible professionals rather than replacing that plan with a downloadable worksheet.

## 10. What to do when adults strongly disagree

One person may say "The child is fine here" and another "Home is hard." These describe different circumstances, not necessarily conflicting facts.

Try these steps:

1. Agree on **one specific question**, for example the transition from free play to lunch.
2. Collect short **independent accounts** of what happened in each setting, without assumptions about intent.
3. Ask for the **child's perspective**, using words, gestures, AAC or other accessible communication.
4. Consider a **safe environmental adaptation**, such as previewing the next step or allowing refusal of an optional activity.
5. Review **comfort, choice and participation**, not only the number of crying episodes.

If difficulties continue or more complex decisions are needed, seek advice from an appropriate professional.

## 11. When the issue may be more than environmental demands

Do not automatically attribute new behaviours to personality, parenting, sensory overload or masking. Consider pain, changes in hearing or sleep, constipation, eating difficulties, anxiety, illness and unsafe interactions.

If a child reports fear of a particular person, injury, humiliation, peer aggression or other potential harm, take their communication seriously. Prioritise safety and follow local safeguarding arrangements. Do not dismiss concerns because an educator did not witness an incident, but do not assume another person's guilt without investigating facts.

If a child suddenly loses established speech, communication or motor skills, seek medical assessment without prolonged delay. See [our guide to loss of acquired skills](../child-loss-of-acquired-skills/). Immediate threats to life, serious injuries, seizures or loss of consciousness require emergency care.

## 13. Parents' frequently asked questions

### "Does being calm at preschool mean my child cannot be autistic?"

No. NICE recommends taking family observations seriously and assessing information from different settings. Behaviour in one context alone cannot confirm or rule out autism.

### "If my child cries only at home, does that mean our home is worse?"

Not necessarily. Needs and demands vary. Difficulties relating to the home, preschool or other factors can all occur. Look at specific events and the child's communication without blaming anyone in advance.

### "Can a child hide difficulties at preschool?"

Some autistic people describe masking and its effort. You cannot establish masking in an individual child just from apparent calm. Remove unnecessary demands and seek accessible ways to understand how the child feels.

### "Must rules be identical at home and preschool?"

No. Agree on core approaches for communication and safety, while adapting practical details to each environment. The child must be able to express needs and receive understandable help.

### "Should we complete an elaborate daily behaviour log?"

Not routinely. Detailed tracking may overwhelm a family. A few concrete examples with context often suffice for an initial discussion. Professionals can recommend more structured observation if necessary.

## 14. What the evidence supports and where uncertainty remains

NICE recommends integrating information across settings and adapting communication as well as physical and social surroundings. The National Autistic Society describes home-school differences and masking but does not propose diagnosing masking from a single outward sign.

ASHA emphasises family, cultural and language context and collaboration between caregivers, educators and communication professionals. There is **no single explanation** for every variation in behaviour, and no guarantee that a picture card or quiet space will resolve a particular difficulty.

This article supports noticing needs and constructive collaboration. It is not a diagnostic assessment, a formal functional behaviour analysis or a universal intervention programme.

## Key takeaways

The same child can feel and communicate differently in different environments. Compare demands, communication options and opportunities for support rather than asking where the child "behaves better". Take the child's messages seriously, discuss observations without blame and seek professional help when indicated.
