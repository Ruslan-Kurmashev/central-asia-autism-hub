## Why discuss research gaps when studies already exist?

Suppose researchers interview parents at several clinics and find substantial spending on children's support sessions. That tells us something useful about those families, but it cannot establish the average spending of every family in the country, including those who never attended a clinic or live outside major cities.

This article considers what regional autism research has already examined, which questions lack adequate answers, and which further methods may be useful to autistic people, parents and specialists.

**This is a selective editorial map of scientific questions, not a comprehensive systematic review of every study in Central Asia or an official government research programme.**

## What the first major review of regional autism research found

**Rano Zakirova-Engstrand** of Stockholm University and **Gulnoza Yakubova** of the University of Maryland reviewed empirical publications from the five Central Asian countries.

Their review was published online on **10 May 2023** and in the February **2024** issue of *Autism*. The last database search was on **8 January 2022**. They looked for peer-reviewed empirical studies in English and Russian across several international databases.

Eleven papers published in **2013-2021** met their eligibility criteria:

| Country | Papers in the historical review |
| --- | ---: |
| Kazakhstan | **10** |
| Uzbekistan | **1** |
| Kyrgyzstan | **0** |
| Tajikistan | **0** |
| Turkmenistan | **0** |

*Source: [Zakirova-Engstrand and Yakubova, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613231170553). Zero refers only to the review's criteria and dates. It does not mean no autistic people, specialists, research or published works exist in those countries.*

The authors classified papers into seven fields, including diagnosis, biology, risk factors, services and interventions. Their eligible sample contained no studies of **lifespan issues** or **epidemiological monitoring infrastructure**.

This described the research landscape **at the start of 2022**. By October 2026 the picture had changed because registry studies from Kazakhstan and Tajikistan were published after the review's search.

### Why the review could not find every single study

Conference abstracts, dissertations, review articles, inaccessible full texts and studies that did not match predefined conditions were excluded. Research in all local languages was not comprehensively searched.

Consequently, **eleven means eligible papers in that specific review**, not the total number of autism studies ever produced in Central Asia.

The authors also found no collaboration between authors based in different Central Asian countries within those eleven papers. This is a historical observation about a particular sample, **not proof of no international collaboration in 2026**.

## Six areas requiring more precise answers

![Original AutismHub conceptual diagram of six research directions: comparable population evidence, adult lives, local languages, outcomes, community involvement and regional collaboration.](/central-asia-autism-hub/images/research/central-asia-autism-research-gaps-roadmap-en.svg)

*Figure 1. Original AutismHub educational diagram. The six directions derive from the early review and later literature. Their order is not a ranking of urgency, funding or effectiveness.*

Each direction is examined through three questions: **what we know, what the evidence cannot establish and what kind of study could give a better answer**.

## Area 1. How many autistic people live in each country?

### What is already known

Kazakhstan researchers increasingly use government medical datasets. **Nukeshtayeva and colleagues** published a 2025 analysis of registry rates for **2016-2022** among children aged 0-17.

In 2026, **Baikatov and colleagues** investigated nationwide electronic records for ages **0-15**, covering **2014-2024**. Their dataset contained **214,904 child-year observations** across several neurodevelopmental diagnosis groups.

These studies show **how many diagnoses are visible to the healthcare system and how registration changed**. Neither was an independent assessment of the entire population.

### What gap remains?

Medical registers primarily cover children who reached participating services and obtained an entry. Children receiving services in private or educational settings, or not yet assessed, may be missed.

Crucially, **214,904 does not mean 214,904 distinct autistic children**. A child may appear repeatedly across years, and the dataset includes diagnostic categories other than ASD.

The authors also noted that uncertain linkage between annual identifiers prevented perfectly tracking each unique child throughout the whole observation period.

### What research might help?

**Population-based studies** should use consistent age bands, representative sampling, harmonised definitions and standardised independent professional evaluation.

Simply adding private and public register counts together is not enough: data may overlap and differ in completeness or accuracy. Sound methods should be designed before collecting figures.

For details, see [Why are autism diagnoses increasingly registered in Kazakhstan?](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/en/research/why-autism-diagnoses-are-increasing-in-kazakhstan/).

## What changed in Tajikistan after the initial review?

In 2026, **Addullozoda and colleagues** analysed official ASD registrations for children aged **0-17** across **2020-2025**.

They found a cumulative **170 registered ASD cases over six years**, with the rate increasing from **0.4 to 1.2 per 100,000 children** between 2020 and 2025. Most recorded cases came from Dushanbe.

It is a significant addition to the literature, because the 2024 scoping review included no eligible study from Tajikistan.

But this remains **registration information**, and 170 cases across six years must not be described as all autistic children living in the country on one date.

Nor do the regional differences prove that autism is inherently more common in Dushanbe. Under-identification and uneven access to diagnosis may contribute.

[Tajikistan registry analysis, 2026](https://www.vestnik-ipovszrt.tj/en/detail.php?id=106).

**Conclusion:** a historical absence of registry studies is being partly addressed, but reliable comparable population prevalence across five countries remains an open question.

## Area 2. Autistic teenagers and adults: life beyond paediatric services

Most of the studies discussed focused on children and their parents. Autism does not disappear after schooling or upon reaching adulthood.

### Which questions are still under-studied?

Planning support requires attention to:

- identification and diagnosis of autism later in adulthood
- transitions from paediatric to adult healthcare
- continuing education, job training and employment
- autonomy, living conditions, accessibility and communication
- social relationships, wellbeing and quality of life
- support for women, people with differing communication needs and adults with co-occurring conditions.

**The absence of adult-life research from a review searching to January 2022 is not proof that none existed by 2026.** It highlights a gap in the review's historical evidence base.

### How could adulthood transitions be studied?

**Longitudinal research** could, with participants' consent, examine people at multiple times, such as when leaving school and again during further study or employment.

It could address not only recorded diagnosis but **what makes continued participation in community life possible**.

Interviews and studies co-designed with autistic adults are also necessary. Their direct experiences should not automatically be replaced by the views of parents or professionals.

## Area 3. Early developmental assessment in local languages

### What has already been done?

In 2022, **Nukeshtayeva and colleagues** studied Russian and Kazakh versions of **M-CHAT-R/F** used in Central Kazakhstan.

Their final analysis involved **171 children**, with internal consistency coefficients (Cronbach's alpha) of **0.87 for Russian** and **0.93 for Kazakh**.

This helps describe properties of the questionnaires.

### Which question remains unresolved?

High internal consistency **does not mean 87% or 93% accuracy in detecting autism**. It cannot substitute for estimates of sensitivity and specificity against independent professional diagnostic evaluation.

Children in the study were **24-48 months old**, whereas the instrument's standard intended age interval is **16-30 months**. Some children therefore fell outside the original age range.

Further studies should test **question comprehension, cultural suitability, screening accuracy and referral processes**, not only translation of individual words.

### What research might help?

A well-designed study might recruit families from different language groups and urban and rural areas, follow up children with positive and negative screens, and obtain independent developmental assessment.

**Screening is not a diagnosis.** Such research should aim to improve timely referral to qualified professionals.

See [Early autism identification in Kazakhstan: what does research show?](/central-asia-autism-hub/kz/en/research/early-autism-identification-research-kazakhstan/) for more.

## Area 4. What changes after receiving support?

Identifying elevated screening risk or describing parental spending is important, but **neither automatically demonstrates the effect of a specific programme on everyday life**.

### What has already been studied?

Kazakhstan studies include family interviews, access-to-care surveys and small social-robot projects. For example, in 2025 **Imamatdinova and colleagues** reported the experiences of **390 parents** with services, expenses and support.

This helps identify family needs, not the comparative effectiveness of an educational or clinical programme.

### Which outcomes should be measured?

Depending on the objective, possible outcomes include:

| Area | Possible research outcome |
| --- | --- |
| Communication | Can a child express choices, requests and ideas with different people? |
| Education | Can a child take part in lessons and understand materials in an accessible format? |
| Daily life | Which meaningful skills and personal opportunities have become more accessible? |
| Wellbeing | How do people and families judge quality of life and acceptability of support? |
| Service access | Do waiting times decrease and does appropriate support become easier to access? |

**These are examples of research outcomes, not a pre-approved intervention programme or an official assessment scale.**

Even a study finding no benefit can be scientifically rigorous. Publishing results that **fail to confirm expected effectiveness** can help families and organisations avoid spending resources on unsupported promises.

### What study design could answer the question?

Some programmes can be tested comparatively using a published protocol and predefined endpoints. Changes to education systems might require qualitative, mixed-methods or quasi-experimental designs.

**No one method fits all questions**. The research design should be capable of testing the precise claim under consideration.

## Area 5. Who decides what researchers investigate?

Traditionally universities and professional teams plan a study, then invite autistic people and parents to complete questionnaires, be interviewed or undergo assessment.

This matters, but **participating as a respondent does not necessarily mean influencing the research agenda or key decisions**.

### What do international examples suggest?

In a Scottish study in *Autism*, **225 autistic adults** ranked research priorities. They identified mental wellbeing, diagnosis, service access, public perceptions and concerns affecting autistic women.

These findings **are not already established priorities for residents of Kazakhstan or Uzbekistan**. Different services, languages and life experiences may lead to different priorities.

Another study of the journal *Autism* showed that reporting of community involvement increased after a new editorial policy. Researchers cautioned that consultation often left decisions firmly with the academic team.

**A claim that researchers 'consulted families' is not automatically a claim of equal partnership.**

[Study of autistic adults' priorities](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11401337/) and [study of community-involvement reporting](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11816470/).

### How could communities participate in regional research?

One possible process is to:

1. Invite autistic people, parents and professionals to propose research questions.
2. Ask participants what outcomes would make the work useful to them.
3. Jointly review questionnaire language, consent and information materials.
4. Make communication accessible and provide reasonable compensation for contributors.
5. Explain which research decisions changed in response to their feedback.

The steps need adaptation to context and resources. They are **a proposed ethical approach, not an existing five-country scheme**.

## Area 6. Collaboration across five countries and responsible data use

Researchers in Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan, Tajikistan and Turkmenistan might share methods and knowledge, but **standardisation must not erase linguistic, cultural and administrative differences**.

### What is required for country comparisons?

Researchers would need to agree in advance on:

- definitions of the condition and comparable age groups
- participant recruitment and inclusion of difficult-to-reach populations
- diagnostic confirmation or specific developmental characteristics
- cultural and linguistic questionnaire adaptation
- how endpoints will be measured
- handling missing data and potential biases
- lawful sharing and protection of sensitive information.

Without these safeguards, even a large study may produce **an illusion of accuracy** through inconsistent comparisons.

### Personal information need not cross borders

Cooperation can use shared protocols, common variable definitions, aggregated de-identified findings and joint analysis plans.

Sharing identifiable medical or educational records requires separate legal, ethical and technical review. Removing names is not a guarantee against reidentification.

**The value of research never justifies needless collection or insecure release of a child's personal data.**

## Uzbekistan shows why a small study can matter

**Asaro-Saddler, Hamsho, Uzoqova and Ghazali** studied **seven mothers from Uzbekistan** in a paper published online in 2025 and included in a 2026 issue.

In a remote focus group, mothers discussed their understanding of autism and how they sought assessments for their children. The themes included lengthy diagnostic journeys and limited availability of trained professionals.

**Seven interviews cannot yield a nationally representative percentage of Uzbekistan families facing these barriers.** They can, however, identify questions for broader future studies.

[Original Uzbekistan mothers' study](https://doi.org/10.1177/15407969251343493).

A qualitative study **is not inherently poor because its sample is small**, provided its goal is to understand experiences, not estimate national prevalence.

## What does a well-formulated research question look like?

Consider these contrasts:

| Overly broad question | More testable research question |
| --- | --- |
| Why has autism increased in Central Asia? | How did the completeness of ASD registration in a defined age group change after new referral and assessment procedures? |
| Does AI technology work? | Does a particular tool improve accessible communication using predefined measures and a suitable comparison? |
| Do people treat autistic people well? | What forms of stigma and support are reported by autistic adults and families across languages and regions? |
| Does inclusion work? | Are pupils with particular needs able to participate in classes and what changes with specified adjustments? |

The right-hand questions are **examples of possible research, not reports of studies already completed**.

### Are large and expensive national studies the only ones that matter?

No. Qualitative studies can surface problems, and testing how a questionnaire has been translated may reduce measurement errors.

But the conclusions must match the design. **A small interview study is not national statistics**, just as a large register alone is not a population-based diagnostic survey.

## What this scientific map means for families

Lack of published evidence on one difficulty does not mean the child's need does not exist or should be ignored until an article appears.

Families may use research to ask professionals and service organisations:

- Who took part in studies of the proposed approach?
- Do the study conditions resemble those of our family?
- Were outcomes measured during sessions or in everyday life?
- Are limitations and cases without improvement reported?
- Did autistic people and families help define the research question?

Uncertainty requires care with claims, but **does not mean families should be denied accessible, respectful and individually appropriate support**.

## Main conclusions

**Established:** the historical review found uneven research representation across five countries. Subsequent articles examined medical registers, screening and families.

**Some historical gaps partly narrowed:** registry studies appeared in Kazakhstan and Tajikistan, though registered diagnoses remain distinct from true population prevalence.

**Outstanding questions:** adult autistic lives, language-specific screening performance, lasting support outcomes, regional service availability and meaningful involvement of autistic people in research decisions.

**Core message:** regional research needs more than additional publications. **Questions must guide methods, evidence must be transparent, and conclusions should help explain autistic people's and families' lives**.
