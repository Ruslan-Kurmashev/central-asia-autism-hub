When parents notice that their child communicates, responds to people or plays in their own way, they may wonder whether support must wait until a definitive diagnosis.

In 2021, Australian researchers published a clinical trial in *JAMA Pediatrics* in which family support began **before autism was diagnosed**. The authors followed children until age three and compared outcomes with those receiving usual services.

The study attracted attention because at age three, **6.7% of children receiving additional support** and **20.5% of children receiving usual care** met autism diagnostic criteria. Does that mean autism was prevented? No. To understand the result, we need to consider who participated, what the programme involved and how changes were assessed.

## What does early support mean?

Early support means helping a child and family when developmental concerns arise, even before diagnostic assessment is complete.

It may address communication, shared play, access to daily activities and recognition of how a child expresses needs.

Early support does not necessarily mean trying to make a child's behaviour resemble that of other children. Its goals should take account of the child's wellbeing, comfort, initiative and individual characteristics.

The authors studied one particular programme. Findings cannot automatically be applied to every approach described as early intervention.

## What question did the researchers ask?

They wanted to investigate whether additional support for interaction between parents and infants showing early differences in social development could change some developmental outcomes in subsequent years.

The researchers were interested in two main outcomes:

- How would observable behavioural characteristics used in autism assessment change with age?
- How many children would meet autism diagnostic criteria at an independent assessment around age three?

These are distinct measures. A change in a standardised score does not necessarily mean a change in diagnosis, and diagnostic classification does not describe all of a child's needs.

## How was the study conducted?

The study took place in Perth and Melbourne, Australia. Infants aged **9-14 months** were recruited after specialists identified early differences in social attention and communication.

After eligibility assessment, 104 infants were randomly assigned to two groups. One participant was excluded after allocation for not meeting the language criterion. The main analytical sample comprised 103 children.

Randomisation means assignment by chance. It helps reduce the possibility that observed group differences are due to families' baseline characteristics.

**Group 1:** children received usual services and also participated in iBASIS-VIPP.

**Group 2:** children received usual care available through local professionals.

Assessments took place at baseline, after the programme, around age two and at age three. The final assessment included 89 children, 45 from the first group and 44 from the second. Outcomes at age three were therefore not known for every originally randomised child. Missing data may affect the precision of findings.

This distinction matters: 104 children were randomised, but diagnostic outcomes at age three were available for 89.

## What was the iBASIS-VIPP programme?

VIPP stands for *Video Interaction to Promote Positive Parenting*, using video feedback to support responsive parenting.

Families were offered 10 sessions over about five months. A specialist recorded ordinary parent-child interactions, then reviewed excerpts with the parent and discussed how the child expresses interest, initiates contact and responds to communication.

The goal was not to force eye contact or suppress natural movements. The specialist helped the parent recognise the child's initiatives and respond in ways that support shared communication.

This was a structured programme with trained practitioners and a specific protocol. It is not equivalent to arbitrary home games, educational videos or self-designed therapy.

## How were outcomes assessed?

The primary outcome was change in observable characteristics associated with autism assessment across several assessment points.

During infancy, the researchers used the Autism Observation Scale for Infants (AOSI), a scale for observing early developmental characteristics. Later they used the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Second Edition (ADOS-2), a standardised observational instrument used in autism assessment.

At age three, specialists who did not know which group the child had been assigned to conducted a separate diagnostic assessment.

This approach reduces the influence of assessors' expectations. But an early diagnostic assessment cannot predict a person's entire future life course.

## Main finding: changes in observed outcomes

The authors found a statistical difference in the overall trajectory of observed behavioural characteristics. On average, iBASIS-VIPP participants showed a greater reduction in scores used in autism assessment than the usual care group.

The difference was observed through follow-up around 18 months after the programme ended, when children were three years old.

This was the primary study outcome. It was evaluated statistically across multiple assessment points, rather than from a single test result.

The authors had prespecified one-sided statistical tests, which examine an effect in a specified direction. The primary result was significant under this approach, but the authors noted that it would not reach the conventional threshold under a two-sided test.

The result is therefore important but requires careful interpretation and further independent confirmation.

## What did the diagnostic assessment at age three show?

When children were reassessed around age three, autism was diagnosed in:

- 3 of 45 children (6.7%) in the additional support group.
- 9 of 44 children (20.5%) in the usual care group.

The difference was approximately 13.8 percentage points.

In a model adjusting for certain characteristics, researchers found statistically significantly lower odds of meeting autism diagnostic criteria in the iBASIS-VIPP group.

There is another important nuance: comparison of the distribution across three diagnostic categories (autism, other developmental differences, typical development) did not show a statistically significant difference. Significance was found in a separate analysis of two categories (autism and no autism diagnosis), adjusted for covariates.

This does not negate the finding, but it prevents it being simplified into a claim that the programme demonstrably prevents autism.

## What happened to other skills?

The authors also examined communication, language development and parent-child interaction.

Some measures of parental responsiveness improved in the additional support group. Parents also reported greater gains in certain language skills and gestures.

However, not all standardised developmental measures differed significantly between groups. Differences on some language, motor and adaptive development measures remained uncertain.

Families cannot therefore be promised that the programme will necessarily accelerate speech, improve all skills or lead to a particular diagnosis.

## Can we say autism was prevented?

No. The study did not establish that conclusion.

Infants were enrolled because of early developmental characteristics, not confirmed autism diagnoses. It is impossible to know in advance which children would have received a diagnosis without intervention.

Even children who did not meet all autism criteria at age three could still have other developmental differences and support needs.

A reduction in diagnostic signs does not make someone more valuable or more normal, nor does it mean that the goal of support should be the disappearance of autistic characteristics.

Support should aim to improve communication, accessibility, safety and quality of life for children and families, rather than solely change diagnostic status.

## What does this mean for parents and professionals?

The study supports a practical point: it is not always necessary to wait for a definitive diagnosis before discussing a child's needs and available support with the family.

If there are developmental concerns, families can consult qualified professionals. They can assess communication, hearing, play, social interaction, medical characteristics and the situations in which the child experiences difficulty or comfort.

Clear, responsive communication and respect for the child's initiative can be helpful. Specific goals, methods and intensity should be chosen individually.

The study does not establish the effectiveness of all early intervention programmes and is not an instruction manual for delivering iBASIS-VIPP independently.

In Kazakhstan, the availability of trained practitioners, cultural and linguistic adaptation, costs and outcomes of local implementation would need separate investigation.

## What does the study not demonstrate?

The study has important limitations.

First, the sample was small. Although 104 children were randomised, diagnostic outcomes at age three were available for only 89.

Second, follow-up ended around age three. The study does not show children's long-term development at school, in adolescence or adulthood.

Third, some outcomes were rated by parents who knew whether they were receiving the programme. This may affect subjective ratings.

Fourth, the primary outcome analysis used one-sided statistical tests. Some other outcomes showed no convincing statistical differences.

Fifth, families participated at two Australian sites. Findings cannot automatically be generalised to all children, other healthcare systems or Kazakhstan.

Finally, the trial examined a particular programme, not sensory integration, adapted physical activity, ABA or other support approaches.

Clinical decisions require further independent research, including longer follow-up and consideration of children's and families' actual needs.

## What is worth remembering?

In an Australian randomised trial, families of infants showing early developmental differences received usual care alone or usual care with iBASIS-VIPP.

Researchers found differences in standardised behavioural scores over time. At age three, autism was diagnosed in 3 of 45 children receiving additional support and 9 of 44 receiving usual care.

These findings do not mean autism can be prevented, and they cannot guarantee a particular outcome for an individual child.

The central point for parents and professionals is that developmental support can be discussed when needs emerge, without waiting for a final diagnosis. Support should nevertheless be individualised, respectful of the child and informed by evidence of effectiveness and safety.
