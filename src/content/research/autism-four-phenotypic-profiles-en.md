Two children diagnosed with autism can be very different from one another. One may speak fluently but find it difficult to communicate with peers. The other may use alternative ways to communicate and need substantial support in everyday life.

Why are autistic characteristics so varied? Are these differences related to genetics?

In 2025, an international research team published a study that brought scientists closer to answering these questions. They examined data from more than five thousand autistic children and adolescents and identified four characteristic combinations of developmental and behavioural features.

## What did the researchers want to find out?

Autism is described as a spectrum because people's characteristics, abilities and needs can differ substantially.

But the word 'spectrum' is sometimes mistakenly understood as a single scale on which everyone can be placed, from 'mild' to 'severe' autism.

In reality, things are more complex. One person may have considerable difficulty with speech but relatively few difficulties with attention. Another may speak fluently but find it difficult to manage anxiety, changes in routine or social situations.

The researchers wanted to find out whether these characteristics formed consistent combinations and whether those combinations were associated with particular genetic patterns.

## How was the study conducted?

The researchers used data from SPARK, a large US research programme focused on autism genetics.

The main analysis included 5,392 autistic children and adolescents.

For each participant, the researchers examined 239 characteristics obtained from standardised questionnaires and developmental histories.

These included:

- communication and social interaction characteristics
- repetitive actions and interests
- attention and behaviour
- anxiety and emotional difficulties
- speech and motor skill development
- other developmental characteristics.

The researchers then used a computational statistical model.

Imagine a large table. Each row describes one child and each column contains a particular characteristic. The computer analyses the data and looks for groups of people among whom certain characteristics tend to occur together. The initial model used developmental and behavioural data, not genetic measures.

The researchers did not specify four groups in advance. They tested solutions ranging from two to ten groups and selected the one that best balanced statistical fit and interpretability.

They later evaluated the results in an independent research sample of 861 autistic people.

## What did they find?

The researchers identified four groups with different combinations of characteristics.

### 1. Moderate challenges: about 34% of participants

In this group, some of the measured communication, behavioural and developmental difficulties were, on average, less pronounced than in the other groups.

This does not mean that these participants did not need support. It refers only to comparisons within the study sample.

### 2. Social and behavioural characteristics: about 37% of participants

This group more often showed substantial difficulties with social interaction, attention, emotional regulation and behaviour. Pronounced early developmental delays were not characteristic of the group as a whole.

The researchers also found a higher prevalence of certain co-occurring conditions, including ADHD, anxiety and depressive disorders.

### 3. Autism with developmental delays: about 19% of participants

This group was characterised by more pronounced delays in some developmental milestones, including speech and motor skill acquisition.

Recorded intellectual and motor impairments were more common among participants in this group.

### 4. Broadly affected: about 10% of participants

This group had higher scores for difficulties across several measured areas at once.

Combinations of communication, developmental and behavioural characteristics and co-occurring conditions were more frequent.

The four groups in the published version of the study comprised 1,860, 1,976, 1,002 and 554 people respectively. These correspond to approximately 34%, 37%, 19% and 10% of participants. The counts for the first two groups were reversed in an earlier preprint. The figures given here come from the final peer-reviewed publication in *Nature Genetics* (2025).

These names describe average patterns within research groups. The percentages apply to the participants in this study, not all autistic people in the world or in Kazakhstan. The groups are not four official diagnoses and are not intended for independently classifying children.

## How is genetics related to these differences?

After analysing participants' characteristics, the researchers turned to genetic data. Individual genetic comparisons included fewer people than the original 5,392 participants because the required genetic information was not available for everyone.

Genes are regions of DNA containing instructions needed for the development and functioning of the body.

Everyone has many genetic variants. Some are inherited from parents, while others arise for the first time during the formation of a child. These newly arising changes are called *de novo* variants.

The researchers found that the identified groups differed in some genetic patterns as well as in behaviour and development.

For example, certain rare genetic changes that had newly arisen in the child were more frequently observed in the broadly affected group.

In the group with developmental delays, other combinations of rare inherited and newly arising variants were found.

The researchers also examined the stages of brain development during which the genes related to these variants are typically active. Some patterns were associated with genes particularly active before birth, while others involved genes more active after birth.

These findings help scientists formulate new hypotheses about why autistic people's development may follow different trajectories.

However, the associations do not mean that DNA analysis can already predict an individual child's abilities, development or future.

## Why does this finding matter?

The main value of the study lies not in the number four itself, but in the identification of statistical associations between combinations of developmental characteristics and genetic variants.

Earlier studies often considered individual characteristics, for example speech difficulties alone or intellectual development alone.

In this study, researchers attempted to consider the person more comprehensively, taking many characteristics into account at once.

This approach may help explain why genetic studies sometimes produce different results and why autistic people's needs are so diverse.

In the future, similar research may contribute to more individualised approaches to developmental assessment and support.

That is a direction for future research, not an established tool for clinical practice.

## What does this mean for parents and professionals?

Imagine two autistic children of the same age.

The first needs additional communication support but can manage many everyday tasks independently.

The second needs alternative communication tools, help developing daily living skills and adaptations to the educational environment.

Both may have the same diagnosis, but their individual needs will differ.

That is why support should be informed by the person's abilities, difficulties, preferences, living circumstances and goals, not just the diagnosis itself.

The study helps us understand the scientific basis for this individualised approach.

The four research groups cannot be used to select a therapy, determine a prognosis or prescribe genetic testing. Their practical clinical applicability has not yet been established.

## What does the study not yet establish?

Despite the large dataset and the evaluation of results in an independent sample, the study has limitations.

First, the main sample included children and adolescents from a US research programme. The findings cannot automatically be generalised to all autistic people, including adults and people living in other countries.

Second, a substantial proportion of the information came from questionnaires and parent reports. Such data are useful, but may depend on how different people understand and assess a child's characteristics.

Third, the groups were created by a statistical model. Other studies using different characteristics or analytical methods may identify a different number of groups.

Finally, the associations between genetic variants and developmental characteristics do not establish that each identified variant directly causes the observed difficulties.

Further research with more diverse groups, long-term developmental follow-up and independent verification of the results is therefore needed.

## What to remember

Autism encompasses a spectrum of developmental characteristics, not one uniform set of features or a simple scale of difficulty.

The 2025 study found recurring combinations of developmental and behavioural characteristics in autistic children that may be associated with different genetic patterns.

This is a step towards a deeper understanding of the diversity of autism.

For parents and professionals, the main message is that a diagnosis alone does not tell us everything about a person. Understanding their needs requires consideration of their individual development, communication, behaviour, abilities and environment.
