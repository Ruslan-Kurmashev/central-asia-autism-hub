Imagine two children who experience communication difficulties, considerable fatigue after school or unusual sensory reactions. One is referred for autism assessment at an early age. For the other, these experiences are explained for a long time as shyness, anxiety or personality traits.

Why can the age at which autism is identified differ? And do girls and women sometimes receive a diagnosis later?

In 2024, researchers published a study in the journal *Autism* comparing **three independent samples of autistic people**. In two of these samples, they found statistically significant differences in diagnostic timing. But it is important to understand exactly what the researchers measured and why the same conclusion cannot be applied to every girl.

## Why does age at diagnosis matter?

A diagnosis does not determine someone's value or fully describe their abilities. However, timely and thorough developmental assessment may help a family understand a child's needs and obtain appropriate support.

When someone's characteristics remain unrecognised for a long time, it may be harder for them to explain their difficulties to others, find suitable educational conditions or access necessary services.

In previous studies, later diagnosis was also associated with certain mental health problems. An association does not mean that the later diagnosis itself caused these problems. Many circumstances may contribute.

## What did the researchers want to find out?

The authors noted that many publications compare age at diagnosis for boys and girls but do not always distinguish clearly between two concepts:

- **Sex assigned at birth.** This is usually recorded in medical documentation when a child is born.
- **Gender identity.** How a person identifies their own gender.

The researchers also examined **gender diversity**, referring to situations in which a person's gender identity differs from the sex assigned at birth or falls outside the two conventional gender categories.

These concepts are not synonyms. By analysing them separately, the authors sought to understand differences in diagnostic timing more precisely.

The study was **not** designed to establish that sex or gender identity causes autism.

## How was the study conducted?

The researchers compared three groups of autistic people in the United States. The groups differed in age and how participants were recruited.

**First group: GENDAAR, 193 people.** Children and adolescents aged 8-18 years enrolled in a research programme. This group was recruited according to specific criteria and was not a random sample of all autistic children.

**Second group: Children's National Hospital, 1,550 people.** Patients at a specialist clinic, approximately 1-25 years old. Clinical information on age at diagnosis was used.

**Third group: SPARK, 244 people.** Adult members of a research community who were approximately 18-30 years old when they participated.

Across the three samples there were **1,987 people** (193 + 1,550 + 244). However, individual comparisons only included records containing the relevant information. Consequently, 1,987 is not the denominator for every statistical test. Researchers did not make a new diagnosis for everyone. They examined information about **the age at which autism had previously been diagnosed** and compared that measure between groups.

Analyses considered sex assigned at birth, gender identity and gender diversity separately. Not all comparisons were possible in every sample.

## What did the researchers find?

### 1. In the clinical sample, girls and women were diagnosed later

In the Children's National Hospital sample, the average age at diagnosis for people assigned female at birth was **8.67 years**. For those assigned male at birth, the average was **7.41 years**.

The difference was approximately **1.26 years** and was statistically significant.

These are averages for a particular clinical sample, not an established period of diagnostic delay for every girl.

### 2. In the first research sample, the difference was not conclusive

In GENDAAR, the average age at diagnosis was approximately **6.25 years** for those assigned female at birth and **5.58 years** for those assigned male at birth.

The difference was approximately eight months but **was not statistically significant**.

This is an important part of the findings. Scientific research requires reporting not only differences that support a hypothesis but also situations where convincing evidence was not found.

### 3. Two samples showed differences associated with gender identity

In the clinical and adult samples, the authors also found that participants identifying as female were diagnosed later on average than participants identifying as male.

Separate comparisons showed later diagnosis among gender-diverse participants than among cisgender participants. The term **cisgender** describes someone whose gender identity corresponds to their sex assigned at birth.

In the clinical sample, the average age at diagnosis was approximately **13.01 years** for gender-diverse participants compared with **7.33 years** for cisgender participants. In the adult SPARK sample, the corresponding averages were **16.73 and 12.73 years**.

However, the groups differed substantially in age and recruitment methods. These figures cannot therefore be interpreted as evidence that gender identity itself causes diagnostic delay.

## Why did the findings differ across the three samples?

Imagine two studies. One involves children who have been followed in a specialist programme for a long time. The other involves people who sought assessment themselves in adulthood.

The average age at diagnosis in these groups is likely to differ even without any biological differences between their participants.

This is why the authors compared independent samples and specifically discussed **how participants were recruited**.

If certain people are more likely to access specialist services only when they experience substantial difficulties, that may influence results. If people who have long searched for an explanation of their characteristics join an adult programme, that also affects the averages.

Such studies help identify inequalities in access to diagnosis, but do not automatically establish their causes.

## Does this mean autism in girls is always hidden?

No. Autism in girls and women is as diverse as it is in boys and men.

In some children, communication or behavioural characteristics are apparent early. In others, they may be less obvious to people around them, occur alongside anxiety or differ between school and home.

Researchers sometimes discuss **masking**, when a person consciously or unconsciously tries to meet social expectations, for example rehearsing conversations in advance or suppressing noticeable reactions. This is an important concept in autism research.

However, the study by McQuaid and colleagues **did not directly test whether differences in age at diagnosis were caused by masking**. Nor does it prove that all girls are better at concealing autistic characteristics or that there is one distinct female type of autism.

A scientific hypothesis should not be turned into a rule for every child.

## Why is this finding important?

The study reminds professionals that the circumstances in which people undergo developmental assessment may affect how promptly autism is identified.

If assessment is built around only a few familiar examples of behaviour, some people may be missed. It is therefore important to consider **the full developmental history and individual characteristics**, rather than conformity to a stereotypical presentation.

The study also shows why sex assigned at birth, gender identity and participant recruitment should be distinguished in scientific research. Otherwise, different sources of variation may be combined in one measure.

## What does this mean for parents and professionals?

If a child has persistent communication difficulties, sensory differences, repetitive patterns of behaviour or other developmental concerns, it is worth discussing them with a professional regardless of the child's sex.

It is useful to consider **how the child manages everyday life**, not just how apparent their characteristics are to other people.

For example, a child may diligently follow school rules but experience substantial fatigue from social interaction and noise. This situation does not in itself demonstrate autism, but deserves a careful discussion about the reasons for discomfort and possible support.

Information from different settings, such as home, school and other important situations, may be useful during assessment. Decisions about diagnosis are made by qualified professionals following a comprehensive assessment.

In Kazakhstan, access to diagnosis for girls and women particularly merits separate investigation. The US study did not measure diagnostic timing among residents of Kazakhstan.

## What does the study not demonstrate?

The study has important limitations.

**First**, the analysis included only people who had already received an autism diagnosis. It does not show how many girls, boys or adults remain undiagnosed.

**Second**, the three samples differed substantially in age, recruitment procedures and assessment settings. The findings should not be combined into a single universal estimate of diagnostic delay.

**Third**, information on gender identity was collected in different ways. Some age and gender categories contained few participants.

**Fourth**, the study was not an experiment. It identified associations but could not determine which particular circumstance caused later diagnosis.

**Finally**, all samples were recruited in the United States. Their findings do not necessarily describe other healthcare and education systems.

## What is worth remembering?

Researchers compared information from three groups of autistic people. In two samples, they identified later diagnoses among some groups of participants assigned female at birth and gender-diverse participants.

In the first sample, however, they did not find conclusive differences, and the reasons behind the observed patterns require further research.

**Main point for parents and professionals:** the likelihood that someone may need developmental assessment should not be determined solely by a stereotypical image of autism or by a child's sex. Individual characteristics deserve attention and everyone should have access to qualified assessment.
