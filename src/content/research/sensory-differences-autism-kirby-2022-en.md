Imagine a room with ordinary lights, someone talking and traffic audible outside. One person barely notices the sound and light. Another may find them distracting, exhausting or extremely uncomfortable.

Differences in the perception of sounds, light, touch and other sensations are common in autism. But how common are they? And how do researchers investigate these experiences?

In 2022, researchers published a study drawing on records of 25,627 autistic children. Sensory differences were documented for nearly three quarters of participants. It is worth understanding what that figure actually represents.

## What is sensory perception?

Our brains continuously receive signals from the senses and other bodily systems. We hear sounds, see light and movement, experience touch, smell and taste, and sense our body position and movements.

Sensory processing describes how the nervous system receives these signals, combines information and helps us respond to what is happening.

People may experience the same stimuli differently. For example, a vacuum cleaner may sound ordinary to one person but unpleasant or excessively loud to another.

Sensory differences are not a measure of intelligence and do not mean that someone is deliberately misbehaving. They can have a substantial effect on everyday life.

## What sensory differences occur in autism?

Several patterns of sensory responses are commonly described. They can coexist in one person and vary according to the situation.

### Increased sensitivity

Some sounds, lights, fabrics or types of touch may feel especially intense and cause discomfort.

For example, a child may cover their ears in a noisy dining hall or avoid clothing with rough seams. This is an example of a possible response, not a universal sign of autism.

### Reduced sensitivity

A person may notice certain sensations less readily or respond to them differently from what others expect.

For example, they may not immediately respond to touch or may show less noticeable reactions to certain temperatures. However, a lack of response is not always explained by sensory differences. Possible pain, hearing impairment or another medical condition requires appropriate assessment.

### Sensory seeking

Some people actively seek particular sensations, such as movement, pressure, textures or visual effects.

For example, a child may enjoy handling objects with different surfaces or rocking. Sensory seeking is not inherently a problem. What matters is whether it affects safety, comfort or necessary activities.

Importantly, Kirby and colleagues did not measure the prevalence of these three patterns separately. They are basic concepts useful for understanding the topic.

## What did the researchers want to find out?

Sensory differences had already been described in numerous smaller studies and are included in contemporary autism diagnostic criteria.

The authors asked a different question: how often were sensory differences noted in records for a large group of autistic children?

They also wanted to determine whether documentation of sensory differences was associated with age, sex, other developmental difficulties and everyday functioning.

This study did not aim to determine which sounds or types of touch each child disliked. It investigated patterns across a large collection of records.

## How was the study conducted?

Researchers used data from the US Autism and Developmental Disabilities Monitoring Network (ADDM). Its specialists analyse medical records and, in most regions, educational records to investigate children's developmental characteristics.

The study included records for 25,627 autistic children in several US regions. There were two age groups: 2,913 four-year-olds and 22,714 eight-year-olds. Researchers used surveillance data from 2006-2014.

They checked whether records contained a description of unusual responses to sensory stimuli. This could include:

- pronounced sensitivity to sensations
- reduced responses to certain stimuli
- unusually strong interest in particular sensations.

The researchers then compared the proportion of children with these records and examined statistical associations with other characteristics.

An important limitation of this method is that researchers did not invite all 25,000 children for a new sensory assessment. They worked with existing documents. An undocumented characteristic was not necessarily absent.

## Main finding: sensory differences were documented for 74% of children

Medical and educational records documented sensory differences for 18,968 of 25,627 children, or 74%. The article reports a 95% confidence interval of 73.5% to 74.5% for this estimate in the studied sample. That reflects statistical precision, not applicability across all countries or ages.

The corresponding figure was 70.1% for four-year-olds and 74.5% for eight-year-olds.

In other words, professionals had previously recorded unusual sensory responses for about three of every four participants.

This figure should not be interpreted as the exact prevalence of sensory differences among all autistic people. It concerns a particular US sample of children and characteristics that were documented.

The study also did not follow the same group of children from age four to eight. Different groups were compared, so these figures do not establish that sensory sensitivity necessarily increases with age.

## What were sensory differences associated with?

The authors found that documented sensory differences more often co-occurred with documented difficulties in several areas:

- sleep
- eating and feeding
- attention and increased activity
- emotional responses and fears
- motor development
- adaptive behaviour, meaning skills needed in everyday life.

For example, sound or light may interfere with a child's sleep, while uncomfortable sensations in the mouth can sometimes make eating harder. These are everyday examples of possible associations.

The study did not establish that sensory differences themselves cause difficulties with sleep, eating or behaviour. Related phenomena may affect one another or have additional causes.

Interestingly, cognitive scores were not significantly associated with documented sensory differences in the final model. Sensory differences should not be treated as a sign of a particular level of intelligence.

## Why might some sensory differences go unnoticed?

Researchers identified differences in how frequently sensory differences were documented across some demographic groups.

In particular, records were somewhat less common for girls and for several groups with different sociodemographic characteristics.

The authors highlighted possible differences in access to assessment, completeness of documentation and professional attention to particular characteristics.

This does not establish that girls or children from particular backgrounds experience such sensations less often for biological reasons. Differences in records may reflect differences in identification and documentation.

For Kazakhstan, this is a useful research question: how consistently do professionals ask about children's sensory needs, and how does access to services affect identification? The US study itself does not answer these questions about Kazakhstan.

## What does this finding mean for parents and professionals?

The main practical relevance is that sensory differences are common enough to merit attention when assessing children's daily difficulties.

If a child avoids a situation, it is helpful to investigate what specifically causes discomfort first. It may be noise, bright light, unexpected touch, a difficult task, anxiety, pain or several factors together.

Depending on the person's needs, reasonable environmental adjustments may help, such as access to a quiet space, advance notice of loud sounds, more comfortable clothing or suitable lighting.

These are examples of individual accommodations, not methods whose effectiveness was tested by Kirby and colleagues.

The findings also do not establish the effectiveness of a particular sensory therapy programme. If a specialised intervention is being considered, its goals, evidence of effectiveness, safety and the child's own preferences need separate consideration.

## What does the study not demonstrate?

Despite its large sample, the study has limitations.

First, data came from records rather than direct standardised sensory assessment of all participants.

Second, sensory differences were recorded as one combined variable. This did not permit precise comparisons of sensitivity to sound, light, touch or other types of sensation separately.

Third, the absence of a record does not mean the absence of a characteristic. The measure may depend on access to assessment and completeness of documentation.

Fourth, the analysis used US data from 2006-2014. The findings cannot be applied to adults, all countries or Kazakhstan without further investigation.

Fifth, statistical associations with behaviour, sleep and eating do not establish the causes of these difficulties.

## What is worth remembering?

In a study of more than 25,000 autistic children, sensory differences were documented for 74% of participants. This illustrates the importance of considering sensory experiences when discussing development and everyday support.

Sensory responses can take different forms: a person may avoid certain stimuli, notice them less readily or actively seek particular sensations.

For parents and professionals, the main point is not to demand identical responses from every child, but to understand individual needs, reasons for discomfort and ways to make the environment more accessible and safe.
