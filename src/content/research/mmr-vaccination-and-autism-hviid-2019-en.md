Parents sometimes notice developmental differences around the age when routine vaccinations are given. When events occur close together in time, they may wonder whether they are related.

In 2019, Danish researchers published a study of 657,461 children in *Annals of Internal Medicine*. They investigated whether vaccination against measles, mumps and rubella was associated with an increased likelihood of an autism diagnosis.

No increased risk was detected. Here is how the researchers reached that conclusion and what can and cannot be learned from a single study.

## Which vaccine was studied?

The vaccine against measles, mumps and rubella is known as MMR. In Russian-language sources it is also abbreviated as КПК.

It is a combination vaccine that helps the immune system develop protection against three infections.

Measles can lead to pneumonia and serious neurological complications. Mumps can sometimes cause complications, including inflammation of the testes and nervous system involvement. Rubella is particularly dangerous during pregnancy because it can cause serious fetal developmental problems.

Hviid and colleagues investigated MMR specifically, not every vaccine, medical product or the entire vaccination programme at once.

## Why did questions arise about vaccination and autism?

Early autistic characteristics often become noticeable during early childhood. In many countries, routine vaccinations take place during this period.

When a child receives a vaccine and is diagnosed with autism some time later, the events may seem related. But their order in time does not establish that the first caused the second.

In 1998, a small study suggesting an association between MMR and autism was published. Serious problems with the work were subsequently identified and the journal retracted the publication.

The question continued to be investigated in much larger and better-designed studies. Hviid and colleagues' work was an important part of this investigation.

## What exactly did the researchers want to test?

The authors asked three questions:

- Is an autism diagnosis more common among children who received MMR than those who did not?
- Is there an increased risk in groups of children who already have other factors associated with autism, such as an autistic sibling?
- Do more diagnoses occur shortly after vaccination when different time intervals are examined?

They therefore investigated the overall statistical association and the hypothesis that MMR might affect particular groups or trigger autism soon after vaccination.

## How was the study conducted?

The researchers used Danish national registries, which contain information on births, vaccinations, medical diagnoses and other characteristics of residents.

The cohort comprised 657,461 children born between 1999 and the end of 2010.

Children were followed from one year of age, with follow-up ending on 31 August 2013. Follow-up duration differed because children were born in different years.

Researchers linked MMR vaccination records with the dates of autism diagnoses. Total follow-up amounted to 5,025,754 person-years.

Person-years represent the combined duration of observation across individuals. For example, following 100 people for two years gives 200 person-years. This is not the number of participants.

During follow-up, 6,517 children received an autism diagnosis.

## Why not simply compare the groups without adjustments?

Children who did and did not receive MMR may differ in ways other than vaccination.

For example, sex, parental age, perinatal characteristics and the presence of other autistic family members may affect the likelihood and timing of autism identification.

Diagnostic practices and medical follow-up may also change over time.

The authors therefore used a statistical model accounting for age, sex, year of birth, other childhood vaccinations, autistic siblings and several additional factors associated with the likelihood of diagnosis.

Such adjustments improve the comparison, although an observational study cannot guarantee that every source of difference has been eliminated.

## Main finding: no increased risk after MMR was detected

After adjustment, researchers obtained a hazard ratio of 0.93, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.85 to 1.02.

The measure compares rates of autism diagnosis over time among MMR-vaccinated and unvaccinated children, accounting for the specified factors.

A hazard ratio of exactly 1 would correspond to no difference between groups in the model.

The estimate of 0.93 was slightly below 1. This does not establish that MMR protects against autism. The confidence interval includes 1, so the data are compatible with no statistically significant difference.

The scientific conclusion is that this very large cohort provided no evidence of an increased risk of autism diagnosis after MMR vaccination.

## What if a family already has an autistic child?

This question is particularly important for parents with a child who already has an autism diagnosis.

Researchers separately examined children with autistic siblings and children with other characteristics statistically associated with a higher likelihood of diagnosis.

They found no consistent evidence of increased risk following MMR in these subgroups either.

This does not mean that a child with an autistic sibling has no genetic or other factors associated with autism. The study did not support the hypothesis that MMR adds to that risk.

As with any subgroup analysis, estimates may be less precise when fewer participants are included.

## Did researchers examine the period immediately following vaccination?

Yes. They analysed different time intervals after MMR vaccination separately.

If vaccination caused autism after a particular interval, one might expect a statistical increase in diagnoses in that interval.

The authors found no consistent clustering of diagnoses after vaccination that supported this hypothesis.

This is distinct from individual family experiences. In a large population, some diagnoses will inevitably occur shortly after vaccination simply because both events occur in childhood.

To establish causation, researchers must test whether this occurs more often than expected and whether the pattern is reproduced in well-designed studies.

## How should the figure of 6,517 be understood?

During follow-up, 6,517 of 657,461 children received an autism diagnosis.

This does not represent the number of children who supposedly developed autism because of vaccination. It includes diagnoses throughout follow-up regardless of vaccination status.

The proportion among all participants should also not be treated as current autism prevalence in Denmark or Kazakhstan. The sample, years of birth, follow-up duration and diagnostic system matter.

The main finding was based on group comparisons accounting for time and other factors, not a simple proportion of diagnoses.

## What do other large studies say?

Hviid and colleagues' findings should be considered alongside other evidence.

In 2021, an updated Cochrane systematic review (updating the 2020 review) examined MMR and MMRV vaccines and their safety. The authors found no evidence supporting an association between these vaccinations and autism. The combined evidence did not establish an increased autism risk after MMR.

In December 2025, the World Health Organization reported a new assessment by its Global Advisory Committee on Vaccine Safety. The committee examined research published through August 2025 and reaffirmed that high-quality evidence does not support a causal relationship between vaccination and autism.

Hviid's study concerns MMR. The WHO assessment examines a wider body of research on different vaccines. These are distinct levels of evidence.

## Does this mean vaccines have no side effects at all?

No. The absence of a link with autism does not mean that vaccines never cause adverse reactions.

Brief reactions such as injection-site pain, fever or rash may occur after MMR. Rare, more serious adverse reactions are investigated through vaccine safety surveillance.

The frequency of reactions, contraindications and appropriate use of a particular product should be considered using official product information and national clinical guidance.

Adverse reactions and autism must be considered separately. Evidence about possible MMR side effects does not establish that the vaccine causes autism.

## Why does this matter for parents and professionals?

Parents sometimes postpone vaccination because they are concerned that it may affect their child's development.

High-quality studies, including the Danish study of more than 650,000 children, do not establish an increased autism risk after MMR.

Developmental concerns should be discussed with a qualified professional in a timely manner, regardless of which vaccines the child has received.

Developmental support should not be postponed while an alleged association with vaccination is investigated, because the study gives no reason to consider MMR a cause of autism.

Questions about contraindications, previous reactions or the vaccination schedule should be discussed with a qualified healthcare professional, considering individual circumstances and current requirements in Kazakhstan.

## What limitations does the study have?

Despite the very large sample, the work has limitations.

First, this was an observational rather than a randomised study. Children were not randomly allocated to vaccination or no vaccination.

Second, national registries were used. Researchers did not independently re-examine every individual medical record or autism diagnosis.

Third, many factors were accounted for, but not every possible difference between vaccinated and unvaccinated children could be excluded.

Fourth, follow-up duration differed. Children born closer to 2010 had less time before the end of the study to receive a diagnosis at an older age.

Fifth, the study concerned Denmark and specific birth years. It did not directly examine vaccine coverage, diagnostic practices or health-system characteristics in Kazakhstan.

Finally, statistical data cannot establish the absolute impossibility of every conceivable tiny effect. However, the large cohort showed no increased risk, and the overall body of high-quality evidence supports the absence of a causal association between MMR and autism.

## What is worth remembering?

In a Danish study of 657,461 children, the researchers found no increased risk of autism diagnosis following measles, mumps and rubella vaccination.

Subgroup analyses found no increased risk among children with autistic siblings and did not support clustering of diagnoses in specified periods after vaccination.

The findings are consistent with independent systematic reviews and the updated WHO assessment.

Scientific evidence does not support the claim that MMR vaccination causes autism. Vaccination decisions should be informed by reliable information on infection prevention, contraindications and known adverse reactions, while developmental concerns should be assessed separately.
