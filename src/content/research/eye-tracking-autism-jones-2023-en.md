Can we understand how a child experiences the world by observing where they look? Specialised cameras can now record eye movements with high precision. Researchers are investigating whether such measurements can help assess autism early in life.

In 2023, a study in *JAMA* compared measurements of gaze direction with comprehensive diagnostic assessment in 475 young children.

The findings are promising but have important limitations. The test missed some children with autism, and some children without autism had positive results. It is a potential supplementary tool, not a replacement for clinicians and psychologists.

## What is eye tracking?

Eye tracking is a technology that determines where a person looks and how their gaze moves.

Systems usually use cameras and infrared light sources to measure eye position relative to a screen. Software then calculates which parts of an image a person looks at and for how long.

For example, when a scene of people talking appears on the screen, researchers can measure how much time a child spends looking at faces, movements and other objects.

Importantly, gaze direction is not equivalent to the desire to communicate, intelligence or the ability to understand another person.

## Why study visual attention to social information?

From early infancy, children attend to faces, movements and interactions between people. Each child develops attention in their own way.

Several studies have identified group differences in how autistic and non-autistic children distribute visual attention when viewing social scenes.

Researchers call this social visual engagement. Jones and colleagues examined numerous brief responses to specially prepared videos, rather than a single instance of eye contact.

The test measures statistical patterns in gaze distribution, not the ability to look someone in the eyes. Lack of eye contact alone is not evidence of autism.

## What question did the researchers ask?

The authors wanted to know how closely automated gaze measurements agreed with diagnoses made by experienced specialists using accepted procedures.

They also investigated whether gaze patterns were associated with clinical assessments of communication, social development and certain cognitive skills.

The study did not seek a universal sign of autism in every child. It tested a specific technology in specific clinical settings.

## How was the study conducted?

The researchers enrolled 499 children aged 16-30 months referred for autism assessment at six US specialty clinics.

For 475 children (95.2% of those enrolled), researchers obtained eye-tracking data meeting quality requirements. Only these measurements were included in the primary accuracy analysis.

Participants underwent two independent assessments.

**First assessment: clinical diagnosis.** Experienced specialists examined developmental history, observed the child and used standardised instruments including ADOS-2 and the Mullen Scales of Early Learning. They determined whether the child met autism diagnostic criteria using all the information.

**Second assessment: automated gaze test.** The child watched videos while the device recorded gaze direction. An algorithm compared the data with prespecified characteristics and produced a test result.

The study was double-blind: clinicians did not know the device results, and the eye-tracking team did not know the clinical conclusions.

This reduced the risk of one team's expectations influencing the other team's assessments.

## Main finding: how accurate was the test?

Expert assessment classified 221 of 475 children as having autism and 254 as not having autism.

Compared with these conclusions, the technology showed:

- Sensitivity of 71.0%. Among children classified as autistic by experts, the test correctly identified about 71 in every 100.
- Specificity of 80.7%. Among children classified as not autistic by experts, the test correctly returned a negative result for about 81 in every 100.

Both measures matter, but neither represents perfect accuracy.

At 71% sensitivity, about 29 of every 100 autistic children could receive a negative test result.

At 80.7% specificity, about 19 of every 100 non-autistic children could receive a positive test result.

These are approximate explanations of percentages in the clinical sample, not predictions of errors among all children in Kazakhstan.

## Why was performance better in part of the sample?

After the clinical assessment, specialists rated how certain they were of their diagnosis.

For 335 children, clinicians were certain. In this group, sensitivity was 78.0% and specificity was 85.4%.

For the remaining 140 children, clinicians reported diagnostic uncertainty. Performance was lower: sensitivity 56.3% and specificity 68.1%. These subgroup results must not be confused with measures for the full sample of 475 children.

In cases where clinical diagnosis was less clear, automated gaze measurement also agreed less closely with the reference assessment.

This is particularly important for parents. The technology cannot be presented as a device that resolves every difficult diagnostic case without error.

## What do sensitivity and specificity mean?

These terms are often confused with the probability of a diagnosis for an individual child.

Sensitivity describes how well a test detects people in whom the condition is established by the chosen reference method.

Specificity describes how well the test identifies people in whom that condition is not established.

A positive test does not mean that the probability of autism is necessarily 71% or 80.7%.

The meaning of a positive result also depends on how common autism is among the children being assessed. A specialty clinic and routine screening of every child in a preschool are very different situations.

Clinical study results therefore cannot be transferred directly to population-wide screening.

## Which other assessments were associated with test results?

Researchers also compared gaze measurements with standardised assessment results.

They found statistical associations with ADOS-2 social measures and verbal and nonverbal ability scores on the Mullen scales.

However, correlation does not make gaze measurement a substitute for comprehensive developmental assessment. These associations describe patterns in the study sample, not the abilities and needs of every child from one measure.

Understanding someone's everyday life still requires observation, discussion with the family and assessment of skills across situations.

## Could the test help children receive assessment sooner?

Automated measurements took relatively little time. The authors reported that results could be obtained in less than 30 minutes after data collection.

This is a potential advantage in specialty clinics with pressure on diagnostic services.

But a short procedure is not equivalent to a diagnosis in 30 minutes. In this study children underwent comprehensive clinical assessment separately from automated gaze measurement.

The study did not establish that implementation would shorten diagnostic waiting times or improve access to services in Kazakhstan. Separate real-world studies would be needed.

## What should be considered when interpreting the results?

Participants were children already referred to specialty clinics, not the general population.

Many had other developmental difficulties, making clinical diagnosis challenging. Some children without autism also had substantial language or other developmental delays.

The study included only children in a specific age range who met eligibility criteria, including vision and hearing requirements.

Performance may therefore differ in older children, general clinics or groups with other developmental characteristics.

Accuracy in Kazakhstan was not established. Language, cultural context, video stimuli, equipment and diagnostic organisation may influence results.

## Did the authors have commercial interests?

Yes. This is important information when discussing diagnostic technology.

Some study authors disclosed links to EarliTec Diagnostics, the company to which the technology was licensed. Warren Jones and Ami Klin reported involvement in establishing the company, equity interests and consulting activity. Technology-related patents were also disclosed.

Commercial interests do not in themselves invalidate the results. But they warrant careful assessment and independent replication.

The disclosures are available in the original *JAMA* article.

## What does this mean for parents and professionals?

If developmental concerns arise, families do not need to wait for or seek access to specialised gaze-tracking equipment before obtaining professional assessment.

When autism is suspected, diagnosis should be based on comprehensive assessment of developmental history, observation, communication, behaviour and medical information.

A positive eye-tracking result is not itself a diagnosis, and a negative result does not rule out autism.

Children should not be forced to make eye contact to pass such an assessment. The study did not examine the effectiveness of doing so or propose it as support.

## What does the study not demonstrate?

The study has several important limitations.

First, the test was evaluated in six US specialty clinics. Population-wide screening was not studied.

Second, usable data could not be obtained from 24 of the 499 children enrolled. This may matter for real-world implementation.

Third, test performance was substantially lower when clinicians were uncertain about the diagnosis than when they were certain.

Fourth, the reference was expert clinical diagnosis, which itself can be difficult in early childhood. The study did not establish an absolute biological truth from eye measurements.

Fifth, it did not show that the technology can independently replace ADOS-2, a specialist or comprehensive assessment.

Sixth, some authors' disclosed commercial interests underline the need for independent evaluation and careful examination of actual clinical benefits.

## What is worth remembering?

Eye tracking helps researchers objectively measure how children distribute visual attention while viewing social scenes.

In a study of 475 children the technology achieved 71.0% sensitivity and 80.7% specificity relative to expert clinical conclusions. This is promising, but some results were incorrect.

For parents and professionals, eye tracking may become a supplementary assessment tool. A single gaze-measurement result cannot reliably confirm or exclude autism, and professional assessment remains necessary.
