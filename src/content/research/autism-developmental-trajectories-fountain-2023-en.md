When a child is diagnosed with autism, parents often wonder how their development will change in five, ten or twenty years.

Some children gradually acquire new ways of communicating. Others develop certain skills more slowly. New difficulties may arise during adolescence, but new opportunities may emerge as well.

Can researchers identify common patterns in these changes?

In 2023, researchers published a study in *Pediatrics* based on records spanning many years for more than 71,000 autistic people. They examined how recorded measures of communication and social interaction changed from childhood into adolescence or young adulthood.

The findings showed several different developmental trajectories rather than a single common one.

## What is a developmental trajectory?

Imagine a road on a map. You can mark how a person's position changes over time.

In the study, the road was represented by successive evaluations of skills. For example, professionals recorded the extent to which a person used words to communicate and participated in interactions with others.

Connecting the results of several evaluations gives a trajectory, a description of changes with age.

A trajectory is not a person's destiny and says nothing about their worth. It shows how a particular measure changed within a specific system of observation.

## What did the researchers want to find out?

The authors asked three main questions:

- What recurring patterns of change in spoken communication occur among autistic people?
- How do recorded measures of social interaction change with age?
- Are different trajectories associated with living circumstances, access to services and sociodemographic characteristics?

The researchers did not attempt to determine which participants were 'more' or 'less' autistic. They examined two areas of functioning that may change throughout life.

## How was the study conducted?

The authors used records from the California Department of Developmental Services (DDS), a state service system for people with developmental disabilities in California.

The study included people with a recorded autism diagnosis who were born in 1992-2016. Service records were linked with birth records, and observations continued until November 2019.

To study development over time, the researchers included only people with at least four evaluations.

The overall sample reported in the abstract comprised 71,285 people. Records containing the required measures were used to model trajectories: 71,222 people in the communication analysis and 71,184 in the social interaction analysis. Sample sizes can therefore differ across tables and models, and 'more than 71,000' refers to the overall sample.

A separate analysis of associations with sociodemographic characteristics used records for 67,888 people with the required information.

The duration of follow-up varied. Some participants had records spanning many years, but this does not mean that every individual was followed from early childhood to age 27.

## What exactly did professionals evaluate?

The researchers used two measures from administrative records.

Communication: the extent to which a person used words and sentences to communicate. Scores on the scale used in the study ranged from 1 to 5.

Social interaction: the extent to which a person engaged in communication with others and could initiate and maintain contact in familiar or new situations. This scale ranged from 1 to 4.

The researchers harmonised evaluations recorded using different versions of the documentation and used statistical modelling to identify groups with similar changes in scores.

This was not a direct assessment of all aspects of communication or quality of life. For example, the spoken communication scale cannot fully assess the abilities of people who communicate through gestures, cards, communication devices or other forms of augmentative and alternative communication (AAC).

## Finding 1. Six trajectories of spoken communication development

The researchers identified six statistical trajectories of spoken communication.

In five of the six groups, recorded scores for using words and sentences improved over time, although changes began at different ages and progressed at different rates.

Some participants showed gains in speech scores early. For others, improvement was more gradual or began later. In several groups, scores then reached a relatively stable level.

The sixth group, comprising approximately 8.6% of participants, had consistently low scores on the word-use scale.

This does not mean that people in this group could not communicate at all. The administrative scale primarily reflected spoken language and did not fully describe communication without speech.

The central finding is diversity: the development and use of spoken language can differ even among people with the same diagnosis.

## Finding 2. Seven trajectories of social interaction

For social interaction scores, the authors identified seven trajectories.

Scores improved with age in three groups. In another three they remained relatively stable. Together, the groups with stable scores constituted the majority of the study sample.

There was also a seventh group to which the researchers paid particular attention.

### What happened among approximately 5% of participants?

About 5% of people belonged to a statistical group characterised by relatively high childhood social interaction scores that declined during adolescence.

The authors called this pattern *adolescent decline*.

This does not mean that one in five or one in two autistic adolescents loses skills. The study identified this trajectory in a small proportion of participants.

Nor can we conclude that every individual in the group experienced a confirmed loss of a specific skill. Administrative scores may depend on environmental demands, available support and how professionals recorded interaction.

Further research is needed to understand why some adolescents' scores declined.

## Do communication and social interaction always change together?

No. The researchers examined how trajectories in the two areas were associated.

Groups with increasing spoken communication scores were often associated with groups whose social interaction scores improved. But the relationship was not straightforward.

For example, among participants whose social scores declined during adolescence, more than half belonged to a group with early improvement in spoken communication.

This is an important observation: spoken language development and social interaction do not necessarily change in the same way.

A person may use words well yet struggle in new social situations. Another may use little speech but successfully express their intentions through other means.

An assessment of needs should therefore avoid reducing all development to a single scale.

## What did the study show about social circumstances?

The authors examined associations between trajectories and several characteristics, including maternal education, neighbourhood characteristics, the date of entry into services and recorded intellectual disability.

They found statistical associations between some trajectories and socioeconomic characteristics. For example, groups with early improvement in spoken communication relatively more often included people from families with more favourable socioeconomic indicators.

For the group whose social scores declined during adolescence, the authors also found associations with certain individual and neighbourhood characteristics.

However, these findings do not establish that income, parental education, sex or background themselves cause a particular developmental trajectory.

Differences in access to support, timing of diagnosis, service organisation and completeness of records may affect the observed scores. The study could not separate all these factors and establish causal mechanisms.

## Why does this study matter?

One of the study's central strengths was the observation of changes over an extended period using a large database.

Rather than describing a single average developmental curve, the researchers identified several recurring statistical patterns.

This approach shows why autistic people's development cannot be described in advance using one universal model.

The study also highlights the importance of examining adolescence, not only early childhood. Communication, education and support needs may change as the social environment changes.

However, these results describe administrative assessments rather than people's full lived experience.

## What does this mean for parents and professionals?

The main practical message is that development should be assessed regularly and individually.

If a child learns new words more slowly than others, this alone does not tell us how they will communicate in a few years.

If an adolescent finds it more difficult to participate socially, it is useful to consider what has changed: school demands, workload, relationships with peers, emotional wellbeing, the sensory environment or other circumstances.

Support goals may need to be reviewed as a person grows older. Spoken language and externally observed social skills are not the only important outcomes. Accessible communication, independence, wellbeing and the person's own preferences matter too.

The study did not compare the effectiveness of specific therapies. The identified trajectories cannot determine which intervention to choose or guarantee a particular outcome.

## What does the study not yet establish?

Despite the very large sample, the study has important limitations.

First, the data came from an administrative database of one US service system. It may not have included all autistic residents of California, and recording practices may have changed over the years.

Second, the measures were limited to two short scales. They did not provide a comprehensive assessment of alternative communication, quality of life, subjective wellbeing or many daily living skills.

Third, the number and duration of observations varied between participants. The study cannot provide an accurate individual prognosis up to age 27.

Fourth, the identified groups were generated by a statistical model. They are not official autism types or predefined biological categories.

Fifth, the study identified associations with social characteristics but did not establish what caused the differences.

Finally, results from the US service system cannot automatically be applied to Kazakhstan. Local research and data about access to support are needed.

## What to remember

A large longitudinal study identified six patterns of change in spoken communication and seven patterns of change in social interaction among autistic people.

For most participants, recorded speech scores improved with age, while social scores in many groups remained relatively stable. For a small proportion of participants, social interaction scores declined during adolescence.

These findings do not provide a universal prognosis for every child.

For parents and professionals, the main message is that autistic people's development is diverse. Rather than comparing someone against one 'correct' trajectory, it is important to recognise their own changes, consider their needs and review their support as they grow older.
