It is sometimes said that autistic people inherently find it harder to communicate with others. But communication always takes place between people. The extent to which they understand one another depends on how they express their thoughts, their habits and experiences, and the situation itself.

How can we distinguish someone's ability to convey information from other people's impressions of whether they communicate in the expected way?

In 2025, researchers published an experiment involving **311 adults** in *Nature Human Behaviour*. They compared the accuracy of information transfer between autistic people, between non-autistic people and in mixed groups. The findings were unexpected even for the researchers themselves.

## Why did researchers approach communication differently?

Many autism studies assess communication by how closely a person's behaviour matches common social expectations. For example, they consider whether someone uses eye contact, intonation or gestures in familiar ways.

But **a conventional communication style and successful information transfer are not the same thing**. A person may speak directly, look away and still explain their point precisely.

Rather than assessing the outward impression of a conversation, the researchers decided to measure a concrete outcome: how much information one person could convey to another.

They also examined how comfortable, friendly and understandable the interaction felt to participants.

## What is the double empathy problem?

Autism research discusses an idea known as the **double empathy problem**.

The idea is simple: when two people experience the world differently and use different ways of communicating, misunderstandings may arise on both sides. The reason need not lie entirely with one conversation partner.

For example, one person may prefer explicit wording while the other is accustomed to reading between the lines. Both may misinterpret the other's intentions.

This is a useful scientific hypothesis, not a rule that applies to every conversation. The 2025 study tested some of its predictions in a controlled experiment.

## How was the study conducted?

**311 adults** attended the research sessions, including **154 autistic** and **157 non-autistic participants**. The team originally recruited 324 participants, but not all attended the experimental sessions, so the analysis used data from 311 people. Recruitment took place in Edinburgh and Nottingham in the United Kingdom and Dallas in the United States.

The researchers organised **54 information transfer chains**. A chain typically consisted of six participants.

Imagine a game of telephone, except that participants pass along a short story rather than a short phrase.

The first participant learned the story and retold it to the second. The second passed it on to the third. Information continued to move from person to person until the end of the chain.

The researchers prepared two kinds of material: **a fictional story** and **a factual account**. For each story, they counted how many correct details were retained in retelling. The maximum score was 30 details.

## Which groups were compared?

The experiment involved three types of chain:

- **Autistic:** information was passed between autistic participants.
- **Non-autistic:** information was passed between non-autistic participants.
- **Mixed:** both autistic and non-autistic people took part.

The researchers also compared situations in which people did or did not know their interaction partners' diagnostic status.

After each interaction, participants reported how pleasant, easy, successful and friendly they found the conversation.

The authors therefore examined two distinct questions: **how accurately information was transferred** and **how participants rated the quality of the interaction**.

## Main finding: information transfer accuracy did not differ significantly

The researchers expected information to be lost more quickly in mixed chains. This expectation was based on earlier work by the same research group.

However, **the 2025 study did not detect statistically significant differences in overall information transfer accuracy** between autistic, non-autistic and mixed chains.

In all three types of chain, details were gradually lost as the stories were retold. This is understandable: each subsequent participant receives information from the preceding person rather than directly from the original source.

The finding does not demonstrate that everyone in the three groups has identical communication skills. It shows something narrower and important: **in this task, the researchers did not find the expected overall advantage for any one type of chain**.

Original Figure 3 at the beginning of this article shows how the number of accurately reproduced details declined for fictional and factual stories. Each line represents a type of chain.

## Why did the new result surprise the researchers?

The earlier 2020 study involved 72 people. At that time, researchers found that mixed chains lost more story details than chains consisting solely of autistic or solely of non-autistic people.

The 2025 study was much larger: **311 participants rather than 72**, multiple research sites and a more diverse sample.

The new experiment **did not replicate the finding that information transfer was noticeably worse in mixed groups**.

This is how science progresses. Even an interesting and convincing result needs to be tested again in new samples. Sometimes a larger study confirms an earlier hypothesis, and sometimes it shows that the original picture was incomplete.

The authors suggested that differences in samples, analysis methods and participant characteristics might have mattered. It is not yet possible to establish exactly why the two studies differed.

## How did participants rate rapport?

When the researchers asked how comfortable and pleasant the interaction had been, the results looked different.

On average, participants in **non-autistic chains reported higher rapport** than participants in some other conditions. The differences were particularly apparent in ratings by people passing information to the next participant.

The pattern depended on the person's role in the conversation. For example, ratings by those receiving information did not differ significantly in every comparison.

This means that **information transfer accuracy and perceived rapport do not necessarily coincide**. A person may retell a story accurately but still feel awkward. A pleasant conversation does not guarantee that every detail has been retained.

These findings should not be reduced to the claim that autistic people necessarily feel worse when communicating. The researchers examined group averages in a specific experimental situation.

## What changed when participants knew their partners' diagnostic status?

In some chains, participants knew whether their interaction partners belonged to the autistic or non-autistic group. In other chains, this information was not provided.

When diagnostic status was known, some rapport ratings were higher. The clearest statistical association appeared in ratings by participants who **received information** from a partner. The result was less conclusive for those who conveyed information.

This is an interesting observation. Knowing something about a partner's characteristics may have helped participants interpret their communication style differently. However, the study did not establish the precise mechanism of this effect.

**This also does not mean that autistic people should disclose their diagnosis to every conversation partner.** Disclosure is a personal decision. In everyday life, safety, trust, privacy and the risk of discrimination need to be considered.

## Why is this finding important?

The study illustrates why communication cannot be assessed solely by how closely it resembles familiar social norms.

Successful interaction depends on more than an individual person. Both parties, the nature of the task, the setting and participants' expectations matter.

The study also helps distinguish **observable communication effectiveness** from **subjective feelings of rapport**.

These outcomes deserve separate attention, especially in education, work or social contact.

## What does this mean for parents and professionals?

For parents, educators and other professionals, the main practical point is that **an unfamiliar communication style should not automatically be considered ineffective**.

It is useful to consider the content of communication: whether someone understood the message, was able to express their thoughts, and found the interaction comfortable and accessible.

In everyday life, clear instructions, opportunities to clarify what was said, written messages, additional time to respond and respect for different modes of communication can sometimes help.

However, these are **general examples of an accessible environment, not interventions whose effectiveness was tested in this experiment**.

The findings also do not mean that every autistic person only needs contact with other autistic people. People's needs, preferences and abilities vary.

## What does the study not demonstrate?

The study has limitations.

**First**, the participants were adults. The results cannot automatically be generalised to young children or people with different communication needs.

**Second**, the participants were based in the United Kingdom and the United States. Interaction depends on language and culture, so the situation in Kazakhstan needs separate study.

**Third**, the experiment tested a particular storytelling task. Communication at school, at home or at work is much more varied.

**Fourth**, allocation to conditions was not entirely random, and researchers knew participants' diagnostic status. These factors may have influenced the findings.

**Fifth**, participants rated communication comfort themselves. Such ratings are valuable, but they depend on personal perception and are not a universal measure of social success.

Finally, **a lack of statistically significant differences does not establish absolute equality of all communication abilities**. It applies to particular measurements in the sample studied.

## What is worth remembering?

Researchers examined how 311 adults transferred information to one another in autistic, non-autistic and mixed chains.

**Main finding:** unlike the earlier study, the new work did not detect the expected deterioration in information transfer in mixed groups. Participants' feelings of rapport differed, and knowing a partner's diagnostic status was associated with higher ratings on some measures.

This does not negate the existence of communication difficulties in autism. It reminds us that **communication involves interaction between people, and responsibility for mutual understanding should not automatically be placed on just one of them**.
