The human brain consists of an enormous number of cells that perform different tasks. Some transmit signals, while others help sustain their activity. But are molecular processes the same in all these cells in autism?

In 2019, researchers published a study in *Science* that examined gene activity in individual types of brain cells. They found that differences between the autistic donors and the control group were not distributed uniformly across cell types.

Let us look at what this means and why the findings drew the attention of neuroscientists.

## Why is studying brain tissue as a whole not enough?

Imagine a large city. If you only know the average noise level across the city, it is difficult to tell where there is a busy street and where there is a quiet park.

A similar situation arises in brain research. When scientists analyse a large piece of tissue together, the signals from many different cells are mixed.

**Neurons** help transmit and process information. Other cells, often collectively called **glia**, support the functioning of the nervous system. Different cell types have different functions and patterns of gene activity.

So even when an average measurement in tissue changes, it does not always reveal which cell type is involved.

## What is gene activity and why study RNA?

DNA contains genetic instructions. But not all genes are used by every cell at the same time.

When a cell needs to perform a particular task, it can produce an **RNA** molecule using instructions from DNA. The abundance of these molecules gives scientists information about which genes are active.

This is called **gene expression**, or gene activity. Measuring RNA helps researchers investigate the state of cells, although RNA abundance does not always precisely reflect the abundance of the corresponding protein.

Importantly, differences in gene activity do not necessarily mean that the DNA sequence itself has changed.

## How was the study conducted?

Researchers studied postmortem brain samples from **15 autistic people and 16 controls**, matched for several important characteristics. Donors were approximately 4-22 years old overall.

They used **41 tissue samples** from two regions of the cerebral cortex:

- **Prefrontal cortex:** involved in many complex processes, including planning and regulating behaviour.
- **Anterior cingulate cortex:** involved in information processing, attention and behavioural regulation.

Researchers isolated cell nuclei from frozen tissue. A nucleus contains DNA and some of the cell's RNA. They then used **snRNA-seq**, or single-nucleus RNA sequencing, to study gene activity.

The researchers obtained **104,559 nuclear gene expression profiles**: 52,003 from autistic donors and 52,556 from controls.

Why so many nuclei but relatively few people? Many cell nuclei were analysed from each tissue sample. **The donor, not the individual nucleus, is the independent unit when comparing people:** 104,000 nuclei cannot be counted as 104,000 independent participants. This is fundamental when considering the reliability of the findings.

Computational analysis helped identify the cell types represented by the nuclei and compare gene activity within those cell types across groups.

## What did the researchers discover?

**Main finding:** autism-associated molecular differences were not uniform across brain cell types.

In certain upper-layer cortical neuron populations, researchers identified particularly prominent changes in the activity of genes involved in signalling between nerve cells.

They also found differences in the activity of some genes in **microglia**, cells involved in immune defence and supporting central nervous system function.

This does not mean that all neurons or all microglia in autistic people are damaged. The researchers observed statistical differences in the activity of particular sets of genes in the postmortem samples examined.

## Why are upper-layer cortical neurons important?

The cerebral cortex is organised into several layers. They contain different types of neurons that form a complex network of connections.

The researchers were particularly interested in **excitatory neurons in layers 2 and 3**. Some of these neurons participate in information transfer between cortical regions.

In these cells, gene activity associated with synaptic signalling differed between groups.

A **synapse** is a site of interaction between nerve cells through which signals are transmitted. If we imagine a neural network as a communication system, synapses help cells exchange messages.

The study identified neuronal populations in which researchers could look more closely for autism-associated molecular patterns. It did not show that autistic behaviour can be explained by changes in just one neuron type.

## What are microglia and why did they attract the researchers' attention?

Microglia are a distinct group of cells in the central nervous system. They participate in immune responses, maintenance of the tissue environment and the functioning of neural connections.

Velmeshev and colleagues found differences in the molecular state of microglia in the autistic donor group.

However, **differences in microglial gene activity cannot automatically be equated with inflammation in the brain**. Such a conclusion would require additional evidence and research.

Nor can this study be used to recommend anti-inflammatory medicines or other treatments. The authors investigated molecular characteristics of tissues, rather than testing the effectiveness of medical interventions.

## Are molecular differences associated with clinical characteristics of autism?

The researchers compared measurements of some genes' activity with previously collected clinical information about the donors.

They found that gene activity changes in layer 2 and 3 neurons and microglia were statistically associated with certain clinical ratings in the group studied.

This is an interesting finding, but it requires cautious interpretation.

**A statistical association does not mean that RNA analysis can determine an individual's degree of autism.** The study involved only 15 autistic donors, and the patterns were not validated as a diagnostic test.

Moreover, the clinical ratings themselves do not capture the full diversity of autistic people's abilities, needs and quality of life.

## How did the researchers account for other conditions?

Some autistic donors had a history of seizures. This matters because epilepsy can also be associated with changes in brain gene activity.

To help distinguish the possible effects of epilepsy, the authors additionally examined prefrontal cortex samples from eight people with sporadic epilepsy and seven age-matched controls.

They found that most autism-associated changes did not overlap with the changes in the additional epilepsy comparison group.

Such a comparison strengthens the findings, but it cannot rule out the effects of every co-occurring condition or every characteristic of postmortem tissue.

## Why is this discovery important for science?

The study showed that examining individual cell types can reveal patterns that are difficult to detect when tissue is analysed as a whole.

This enables researchers to ask more precise questions: which cells are involved, which sets of genes differ in activity and which processes deserve further study?

These data may be used in further experiments, research on neural network development and tests of scientific hypotheses.

However, many additional stages of research separate the discovery of a molecular association from the development of an effective medical intervention.

## What does this mean for parents and professionals?

The study helps illustrate an important point: **autism cannot be reduced to one brain region, one gene or one universal mechanism**.

Different cell types perform different tasks, and molecular differences may affect them in different ways.

The findings do not provide parents or professionals with a practical way to determine a child's needs from gene activity in the brain. They do not require families to seek specialised RNA tests or new medical procedures.

When planning support, an individual's characteristics, communication methods, skills, needs and environment remain more important.

## What does the study not demonstrate?

This study has important limitations.

**First**, the samples came from a small number of people: 15 autistic donors and 16 controls. Examining many cell nuclei does not remove limitations arising from the small number of donors.

**Second**, the researchers used postmortem tissue from two cortical regions. The findings do not describe all brain regions or allow changes to be observed directly as they develop over time.

**Third**, some donors had co-occurring conditions, including seizures. The additional comparison helped, but could not exclude all possible influences.

**Fourth**, the study reflects gene activity in cell nuclei, not every process taking place in the living brain.

Finally, the statistical associations do not establish that these molecular differences caused autism. Some differences may reflect downstream processes or characteristics of the tissue samples studied.

## What is worth remembering?

Researchers examined gene activity in more than 100,000 brain cell nuclei and found group differences that were particularly apparent in some cortical neurons and microglia.

This helped the scientific community identify more precisely the cell types and molecular processes that deserve further investigation.

**Main point for readers:** the study expands knowledge of autism biology, but it does not provide a new diagnostic test, predict an individual child's development or demonstrate the effectiveness of any treatment.
