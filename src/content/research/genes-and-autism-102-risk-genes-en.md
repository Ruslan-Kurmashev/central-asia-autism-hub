Why does autism sometimes occur in several members of a family, yet sometimes a child is identified as autistic when neither parent has an autism diagnosis? And why can autistic people differ so much from one another?

One way researchers examine these questions is by studying DNA. In 2020, an international team published a large study in *Cell* that identified 102 genes statistically associated with autism. Does that mean researchers have discovered 102 causes of autism? No. Here is what the study actually found.

## What are genes, and why do they matter?

The body's cells contain DNA. Think of it as a large instruction book for how the body develops and functions.

Genes are individual sections of those instructions. Many contain instructions for making proteins that cells, including nerve cells, need to function.

Human DNA contains small differences known as genetic variants. Most do not lead to serious problems, although some can alter how proteins work.

Autism has complex genetic foundations. There is no single 'autism gene' that explains the characteristics of every autistic person.

## What did the researchers want to find out?

The researchers wanted to learn which rare DNA changes are more often associated with autism and what the corresponding genes do in the nervous system.

Three questions were particularly important: whether the identified genes are involved in early brain development, whether they affect signalling between nerve cells, and how genetic patterns associated with autism differ from those observed in conditions involving substantial neurodevelopmental delay.

## How was the study conducted?

The authors combined genetic data from 35,584 people, including 11,986 autistic participants.

They used exome sequencing, a technology for reading the DNA regions containing most of the instructions for making proteins. The exome accounts for only a small fraction of the entire genome, so the method does not examine every possible genetic change.

The researchers compared:

- DNA from autistic children and their parents to identify rare variants, including variants that had newly arisen in a child.
- Data from autistic participants and control groups to investigate which variants occurred more often than expected by chance.

Changes first identified in a child and absent from the corresponding parental DNA samples are called *de novo* variants. They arise naturally during the formation of reproductive cells or early development.

A computational statistical model brought together information about the rarity of variants, their potential effects on genes and differences between groups. The researchers could therefore analyse data from far more people than in earlier, smaller studies.

## The main finding: 102 risk genes

The researchers identified 102 genes with statistical evidence of an association with autism at a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of 0.1 or less. FDR helps account for multiple statistical tests by estimating the expected proportion of false discoveries. A threshold of 0.1 does not mean the list represents 102 definitively established causes of autism. Of these, 60 had not been identified in the authors' earlier analyses. The authors considered 30 genuinely novel risk genes, because they had not previously been implicated in autosomal dominant neurodevelopmental disorders or shown significant enrichment of rare or de novo variants.

A risk gene is not a gene in which every change inevitably leads to autism. The term describes patterns found by analysing data from many people.

Some rare variants can have substantial effects on development, while others have more complex and variable consequences. Many autistic people do not have an identifiable variant in one of these genes that explains their characteristics.

The number 102 is not the definitive total of genes associated with autism. It reflects the particular data and statistical criteria used in 2020. Subsequent research continues to expand this knowledge.

## Why were the genes divided into two groups?

The authors compared rare variants that can disrupt gene function in people from autism research samples and in people recruited because of substantial neurodevelopmental delay.

They identified two comparative sets:

- 53 genes: the relevant variants occurred relatively more frequently in the autism research samples.
- 49 genes: the relevant variants occurred relatively more frequently in the samples recruited for substantial neurodevelopmental delay.

These are not two types of autism, nor a division between 'autism genes' and genes for other conditions. The grouping shows that different genetic changes may be associated with partly different neurodevelopmental characteristics.

## How are these genes related to brain function?

The researchers found that many of the 102 genes are involved in two particularly important processes.

### Regulating nerve cell development

Some genes help determine which genetic instructions a cell uses and when they should be active.

Imagine a construction site where different teams need to complete their work at the right time. Brain development also depends on many coordinated processes: cells form, become specialised and establish connections.

Changes in the regulation of gene activity can affect individual stages of this development.

### Signalling between neurons

Other genes are associated with the functioning of neurons and the connections between them, called synapses.

Neurons exchange signals. Proteins whose function depends on genes help support and regulate this communication.

The genetic findings therefore point to the importance of both the formation of the nervous system and the functioning of neural connections.

## Why did researchers examine brain development before birth?

The authors compared the identified genes with previously collected data on gene activity during human brain development.

Many of the genes are already active before birth. Their activity was also statistically enriched in developing lineages of excitatory and inhibitory neurons.

Excitatory neurons help transmit signals, while inhibitory neurons help regulate activity within neural networks.

These findings support the importance of processes governing how neural circuits form and function. However, the study did not show that every autistic person has the same disturbance in the balance between excitation and inhibition.

## Why does this finding matter?

Earlier genetic studies were often able to investigate only small numbers of families. Larger samples make it possible to identify more reliable statistical associations.

The work of Satterstrom and colleagues helped move research beyond individual genes towards shared biological processes: regulation of gene activity, cell development and neural communication.

It provides a basis for subsequent brain research, not a ready-made treatment or a new diagnostic test.

## What does this mean for parents and professionals?

A gene list cannot predict an individual child's abilities, behaviour or future.

In some clinical situations, genetic assessment can identify a specific genetic condition and help guide medical follow-up. A qualified professional should decide whether an individual needs such testing, taking their developmental history and medical circumstances into account.

The list of 102 genes from this publication does not replace a comprehensive developmental assessment and is not suitable for making a diagnosis independently.

Support for a child continues to depend on their individual needs, communication, skills and living circumstances.

## What does the study not establish?

Despite the large dataset, the study has important limitations.

First, exome sequencing does not cover the entire genome. Many variants outside protein-coding regions were not part of the main analysis.

Second, a statistical association does not mean that the mechanism through which every identified gene influences development has been fully established.

Third, the results come from specific research samples. They cannot automatically be generalised to all populations, including people in Kazakhstan.

Finally, the work focused mainly on rare variants. It does not account for the full contribution of common genetic variants and other developmental factors.

These findings therefore primarily help formulate new research questions rather than make predictions for an individual person.

## What to remember

The researchers found statistical associations between autism and 102 genes, many of which are involved in nerve cell development or the functioning of neural connections.

The findings helped explain why the biological foundations of autism are so diverse.

For parents and professionals, the main message is that genetics matters in autism research, but it cannot reduce a person to a DNA test result or replace an individual assessment of needs.
